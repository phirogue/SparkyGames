# Fifth edition, second pass: the subtractive edit

The fifth-edition revision fixed structure and added what the critics asked
for. It also took the manuscript from 53,686 words to 63,314 — up 18 per cent
— while three of four critics were asking for a *tighter* book. This pass takes
the fat back out without touching the new structure.

**Target: ~56,000 words.** That keeps every scene added last pass and removes
roughly 7,300 words of accumulated ornament, about 11 per cent.

Book-wide that means cutting roughly 320 of the current 600.

**"The way [clause]" similes: 143 in the book.** Chapters 8, 9 and 12 run at 3
per thousand words. Chapter 4, which got a real pass, runs at 0.6 and loses
nothing. Target ~1 per thousand, so about 87 come out.

**Motif repetition.** The book's own house style says a motif is said once.
Current counts: "four seconds" 11 times; "the small hand" 10; "chest-ledger"
10. These are good devices worn to transparency by overuse.

## The governing principle

The professional writer put it exactly right and it is the whole brief for this
pass:

> The voice does not need protecting; it needs pruning — the best sentences in
> this book are the plain ones standing next to one good figure, and the edit's
> whole job at line level is to build them more of that negative space.

The book already proves this about itself. **"It took three tries."** is the
most affecting sentence in the manuscript, and it works because nothing
ornamental stands anywhere near it. Every figure cut from the paragraphs around
a line like that makes the line louder.

So this is not a hunt for bad writing. Almost every sentence being cut is a
*good* sentence. It is being cut because it is the third good sentence in a
row, and the third one is costing the first two their power.

---

## The seven cut patterns

**1. One figure per paragraph.** Where a paragraph stacks two, three or four
similes and metaphors, keep the best and delete the others outright — do not
rewrite them into something plainer, delete them. The remaining figure gets the
whole paragraph's attention.

**2. The image plus its gloss.** The narrator's most frequent tic: give an
image, then explain what it means. *"the lamps going up one by one, like the
city buttoning its coat"* is complete. When an image is followed by a clause
telling the reader how to take it, cut the clause. Trust the image.

**3. The appositive habit.** Sentences constantly append "which is —", "the way
—", "the kind that —". These are the single largest source of bloat. Keep the
ones carrying information; delete the ones carrying attitude.

**4. Narratorial throat-clearing.** *"I want that noted." "Write that down and
let it stand." "The file does not care." "I record the unfairness." "This
account has rules about lying."* This device is excellent perhaps four times in
a novel. Keep the best four in the whole book; cut the rest.

**5. Motif rationing — plant and payoff, nothing between.**
- **"four seconds of looking"**: keep 4 — Elspeth's rule (ch2), Bodkin's
  version (ch3), the flats (ch9), and the abandoned hunt (ch12), where it is
  the moral hinge of the book. Cut the other 7.
- **"the small hand"**: 10 → 5.
- **"chest-ledger"**: 10 → 6.
- **"spent is spent" / "held is not spent"**: already at 2 each. Leave them.

**6. Em-dashes to periods and commas.** Target ≤5 per thousand words per
chapter. Most em-dash pairs in this book are parentheses around an aside; where
the aside earns its place, commas do the job, and where it does not, delete the
aside. Chapters 7, 8, 10, 11, 12 and 13 need the heaviest work.

**7. Paragraph and sentence length.** Break every paragraph running past about
six lines. Where a sentence carries three or more subordinate clauses, split
it. A young reader named this exactly: "20% less chewing."

## Scene-level trims (secondary — line work comes first)

Structural cutting is nearly exhausted; the four longest chapters are each
doing real work. But four places carry recoverable fat:

- **Ch. 11, the hearing (~2,800 words).** The three supporting testimonies
  (Cardew, Merrow, Brindle) each make their point and then make it again.
  Compress to one strong beat each. Do not touch Shear's failed demonstration
  or the Clerk's finding.
- **Ch. 12, the stated account (~2,500 words).** The moral climax and it must
  stay complete, but the exchanges around it can tighten by 400–500 words.
  Every line of the account itself stays.
- **Ch. 8, the dream sequence (~2,000 words).** Now that the eight lights are
  withheld, some connective narration is doing less work than it was.
- **Ch. 4, the Fish-Giver caper.** Keep the brooch, the magistrate's cat and
  "Paper has teeth. I filed the lesson where I file weapons." The roof-jury
  framing and the dawn payoff can go to a third of their length; the epilogue
  re-establishes him anyway.

## Protected — do not cut, do not rephrase

- The prologue's opening and its closing line, "Then that is where we are
  going."
- The kettle, in all its appearances, and "It took three tries."
- The shroud-seam on the quay.
- Every line the Clerk speaks. He is the funniest character in the book by
  unanimous critical consent and his register is already spare.
- Tansy's escrow line, and her mirror-of-a-mirror hem in the epilogue.
- Bodkin's guest-right speech (ch3) and its payoff at the candle (ch12).
- The chapter 8 roof scene, entire.
- Elspeth's confession in Box 9-Crown, entire.
- The chapter 11 kinship passage ("I am looking at my own elder brother").
- "You fight like a housecat. --- I was one. --- Were." in both its uses.
- "Spent is spent," and Bodkin's last exchange through to the ninth stroke.
- Fenn's accounting at the end of ch12 and Merrow's two-line bill in ch13.
- The epilogue from "She always came to the window" to the end.

When in doubt about a sentence, the test is not "is this good?" — nearly all of
it is good. The test is: **if this sentence went, would the reader lose
anything but a pleasure?** If the answer is no, it goes, and the pleasures that
remain get more room to land.
