# Review: *The Nine Lives of Ashcat* (manuscript)
## Reviewer: the Mystery Devotee — 60+ crime novels a year, Christie to Chandler to Tana French

**Overall verdict: 6/10.**

I came to this book the way I come to everything that calls itself a case: with a pencil, a suspect list, and a grudge against authors who cheat. Let me say up front what this manuscript is not: it is not a cheat. The clueing is honest to a degree I see maybe twice a year, the prose at its best is the kind you read aloud to whoever is in the room, and the emotional ledger — the kettle, the Toll, the confession in the iron box — is kept with real discipline. But judged as a *mystery*, which is how it presents itself ("this account has rules"; "the file keeps what I did"), it has a hollow center: the whodunit is transparent by the one-third mark, the suspect board never holds a single candidate a practiced reader can believe in, the middle third wanders into episodic side-cases while the murder investigation idles, and the backstory that powers the reveal is delivered three separate times, so that the confession that should detonate in Chapter 11 arrives pre-detonated. A 9/10 book is hiding in here. What's on the page is a beautifully written 6: too honest to be a puzzle, too digressive to be a thriller, and too in love with its murderer to be a tragedy that fully pays.

---

## The Good

**The prose, at its best, is genuinely first-rate.** The prologue is as good an opening chapter as I've read in years. "Dusk over Hollowmere, and the lamps going up one by one, like the city buttoning its coat." The kettle sequence — "I took it off the fire. One paw, on the handle, the way I had watched her do it ten thousand times from the hearthrug. It took three tries. Nobody will ever know." — is perfect restraint, and the author has the discipline to pay it off twice: in her posthumous note ("I know you took it off the fire. I know it took three tries") and in the epilogue ("One paw. One try, these days. Everybody knows."). That is craft. The chapter hooks are consistently strong: "Then that is where we are going" (Prologue), "Find the teacher" (Ch. 3), "It had blinked" (Ch. 6 into 7). Chapter 7's opening — "Sootbeard has watched one street for three hundred years, and last month, he blinked" — is a top-five first line in my recent reading, full stop.

**The fair-play clueing is meticulous, and mostly plays fair.** The culprit's name is planted in Chapter 3 with its meaning hidden in plain sight, and cashed in Chapter 11: "A bodkin, she told me once... *the blunt needle, Ash. The one you use to unpick.*" The mirror-stitch is seeded in Chapter 2 before anyone knows it matters — "my paws wanted to run the line mirror-wise, right to left... I remember being irritated at my own hands and thinking nothing of it. Nothing in this book costs me more" — and the eventual howdunit deduction (a familiar learns the stitch mirrored because he watches "from the lap... the needle coming *toward* me — of course it is mirrored") is the best single piece of detective reasoning in the book, both surprising and inevitable. The Chapter 7 reconstruction from three eyelid-less witnesses — a rain barrel, a dreaming mastiff, a frost-pane — is Golden Age method wearing new fur: "Small. Low. Four-footed. Tailed. Breath held for the crossing." Carr would have nodded.

**The best twist in the book is a physical clue re-read.** The candle stub — exhibit one, pinned in Chapter 1 as proof an intruder "waited here, in the dark, comfortable, unhurried" — is inverted in Chapter 12 into something devastating: "She set it out for you that evening, unlit — the good beeswax, bought the week after the water and kept forty years, the light you keep against a guest you owe. You lit it... It is the first exhibit I ever pinned, and the only one in this file with both your hands on it: the guest's, and the collector's." That is what I read mysteries for: the same object, meaning the opposite thing, and both meanings earned. The corroborating receipt ("one (1) candle, beeswax, escrow-grade") is even planted at the bottom of the box a chapter earlier.

**The eliminations are argued, not asserted.** Wick is cleared by economic logic ("You do not go to the expense of killing a woman you have spent a premium to lawfully destroy... A dead seamstress mends nothing"); the Gentleman by the nature of the payment ("He would not spend lives. He *banks*"); Shear by courtroom demonstration — her forced attempt at the stitch, "forward-handed, correct, and *nothing like it*," is a properly staged elimination scene; the old grey tom by forensics ("no splice-callus, no thread-groove, claws gone round as riverstones"). Each crossing-off has a stated cost, too: "a suspect crossed off is a door closed, and the room behind the remaining doors gets darker by exactly that much light."

**The world's ruling metaphor is disciplined.** Everything in Hollowmere — magic, death, grief, silence — is debt, escrow, and audit, and the book almost never breaks its own economy. The life-ledger arithmetic is airtight across 400 pages: "Nine allotted... One spent" (Prologue); the flats are explicitly "My second true death, of nine"; the sign-off reads "nine lives allotted, two spent, seven held / in trust." I checked. It balances. The Clerk is the finest supporting character — "Falling is not an appointment. Falling is an accident that has got above itself" — and his slow thaw ("Sew less tired"; two inkwells; the cat-sized chair "placed, not left") is the book's most elegantly managed relationship.

**The Toll is the rare fantasy cost that actually costs.** When the Court takes the chart-table memory, the book has the nerve to keep the loss lost: "It means the fact is still mine... and the room the fact was kept in is gone. No lamp. No table. No voice over the needle. My own handwriting, telling me there was a voice." And the ending refuses a full refund — Fenn stays dead: "Fenn was taken in those weeks. Fenn was fuel. Fenn does not walk home, that night or ever." Merrow's two-line bill in the epilogue ("You kept the seam. He paid on it. Both lines are true") is the hardest, best moral accounting in the manuscript.

## The Bad

**The suspect structure is a façade.** Count the board: Wick, Shear, the Quiet Gentleman, the senior grey, Bodkin. Wick is eliminated at the exact midpoint (Ch. 5). Shear isn't even introduced until Chapter 8 — far too late to be structural — and is never viable: the narrator concedes it on the page ("It was true, and I knew it was true, and I kept her pin on the board anyway"), and Cardew's silence in the corridor tells the reader the same thing. The Gentleman is never plausible for one minute; his whole characterization (settlement "starves him," he banks rather than spends) rules him out on first meeting. The grey tom is explicitly a decoy the *narrator* buys for himself: "I had spent an evening buying myself a stranger, and the purchase had not held." Which leaves exactly one recurring character with unexplained competence, unexplained knowledge, and a name that means "unpicking needle." A Christie board keeps four live candidates until the last thirty pages. This board holds zero from Chapter 7 onward. The book half-knows it — the Chapter 11 aside, "You have known for chapters... You have been waiting chapters for me to turn around," is disarming and honestly rather brave — but candor about a structural weakness does not repair it. When the detective spends four chapters refusing to write a name the reader has already written, tension doesn't build; it leaks.

**The backstory is delivered three times, and the third time is the one that needed to land.** The eight-lives-for-an-anchor working is shown in the Chapter 8 dream ("eight small lights go out of the grey shape at her skirts... *it did not run*"), then read verbatim off the vault docket in Chapter 9 ("*he offers; I accept; write down that he offers*"), then given a third time in Elspeth's Chapter 11 confession ("He said... 'He offers. Write down that he offers.'"). The confession is the emotional summit of the entire novel — her own hand, "blotted — she who never blotted" — and it arrives carrying no new information. Every beat of it has been pre-read. The docket scene even pre-quotes the confession's signature line. This is the single most damaging pacing decision in the book: the author triple-insures the reveal and thereby cancels its premium.

**The middle third is an episode anthology wearing a trench coat.** Between Wick's elimination (Ch. 5) and the shadow-stitching reveal (Ch. 11), the murder investigation advances by perhaps three genuine increments (the Gentleman's spool, the gargoyle's dating of the technique, the roost postmaster's "the mender comes at the neap"). Around those increments the book stacks: the Ratsmeet, the pigeon election, Tansy's purr, the Fish-Giver's lease, the caulker's shadow, the widow Hake fire, the Understudy's forty-year rehearsal, the Horologists' golden hour, the Locksmiths' reading. Some of these cash out beautifully later — the lease teaches "Paper has teeth," which becomes the bell petition; the golden hour establishes the precedent "the Court will honor a debt of light against a debt of time," on which "the whole last chapter of this book stands" — and the author knows to tell us so. But *telling us so* is the tell: Chapter 10 has to enumerate, "The golden hour goes in this file for three reasons," because the scene itself doesn't feel load-bearing while you're in it. The caulker's-shadow episode exists to state "held is not spent" — a lesson the wraith's pointing arm, the blind postmaster, AND Tansy's escrow question each also deliver. Four deliveries of one thesis is a seminar, not a plot.

**The finale resolves by jurisprudence the reader can admire but not participate in.** The unmasking happens in Chapter 11; Chapter 12's climax is a settlement, a siege, and a sacrifice. Fine — some of my favorite crime novels end past the unmasking. But the actual mechanism of victory is a stack of statutes: "a kept death spends whole" (Ch. 9), "an anchor wants a life that is finished paying" (Ch. 9), accrual on held assets credited against the securing debt (Ch. 12), "void for fraud on the consideration" (Ch. 12, ruled by auditors offstage that same night). All technically planted, none deducible in advance by the reader in the way the mirror-stitch was. The last hundred pages ask me to spectate at a very well-run probate hearing. I enjoyed it. I did not solve anything, and neither could I have — after Chapter 11 the book stops being a mystery and doesn't fully tell you it stopped.

**Prose fatigue is real.** The voice is an aphorism engine: "Coincidence is what mice call carelessness." "Claws are a rumor. Paws are an appointment." "A rat's silence is a price list." "Grief is the one auditor that refuses its own evidence." Any one of these is a keeper; forty a chapter, every chapter, in one register, means scenes stop being distinguishable by texture. The book also leans hard on formula phrases — "close work, old-fashioned" appears at the Clerk's desk, the Gentleman's ledger, the neap knot, the sill vole, the counterfoil; "The cold went. Then the noise. Then the part of me that was worried about the noise" runs three times; "the stairs up are longer than the fall down" is used until the narrator lampshades his own rationing of it ("I have promised to ration that sentence"). The repetitions are partly intentional clue-work (the knot-hand *should* recur), but the density crosses from motif into mannerism.

## The Ugly

**The murderer gets a hero's death, and the book lets him have it at a discount.** Bodkin, by the manuscript's own stated account, murdered *three* stitchers: Elspeth, "the lady of the Tatterstage," and "Widow Sarn of Hemlock Row... whose terrier waited two winters on her step" — the latter two killed in cold premeditation "decades ahead of the need, the way a professional removes the locks before the robbery." He emptied roosts, took Fenn's hall of lamplighters, and "under the whole of it... forty years of stolen half-seconds off the stock of every soul in the city." He also facilitated the Understudy's on-stage death as collateral. And then Chapter 12 hands him the most moving exit in the novel — "the walk-in apologizes for the filing" — and the epilogue remembers him as "the fond ghost of my whole season." The two murdered stitchers get one reopened file and one read-aloud name in the epilogue. Widow Sarn taught "half the quay to mend nets"; her terrier sat two winters on a step; she is worth one clause. Meanwhile the *victim* is indicted at full length — Chapter 11 devotes an entire formal accounting to Elspeth's guilt ("guilty of accepting eight lives that were offered but were not hers to accept...") that runs longer and hits harder than anything the text ever charges to her killer's premeditated serial murders. I understand the design: the book is about debts, and Bodkin's is real. But a crime novel that spends its moral fury on the victim and its tenderness on the murderer had better balance that ledger to the farthing, and this one is off by two dead women and a dog.

**The antagonist's plan contradicts itself, and the book patches it with a sigh.** Bodkin's stated goal: open the seam because the lake promised his eight lives back. But Ash establishes — and says aloud on the sea-wall in Chapter 5, in Bodkin's hearing — that unpicked stitches come off as dead slack: "What was paid into the stitching does not come back out at the unpicking." So the murderer has carried disproof of his own motive "in his own teeth — every stitch you lifted, coming off the seam as slack" for the entire season, and continued anyway. Simultaneously, he is arming, training, and thread-mending the one creature purpose-built to stop him, because "a collection without a stated account is only a theft." So he wants the seam open AND wants to be stopped and settled with. The text's answer — "grief is the one auditor that refuses its own evidence" — is a lovely sentence doing the work of a load-bearing wall. It makes the antagonist's forty-year campaign psychologically poignant and strategically incoherent, and in a mystery the plan is the skeleton. When the skeleton is a mood, the reveal can't be interrogated the way the book's own courtroom rigor teaches us to interrogate everything else.

**The Tatterstage night does not parse.** Chapter 7, same chapter, three versions: the gargoyle's blink dates to "the night the theater's leading lady died mid-line at her own opening"; the Understudy's history says "the lead's throat closed by fog, the house full, her name chalked on the board — and she died on that stage mid-line, at her own opening"; then her testimony says "It was not the fog that stopped **his** breath, little one," followed two lines later by "**my lady** was a stitcher." Who died on that stage, who died of the closed throat, what gender is the lead, and are we to understand two deaths in one night — the stitcher-lead (Cardew's "twice before" victim) *and* the Understudy? A careful reader — and this book demands and deserves careful readers — cannot reconstruct the second murder in the series from the text as written. In a fair-play mystery, that's not a nitpick; the prior crimes are the pattern, and the pattern has a hole in the middle.

**Loose threads a book this proud of its threads should not have left.** (1) Wick's motive for the eviction is literally elided on the page: "I wanted her out, cat. Out of the district, out of the trade, out of — reach." Reach of *what*? The dash is doing the work of a chapter. A monopolist spends a premium destroying the woman whose free labor underwrites his empire, and his reason is a typographical gesture. (2) Chapter 3 flags Brindle's tell — "she paused — half a second, no more, on the cart-slot seal... Half-seconds are the whole currency of the case" — and that pause is never cashed. The book trains me to price half-seconds and then declines to pay one of its own. (3) The thread over the Mere is cut "mid-air. Over the water... where nothing walks" — and the working is explicit that only Elspeth could walk the black glass. How did Bodkin get out there to bite it through? The mechanism is never given. (4) At the climax Ash offers "seven, banked" as a new anchor — his complete remaining allotment, including presumably the one he's living on; the arithmetic that is impeccable everywhere else goes fuzzy at the exact moment it matters most.

## Specific recommendations

1. **Collapse the triple delivery of the eight-lives backstory into two beats.** Keep the Chapter 8 dream as image only — cut the dream's explicit accounting ("Eight spent. Nine owed... rounding up") — and in the Chapter 9 vault scene, let Ash find the docket but be unable to read the anchor's line in full (the Court can seal an open docket's operative entry; the book already has the machinery). Then the Chapter 11 confession delivers "write down that he offers" for the *first* time in full, and the summit of the book detonates on contact instead of on re-read.

2. **Move Mistress Shear's introduction from Chapter 8 to Chapter 3–4, and make her viable.** Give her actual received instruction — two sessions with the mirror-hand seller before it vanished, not just a sample in a box — so she possesses means as well as motive for four chapters instead of half of one, and so her courtroom demonstration in Chapter 11 is a genuine question rather than a formality. The board needs one candidate a smart reader can hold past Chapter 7; she is the only available body.

3. **Cut one of the four "held is not spent" lessons.** The caulker's-shadow episode (Ch. 8) is the redundant one — its thesis is already carried by Tansy's escrow exchange (Ch. 6), the blind postmaster (Ch. 9), and the wraith's pointing arm can be transplanted into the Chapter 9 Mereside descent in two paragraphs. That buys back a quarter of Chapter 8 for the murder case at the exact point the reader's patience is thinnest.

4. **Let Ash write the name when the reader does — once.** Currently he refuses to write it in Chapter 7 ("There was a name the arithmetic liked better, and I would not write it"), again in Chapter 8 ("I did not write the name"), and again in Chapter 9 ("a name I would not write"). Three coy refusals of a name the reader has held since Chapter 3 is a tax on trust. Keep one refusal (Chapter 8's is the best-motivated), and replace the others with active avoidance behavior — the grey-tom snare-hunt already dramatizes the denial far better than narration announcing it.

5. **Price the prior murders in the settlement scene.** In Chapter 12's stated account, the Tatterstage lead and Widow Sarn get one shared sentence. Give them each a line with weight equal to the candle's — e.g., make Sarn's terrier the item at which Bodkin's one eye closes, instead of (or in addition to) the candle — and in the epilogue, give the reopened files a scene, not a clause. The murderer's sacrificial exit can stand *only* if the account he settles visibly includes the victims who had no heir to state theirs.

6. **Repair the Tatterstage chronology.** Decide: the lead (a stitcher, Cardew's second victim) dies of the bought fog; the Understudy dies mid-line of the purchased silence the same night. Then fix the pronouns — "his breath" vs. "my lady" cannot both refer to the lead as written — and have the Understudy's testimony state the two deaths in order, since she is the only witness who saw both.

7. **Finish Wick's sentence.** "Out of — reach" needs its object: one clause explaining why a monopolist would spend a premium to remove his own free ward-stitcher (fear of what she knew about the water wall? a desire to convert her from creditor-of-conscience into dependent?). The elimination logic that clears him is the book's first great deduction; it deserves a motive that parses.

8. **Cash or cut Brindle's half-second pause on the cart-slot seal (Ch. 3).** Cheapest fix: one line in her Chapter 11 testimony — she recognized favor-paper on the seal and held her tongue because the Exchange does not price the Gentleman's stock aloud. The book's own maxim ("Half-seconds are the whole currency of the case") makes the dangling version an unforced error.

9. **Give Bodkin's water-crossing a mechanism.** One sentence when the habit unwinds in Chapter 12 — e.g., the woven wards of the seam's own maker are the one fabric the Mere must bear up, which is *why* he needed to wear her work — simultaneously explains the mid-air cut in Chapter 2 and makes the stolen-thread coat load-bearing instead of costume.

10. **Thin the aphorism density by a third in Chapters 4, 8, and 10, and ration the formula phrases.** Keep "close work, old-fashioned" to its four clue-bearing appearances (Clerk's desk, Gentleman's ledger, neap knot, counterfoil); cut it from the vole and anywhere decorative. The epigrams that remain will land harder for the silence around them — the book already proves this with "It took three tries," which works precisely because nothing ornamental stands near it.

11. **Start the tide-race clock when the petition is filed, not retroactively.** Chapter 12 admits the trick: "I want the reader to have spent the four days knowing there was a race, because I spent them knowing" — but the reader learns of the race in the same paragraph it's won. Post the neap deadline at the end of Chapter 11 (the auditors' "holding; holding; holding at the anchor only" bulletins are ready-made for it), and the bell redemption becomes a countdown instead of a recounting.

---

**Bottom line.** This is the best-written 6/10 I've read in years. The sentences, the clue-mechanics, the Clerk, the candle, the kettle — all of it argues for a higher score, and the transparent culprit, the strawman suspect board, the thrice-told backstory, the anthology middle, and the under-priced murderer argue it right back down. The difference between this manuscript and the genre's shelf of keepers is not talent. It is structure — and structure, as this book knows better than any I've read this year, is just accounting you do before the bill arrives.
