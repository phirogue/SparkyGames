# Book Report: *The Nine Lives of Ashcat* (or whatever it's actually called — the title page wasn't in my packet)

**By me, age 12 (13 in November), reader of all of Percy Jackson, all fifteen Wings of Fire books, most of Warriors, and Skulduggery Pleasant twice.**

**Overall: 6/10.** Which sounds mean but isn't. A 6 from me means I actually finished it and cried once and I'm still a little mad at it. Here's the deal: this book has maybe the best cat narrator I've ever read, a death-desk Clerk I would read a whole spinoff about, and an ending that legitimately got me. It ALSO has chapters that felt like my mom's mortgage paperwork learned to talk, and it made me look up more words than my actual vocabulary homework. Both things are true. That's how you get a 6.

The story: Ash is a witch's cat. His witch gets murdered on page like ten, and he spends the whole book investigating it, which means interviewing rats, magpies, a gargoyle, a ghost actress, and Death's receptionist. The murderer turns out to be — okay I'll save it for The Bad, because I guessed it, and I want credit.

---

## The Good

**Ash's voice is the best thing in the book, full stop.** He narrates like a detective who is also 100% a cat. "A door has never once been an obstacle; it is, however, always an insult." I read that line out loud to my brother. Also "cats do not worry — cats *investigate*." Also the bit in the Prologue where he says he wants the record to show he was happy and didn't know it, "which is the only way anyone ever is," and I had to put the book down for a second because WOW, okay, Prologue, calm down.

**The kettle scene (Prologue).** Elspeth is dead in her chair and the kettle is screaming and nobody's going to it, so Ash takes it off the fire himself, one paw, and it takes him three tries, "and nobody will ever know." That is the saddest sentence involving a kettle in the history of kettles. And then in the LAST chapter the kettle "has not screamed once since the night it screamed, because there is always somebody in this house to take it off the fire in time. One paw. One try, these days." I'm not crying, YOU'RE crying. The book is really good at planting a small thing early and detonating it later — the folded red kerchief does this too, and so does the beeswax candle stub, which goes from "random clue in chapter 1" to absolutely devastating in chapter 12 when you find out SHE put it out for him. Unlit. As hospitality. For her own murderer. That's cold-blooded good writing.

**The Clerk and the death office (all over the book).** Deaths are APPOINTMENTS. If you die without an appointment you get REFUSED and sent back up, minus a filing fee, and "mind the door on your way out; it sticks." The stamp is a paw print and "neither of us remarked on this." When Ash dies in a dumb gutter accident in chapter 4, the Clerk tells him "Falling is an accident that has got above itself." This is Skulduggery-level funny. The cat-sized chair that just quietly appears in chapter 8 — "placed, not left" — got me right in the feelings and it's a CHAIR.

**Tansy (chapter 4 onward).** Tiny orange kitten who sold her purr to save her brothers, sleeps through her own court hearing, then wakes up, walks across all the important cats, sits down against Ash and announces "This is my counsel. He is the best one." Best character introduction in the book. Her line "Yes. We live alone" when Ash says he lives alone is exactly how little kids argue and win. And at the end she learns to stitch by watching Ash the same way Ash learned by watching Elspeth — mirror of a mirror — which is the one plot-math moment that made me go "ohhhh" out loud instead of "wait, what."

**Sootbeard (chapter 7).** A gargoyle who's been on duty for 300 years because his decommission order was "improperly countersigned," and who tries for a MONTH to file a police report about his own blink and nobody will take it. Then he WALKS ACROSS THE CITY carrying Ash in his cupped fists "at parade height" like regimental colors. Funniest image in the whole book. The detective work in this chapter is also the best in the book: they reconstruct the invisible crossing using a rain barrel's ripples, a sleeping dog's dream, and frost on a window that recorded a "corridor of no breath" because the killer held its breath crossing the street. That's genuinely clever mystery stuff and I want more chapters like this one.

**The Understudy (chapter 7, again — this chapter is carrying).** Ghost actress who died mid-line 40 years ago and can't leave until the scene ends correctly, but she remembers her own death wrong and keeps directing everyone into her mistake. Ash fixes it by just... standing where the chalk marks say, in the light, and letting her line land. "Enter the cat, which is more than the management deserves." When her better theater's house lights come up for her I got chills.

**The scary parts are actually scary.** The rag-wraiths (empty clothes whose owners are MISSING, not dead) are a great monster. The Drowned who never hurry because they are "always arriving" — creepy. And chapter 9 on the mudflats is the best scene in the book: the fog offers Ash a deal in the stolen voices of everyone it's taken, one sentence per voice, including a child's voice saying "And we walk home tomorrow," and it offers him NOTHING for himself because it priced him correctly, and he still says no. And then Fenn the lamplighter walks into the fog with empty eyes while Ash screams at his boots, and the lamp keeps burning on the mud. And Fenn STAYS DEAD. The book doesn't take it back. Respect.

**The ending mostly lands.** Bodkin sewing himself into the bottom of the lake as the anchor — "Tell the Clerk that the walk-in apologizes for the filing" — is a top-tier last line for a character. The missing walking home in their coats and feathers, the ninth bell ringing, "Come home" at the end. I finished it at 11:40pm and just sat there.

## The Bad

**I guessed the murderer in chapter 5, and the book didn't admit it until chapter 11.** That's the big one. Look at the clue trail: chapter 2, Ash literally writes "it travels like a cat" and then says he didn't like the note so he turned the page. Chapter 3, a mysterious wise old grey tom with one eye shows up out of nowhere, saves Ash's life, is named BODKIN (which the book later tells us means the needle you use to UNPICK — the name is the confession!), and knows everything about everything. Chapter 5, standing outside the haunted warehouse, Bodkin says "I don't go in there." That's when I wrote "IT'S BODKIN" in my notes. Chapter 7 spells out "small; low; four-footed; tailed" for anyone still asleep. Chapter 8 shows a grey four-footed shape at the witch's skirts having eight lives pulled out of it. And then I read THREE MORE CHAPTERS of Ash suspecting a random court cat instead. The book even knows it did this — in chapter 11 Ash turns to the reader and says "You have known for chapters... You have been waiting chapters for me to turn around." Okay, but if the BOOK knows I solved it in chapter 5, why did I have to wait until chapter 11? Ash being in denial makes sense for his character, and the reveal scene itself is really good — but four to six chapters of dramatic irony is a LOT of me tapping the page going "it's the grey one, buddy."

**Everything is accounting.** Debts, ledgers, escrow, audits, dockets, filings, collateral, accrual, liens, "fraud on the consideration." At first it's a cool theme — a city where favors are money, love it. By chapter 10 the metaphor has eaten the book. The murder investigation gets solved by: reading a ledger (ch 3), reading a shift-book (ch 3), reading a counterfoil (ch 5), a brokerage-trail (ch 11), and the final monster gets defeated by an AUDIT — the Court discovers the pawned bell's interest already paid off the debt, so the paperwork was wrong, so ring the bell. I understood it (mostly — see The Ugly), but the climactic weapon of this fantasy novel is a clerical finding about accrued interest, and I just think a kid picking this up for the cat detective on the cover should be warned.

**Confusing parts, with names, because you asked:**
- **Chapter 9, the vault scene.** "An hour that is never rung is never kept, and so it pools down here with the rest of the unclaimed." I read this paragraph three times. I THINK the deal is: the ninth bell got pawned, so the ninth hour of every night has nowhere to go, so it's been piling up inside the bell for a century, and also deaths only "count" if they happen at a rung hour? The rules about true deaths vs. walk-ins vs. refusals vs. fees vs. the Toll were already a lot (I had to keep a list: docket death = spends a life; accident = refused + fee; Toll = they take a whole memory), and then chapter 9 added "hours keep appointments" on top and I was doing homework.
- **Chapter 11, the parcel timeline.** The spool is 40 years old but was mailed 30 years ago but the note inside is newer because she opened her own mail to her future self and resealed it. The book literally says "Set the row straight, as I had to" — even the CAT needed to diagram it. So did I.
- **Chapter 8, the dream.** It's told in italic fragments, and the most important part is deliberately hidden ("the dream did not show me what the tithe was, and I have chosen not to guess in writing"). I get that it's tasteful but it felt like the book flinching right when I leaned in.
- **Chapter 12, why the bell matters to the sacrifice.** I eventually pieced together that Bodkin's death only "spends whole" as an anchor because the bell makes it a kept appointment. It's in there, but it's assembled from parts across chapters 9, 11, and 12, and I'm honestly only 85% sure I got it right, which is a weird feeling to have about the climax.

**The words.** I'm good at words. These sent me to my phone: *gravamen* (it's a DISTRICT NAME and apparently also a law word, which is a joke for lawyers, not for me), *intestate*, *residuary legatee*, *counterfoil*, *surety*, *lien*, *disgorged*, *amicus curiae* (the book does translate this one, with a moth, which was great), *neap*, *escapements*, and "every watchman's turnip" — I now know a turnip is old slang for a pocket watch, but for a full page I thought the watchmen were carrying vegetables. Also the sentences. Some paragraphs are eight lines long with four em-dashes and a semicolon doing a handstand. Chapter 5's warehouse-economics paragraph and chapter 10's Horologist backstory were where I caught myself reading words without reading them.

**Pacing dips.** Chapters 3 and 4 are good but samey — arrive at a guild, do them a favor, get one clue, get one cryptic sentence at the door, repeat. Chapter 10's first half (clockmakers hoarding minutes for their dying founder) is a whole separate short story parked in the middle of the endgame — the golden hour itself is gorgeous, and the Mere watching it "like a hole cut in the page" is the best creepy image in the book, but I checked how many pages were left during the contract-law setup, and that's never a good sign.

## The Ugly

The stuff that almost made me put it down:

1. **The courtroom chapter (11) nearly lost me.** "In the matter of the estate of E. Vane (intestate): on the question of succession." I like courtroom scenes — I've seen my dad's legal shows — but this one opens with parties, briefs, joined motions, and a petition about "the estate's technical effects," and I put the book down and played Mario Kart for an hour. I came back (Cardew's "There. *Now* scold me" and Shear laying the needle down square are worth it, and the confession in the box wrecked me), but a kid with less stubbornness taps out right there, ten pages before the best reveal in the book.

2. **The middle is talk, talk, talk.** Between the wraith fight in chapter 6 and the flats in chapter 9, almost every scene is Ash sitting across from someone while they exchange extremely careful sentences about debts. The Quiet Gentleman is a cool creepy idea (nobody can remember his face! his voice is "the sound a signature would make"!) but his chapter is two people being cryptic at each other in a nice office. My eyes started skiing down the page during his speech about the spool.

3. **The book repeats its own cool lines until I could predict them.** "The stairs up are longer than the fall down." "Spent is spent." "Close work. Old-fashioned." "The cold went. Then the noise. Then the part of me that was worried about the noise." The first time, each of these is great. By the fourth "close work, old-fashioned," I was saying it along with the book like a song I was tired of. (The book KNOWS it does this — it even says it's rationing the stairs line, and then keeps a bunch of other lines on repeat anyway.)

4. **Reading it feels like work in a way Percy Jackson never does.** Percy tells you what's happening. Ash tells you what's happening inside a triple metaphor about ledgers with a flashback folded in, and sometimes he tells you something is important but refuses to say what ("I filed it instead. Half-seconds are the whole currency of the case"). Cool the first time. The tenth time it feels like the book is hiding my own presents from me.

---

## How I'd Fix It

1. **Let Ash figure out Bodkin sooner — or hide Bodkin better.** Either cut one of the giant clues (honestly, "I don't go in there" outside the exact warehouse where his lives are buried is a signed confession) or have Ash consciously suspect him by chapter 8 and struggle with it out loud. Four chapters of the reader waiting for the narrator is three chapters too many. The denial is good psychology, so keep SOME of it — just make Ash almost-write the name in chapter 8, not chapter 11.

2. **Put the death rules and the bell rules in one place, said simply, and then TRUST them.** The Clerk is your funniest character — let him explain "kept hours" in chapter 9 the way Tansy explains escrow in chapter 6 ("Like when the big kittens hold your tail" — that one sentence taught me more than a page of vault paperwork). If a kitten can nail escrow in eight words, the Clerk can nail the ninth-hour thing in two sentences.

3. **Cut every third fancy repeat and every fifth em-dash.** Keep "spent is spent" (that one's load-bearing). Retire "close work, old-fashioned" after three uses. Break the eight-line paragraphs in chapters 5, 9, and 10 into normal ones. Same story, 20% less chewing.

4. **Shrink the Horologists (chapter 10) to what it needs to be.** Keep the golden hour — it's beautiful and it sets up the precedent — but the founder's-contract backstory and the auditor procedure could be half as long and I'd have lost nothing. It's a side quest wearing a main-quest coat.

5. **Give the courtroom chapter a cold open that isn't legal.** Start chapter 11 with the spooky thirty-years-late postman (he's great!), and get to the hearing after. And translate the Latin the way you translated the moth. If a word would stop a smart 12-year-old, either explain it in-world or pick the plain word. I should not need a phone to read about a cat.

6. **One more real action scene in the middle.** The goose training, the wraith triple-fight, and the flats prove the book CAN do action. Chapters 5 through 8 need one fight or chase that isn't a conversation, because the animals-being-awesome parts are exactly the parts I'd hand a friend.

**Would I have finished it if a teacher hadn't assigned it?** Honestly... yes, but only because of the Prologue. The kettle bought the book about six chapters of credit, and chapters 7 and 9 re-upped the subscription right when it was expiring. I'd tell my friends about the gargoyle carrying the cat, the death desk, the fog making its offer in stolen voices, and the kettle — and I'd also tell them "you can skim the clock chapter, trust me."

**Final: 6/10.** Best cat since Warriors, best afterlife bureaucracy since Skulduggery, buried under a layer of paperwork and fancy sentences about a foot too thick. There's an easy 8, maybe a 9, inside this book. It just needs someone to unpick it. (That's a book joke. The book would appreciate it. The book appreciates everything for slightly too long.)
