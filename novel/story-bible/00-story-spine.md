# Story bible 1: the spine, the beats, the voice

## What this book is actually about

**Surface:** an old cat investigates his witch's murder.

**Actual:** a debt that could not be paid, and what it cost everyone who
touched it.

Forty years ago Elspeth Vane saved Hollowmere by sewing the Mere shut. The
anchor of that working required one finished life. She had none to give — hers
was mortgaged to the seam itself. Her familiar, a grey tom named Bodkin,
offered his. Eight of his nine lives went down her needle into the bottom of
the lake. He walked away with one and was never once thanked, never once
sought out, never once told to his face that he was owed. She wrote *he is
owed* at the head of every page for forty years and never carried it the half
mile to the sea-wall where he sat.

Ash is what she did instead. He is her ninth attempt at restitution — a life
made new and paid forward to the kind, because the debt could not be paid at
the source.

**So the spine is:** Ash sets out to find a murderer and discovers he *is the
settlement of the crime*. The killer is not a stranger. He is Ash's elder
brother in the same craft, from the same lap, and the only difference between
them is which side of the account they were standing on when it came due.

Everything in the book should serve that. Anything that does not is decoration.

---

## The feeling

A warm, small, specific domestic world — a kettle at dusk, a window, a
lamplighter's round walked in the same order for forty years — violated once,
quietly, by someone who had a reason. Then one small competent creature walking
out into a large indifferent civic machine to get an accounting.

The warmth is the point of the book. The bureaucracy is funny because it is
indifferent, never because it is cruel. The tragedy is that nearly everyone
here was decent and it went wrong anyway.

The book is not grim, not edgy, and not clever at the reader's expense. It is
sad and it is fond.

## The voice: what has gone wrong

The narrator is currently **performing**. He reaches for the striking phrase
whether or not the phrase means anything. Three failure modes, all named by the
owner, all to be hunted chapter by chapter:

### 1. The unearned aphorism

> "They gave her to the tide at the turn of the evening, because the tide is
> the only thing in Hollowmere that has never been bribed."

It has the shape of worldly wisdom and no content. Nobody bribes tides
anywhere. No corruption theme pays this off. It is a sentence-shape, not a
thought, and it stops the funeral dead to admire itself.

**Test:** does the clause after "because" actually explain the clause before
it? If not, it is ornament wearing the costume of reasoning.

### 2. The joke that is not a joke

> "The arrangement works, and has worked for fifteen years, which is longer
> than any dog on this street has lasted, and I have seen off three."

Nine words into the character and he is boasting about outliving dogs. It is
not funny, it does not establish him, and it is actively *wrong about him* —
Ash is not a bully and never behaves like one again. It is there to sound dry.

**Test:** if a line is meant to be funny, is it funny? If it is meant to
characterise, does the rest of the book bear it out? If neither, cut.

### 3. The gratuitous dark image

> "wound around wrists and doorposts and the necks of sleeping children"

The sentence needs to say *threads are on everything*. It reaches past wrists
and doorposts to babies' throats for a shiver. It is cheap, and it makes the
narrator sound like he enjoys the frisson. He does not. Cut to "wrists and
doorposts and gateposts."

**Test:** is the darkest element in this image doing work the milder elements
could not?

### The rule that replaces all three

**Ash is an old cat writing a report about the worst year of his life, for
whoever comes after.** He is precise because precision is the only dignity
available to him. He is funny only when accuracy happens to be funny. He never
reaches.

Every sentence must answer yes to one of:
- does it tell me something true about the case?
- does it tell me something true about the world?
- does it tell me something true about the speaker or the person he is
  describing?

If a sentence only makes the prose sound good, it goes — however good it
sounds.

## Ash is old, and the book has not noticed

Fifteen years is old for a cat. The manuscript uses "fifteen years" only as
seniority, never as a body. This is both a missed opportunity and a
consistency problem: a fifteen-year-old cat fights a goose, a counting-house
hound, rag-wraiths, and crosses tidal flats at a sprint.

**This changes from here on.** Before the murder Ash was winding down: a
hearthrug cat whose working nights were behind him, stiff in the mornings,
warm at the correct times, quietly retired. The case is the last thing he will
ever do, and every physical act in the book costs him accordingly.

What this buys:
- The nine lives stop being a videogame allowance and become the reason an old
  cat can attempt any of this at all. He is spending a retirement.
- The chapter 2 training stops being a montage and becomes an old professional
  re-learning a trade too late, badly, and being hurt doing it.
- Bodkin — older still, and down to one life — reads as Ash's near future
  rather than as a monster.
- His endurance becomes admirable instead of routine.

Do not make him feeble. Make him *old*: economical, unhurried, reluctant to
jump what he used to jump, and quietly aware that he is not what he was.

## Game mechanics that must stop being stated

The novel inherited a card game's systems and says them out loud. The story
should read as a story; the game can keep the numbers.

**Delete as systems:**
- The four named strengths ("A cat runs on four strengths: the red, the green,
  the black... the fourth is the pale"). This is a stat block.
- "A cat's natures are a purse, if you keep them right." Resource management.
- "Ash: one. The weather: nothing." A scoreboard joke.
- Nine shadow-pockets enumerated as nine slots for nine lives.
- "banked shine" as a currency with a definition.

**Keep as sensation:**
- The red feeling at the bottom of a cat. That is adrenaline and instinct and
  it is good writing. It simply must not be one of four categories.
- Patience and going unseen are things Ash *does*, not things he spends.
- The pale stays, because it is load-bearing — it is hers, it is in him, it
  runs the Remembered Days, and it runs out at the climax. But it is described
  experientially, never as a stat: something of hers he carries, that he can
  feel go when he needs it, and that there is less of every time.
- The shadow-pockets stay as one eerie image — she sewed pockets into his
  shadow — because chapter 11 pays them off. Lose the inventory arithmetic.

## Two plot holes and their fixes

### A. How did the witches know Elspeth was dead?

Currently three witches simply arrive at first light. Nobody sent for them.

**Fix:** the Circuit is nine seats holding one binding between them. When one
of the nine goes out, the other eight feel the load come onto their own hands.
Cardew felt it at dusk. She came at first light rather than at once because
you do not walk Needle Lane in the dark when something is unpicking it.

This costs two sentences, closes the hole, deepens Cardew, and seeds the
finale — the seam wanting a Needle it no longer has.

### B. The candle: an hour of light in a dark parlor, and nobody saw?

Currently Ash reasons that an intruder waited alone in the dark burning a fine
candle for an hour. On an inhabited lane, at an hour when the whole street's
threads were being cut, someone would have seen a lit window.

**Fix — and this is the best single change available in the early chapters:**
*the shutters were closed.*

Elspeth never closed her shutters. She came to that window every evening of
Ash's life. On the last evening she closed them, set out the good beeswax
candle she had kept forty years, and put the kettle on for two.

So the darkness Ash reads as "she is out" is actually "she was expecting
someone, and did not want it seen." He notes the closed shutters in the
prologue as an anomaly he cannot place. In chapter 1 he builds an intruder out
of the wax. In chapter 12 he learns she lit it herself, for a guest she owed.

Every part of the hole closes and the re-read becomes devastating.

## What works — protect it

The kettle in all four beats. The Clerk, entire. The shroud-seam on the quay.
Bodkin's whole construction — the name given once, the guest-right, "you fight
like a housecat." The mirror-stitch becoming kinship. Fenn's lamp and Merrow's
two-line bill. Tansy. The beeswax candle.

## What does not work — fix it

The performed voice. The stated game systems. Ash's unused age. The witches'
unexplained arrival. The candle logistics. The scoreboard jokes. Metaphors
that obscure rather than clarify. Any sentence whose job is to sound good.
