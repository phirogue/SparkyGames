# Story bible 3: wants in collision

Conflict in this book is almost never good against evil. It is **two people who
are each entitled to what they want, wanting incompatible things.** Where a
scene has no collision of legitimate wants, it is a travelogue and should be
cut or given one.

---

## The engine: Elspeth vs Bodkin (forty years before page one)

She needed an anchor and had nothing to tender. He offered. She *entered his
offer in the record* so that neither of them could take it back — which is
either scrupulous honesty or the most convenient thing she ever did, and the
book must not decide for the reader.

- **She needed** to be forgiven without having to ask.
- **He needed** to be asked.

Neither is villainous. Both are recognisable. Forty years of silence follow,
each waiting for the other to cross a half mile of sea-wall. This is the whole
book in one unpaid invoice, and every other conflict is its interest.

---

## The spine: Ash vs Bodkin

They want **the same event from opposite sides**. Ash wants the murderer
brought to an accounting. Bodkin wants an accounting brought to *him*. The
climax is not a fight because it was never a fight — it is a settlement both
parties have been walking toward from different directions.

The collision is not "will Ash catch him" but **"what does Ash do with him"**:
avenge, forgive, or state the account. Vengeance is refused on the sea-wall.
Forgiveness is not Ash's to give — it was never his debt. What is left is the
thing his witch could not make herself do.

---

## Ash vs Merrow — the sharpest conflict in the book, currently underused

The Mere offers Ash a trade on the flats: stand aside, and every missing soul
in the book walks home on the morning ebb. It prices him correctly. It offers
him nothing for himself.

He refuses — and **Merrow's people stay in the water because of it.** Fenn is
taken during the weeks Ash spends contesting, and spent, and does not come
home. Ash's correct decision has a body attached to it.

Merrow gets the last word and does not soften it: he was taken in the contested
weeks, the hall has done that arithmetic, and the hall lights his lamp anyway.
This is the fairest bill anyone serves anyone in the book.

**Seed this earlier.** Merrow's want (her people back) must be established
hard enough by chapter 3 that the refusal on the flats costs the reader
something, not just Ash.

---

## Ash vs Cardew

She wants the Circuit to survive an eighth empty seat and does not want the old
files opened. He wants exactly the files she signed shut. She is not obstructive
— she is *complicit and ashamed*, which is worse and better. She resolves it by
choosing, late, and reading two forgotten names aloud before the agenda.

## Ash vs Wick

Wick wanted her ruined, dependent and owned. Her death destroys his plan — a
dead seamstress mends nothing. His want is *anti-murder*, which is exactly what
clears him and exactly what makes him contemptible. He is the book's argument
that paper can do what knives do.

## Ash vs Shear

She wants the ninth seat and the hand to deserve it; she has one and not the
other. Ash's claim on the estate blocks her. She is not a villain, she is a
person whose wanting outran her hands, and the book should pity her by the end.

## Ash vs the Clerk

The Clerk wants correct filings. Ash is the thickest irregularity below the
city. Their whole relationship is an institution slowly deciding to bend for
one creature and never once saying so.

## Ash vs Tansy

She wants to come; he wants her at home and safe. It resolves the only way it
can: "Mind the house, Tansy" — the exact words Elspeth used on Ash on the last
morning of her life. He has become the one who leaves.

## Bodkin vs the Mere

**His tragedy stacked on top of his crime.** The lake promised a refund it
could never pay: his eight lives *are* the seam, and thread that has become
stitch does not wind back. He has been carrying the proof in his own teeth all
season — every stitch he lifted came off as dead slack — and has refused to
look at it, because grief is the one auditor that will not read its own
evidence.

He is not only a murderer. He is a defrauded creditor who murdered for a
payment that never existed.

## The Mere vs everyone

Not a monster. A creditor with a legitimate historical claim that the city
stopped honouring. It does not hate. It hopes. Its bill is real and its methods
are monstrous, and both those things stay true at once.

---

## What works and must be protected

- **Bodkin arming Ash.** The best idea in the book. The mentor and the murderer
  are the same person and both relationships are sincere.
- **Wick's motive being anti-murder.** Elegant elimination.
- **Merrow's bill.** The book's most honest page.
- **Shear's silence.** She fails the stitch and says nothing, and the silence is
  the loudest thing at the hearing.
- **The Clerk's retirement of the folder, unresolved.**

## What does not work and must be fixed

- **The Gentleman is supposed to starve on settlement** and instead
  underwrites the largest settlement in the city's history with evident
  satisfaction. Either give him a visible cost or a stated longer game.
- **Cardew's guilt is asserted, not dramatised.** She says she signed the
  closings; we never see it cost her in a scene until the epilogue.
- **The Mere's offer needs Merrow physically present or freshly invoked**, or
  the refusal is abstract.
- **Tansy has no want that is ever frustrated** until the sea-wall stair.
  Give her one small earlier refusal so the last one lands.
