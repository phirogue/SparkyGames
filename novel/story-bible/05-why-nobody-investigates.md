# Story bible 6: why nobody else is investigating

**The owner's note:** it is strange that the most important stitcher in
Hollowmere is murdered and the only creature looking into it is her cat.

He is right, and the fix is not an excuse. It is the book's own theme, stated
where the reader can see it.

---

## The answer, in one line

**Hollowmere has no court for this.** That is why nobody investigates, and it
is also, exactly, why Bodkin murdered her: *"Forty years, kit, I was owed, and
there was no desk in this world or under it where the account could be
stated."*

The absence of a competent authority is not a plot convenience. It is the
condition the entire novel is about. Ash's whole arc is building standing
where none existed — and the last third of the book is him inventing, through
paperwork, the jurisdiction that should have existed on the first night.

Stated properly, the hole becomes the spine.

---

## The three reasons, each of which must appear on the page

### 1. The day-side has no case, because the crime is invisible

To anyone who cannot see the Weft, this is what happened: an elderly woman was
found dead in her own chair, in her own house, unmarked. No wound. No blood. No
forced entry. Nothing taken — not the silver thimbles, not the bolt of
moon-coloured cloth, not the rings on her hand.

There is no crime visible to the body that investigates crimes. The watch is
not negligent. There is nothing for them to see.

**Dramatise it, do not assert it.** In chapter 1, let the day-side watch
actually come: two constables, not unkind, mildly bored. They look at an old
woman who died in her chair. One of them writes it down. They cannot see forty
years of cut workings hanging off the walls all around them at one tidy height.
They step over the candle stub. They are gone in ten minutes.

Ash watches them miss everything, and understands: *if I do not do this,
nobody will.* That is the moment the book's premise becomes inevitable rather
than convenient. Roughly 200 words; take them out of ornament elsewhere in the
chapter.

### 2. The night-side has no constabulary at all

- **The Witches' Circuit** is a trade guild that maintains the Hush. Not
  police.
- **The Court of Whiskers** is a bar association that serves papers with dead
  moths. Not police.
- **The Hollow Court** files the dead and says plainly it does not sit on the
  affairs of the living. Not police.
- **The Magpie Exchange** sells information to anyone. Not police.

No institution in this city owns a killing on the night-side. This is stated
once, plainly, and never whined about.

### 3. The Circuit cannot look, because it is too busy being her

Seven remaining witches must now hold a district's worth of seams that took
Elspeth a decade to build — plus the great binding nobody will name in front of
a cat. Cardew's own assessment already exists in chapter 8: together they
*could not sew one year of her decade.*

**Make that the stated reason nobody investigates.** They are working
themselves hollow doing her job. They do not have a spare hand to ask who
killed her. There is something quietly terrible in that: the murder removed the
only person who could have absorbed the work the murder created.

And Cardew personally is compromised — she signed two closings forty years ago
and is watched. What she can give Ash, she gives: *"I'll hold what doors I have
left."*

---

## The Lamplighters: why the city is not in uproar over forty missing people

Currently the reader may reasonably ask why a mass disappearance causes no
civic alarm.

**The answer is already in chapter 12 and needs to appear earlier.** The hall
walked out on its own feet. Every soul in it had heard there was work
elsewhere, from the one voice that knew where elsewhere was. So the day-side
version is not *forty people vanished* — it is *the lamplighters took contract
work up the coast and left the city short-handed*, which is a labour dispute,
not a catastrophe.

The guild knows better and cannot prove it. The Hollow Court has returned their
filings marked *no appointments under those names*, which the day-side reads as
confirmation that nobody died, and which the guild reads as confirmation that
their people are being held somewhere.

Plant this in chapter 3 as a bitter, ordinary detail: the city is not
indifferent, it is *misinformed*, and the misinformation was arranged.

## Where each piece goes

| Where | What |
|---|---|
| **Ch 1** | The day-side watch comes and finds no case. Ash watches them miss the cut walls and step over the candle. The premise becomes inevitable. |
| **Ch 1** | Cardew states, in her unfinished register, what will and will not happen: the Circuit will hold the seams because it must, and nobody will look, and she has signed closings before. |
| **Ch 3** | The day-side believes the lamplighters took work up the coast. Merrow's fury at being disbelieved sharpens her want, which pays off at the flats and in the epilogue. |
| **Ch 8** | The Circuit session makes explicit that seven witches working flat out cannot hold what one woman held — and that this is why no one is investigating. |

## What this must never become

Not a complaint about institutions. Not a lecture. Nobody in this book is
corrupt or lazy — the watch is honest and blind, the Circuit is competent and
overwhelmed, Cardew is ashamed and old. The city fails to investigate the way
real cities fail: for a set of individually reasonable reasons that add up to
nobody doing anything.

Which is precisely the gap a small, stubborn, retired creature walks into.
