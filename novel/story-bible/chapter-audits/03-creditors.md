# Chapter audit: 3, "Creditors"

Baseline before rewrite: 3075 words, 12 em-dashes.
Audited against bible docs 00–04. Chapter-3 knowledge ceiling (bible 03):
*Bodkin exists, has a name, and gave him a rule. The name means nothing to him.
Wick is buying her debts.* Nothing further.

---

## 1. Defect list

### D1 — unearned aphorism

> "Wickrow after dark is a district that has never once seen itself."

Pure sentence-shape. It has the cadence of an insight and does not survive being
asked what it means — a district cannot see itself, so the negation asserts
nothing. Bible 04's test: it would move to any other chapter unchanged. The
paragraph beneath it already does the real work (light billed by the quarter,
stock control), so the opener only needs to be true and plain.

> "Half-seconds are the whole currency of this case."

Two defects in eight words. It is an aphorism, and it is **future-telling** —
Ash in ch3 has no basis for a claim about what the case runs on, and the
sentence is there to elbow the reader toward ch7's stolen half-second. Ch11 ¶51
confirms the design: Brindle testifies that Ash "was too new to know what he had
seen." A narrator who announces the currency is not too new. Cut; keep "I was
new. I filed it instead."

> "That is a lamplighter's honesty and I respect it and it is a doorway exactly
> the width of your own wits."

The figure is good and the throat-clearing around it is not. "I respect it" is
Ash awarding marks. Reduce to the figure.

> "Lady's Day was most of a season out. I set the date at the head of the
> ledger, and it did not occur to me yet to wonder whose it would turn out to
> be."

**Future-telling**, and the coyest kind — it tells the reader a fact is coming
without paying for it. Cut the clause; keep the date.

### D2 — the joke that is not a joke

> "a shift-boss of the Lamplighters, it turns out, swings a ladder like a
> guildhall falling"

"It turns out" plus a simile reaching for scale. It is the narrator being
entertaining during the one fight in the chapter that ought to be showing us
what a torn flank costs. Replace with the plain fact and the wound.

> "The counting room was warm, and lit by its own stock, and guarded, because of
> course it was."

"Because of course it was" is performed weariness. The sentence is better
without a shrug on the end.

> "But Merrow watched me reading, and read me reading"

Cleverness at the seam of a good paragraph.

### D3 — gratuitous dark image

None. The chapter's darkest image — "coats that kept their shapes the way the
wraith on Needle Lane had kept a shape" — is precise, load-bearing, and pays
off in ch12/ch13. Protect it.

### D4 — stated game mechanics

> "slid it into my shadow, into one of the nine pockets she sewed there"

Same defect as ch2: the count. Ch11 ¶149 needs "Nine pockets. Nine lives." to
arrive as a discovery. Drop the number, keep the pocket.

### Repeated motif

> "She unpicked gates in front of me four hundred times. Watching is not doing;
> but I had stopped merely watching the night I mended her door."

Both halves are repeats of ch2 ¶103, which uses "four hundred times" and
"Watching is not doing" in one sentence about the same woman doing the same
work. "Four hundred" additionally appears in ch4 ¶47 and twice in ch11. Ch2
keeps both; ch3 states the fact plainly and does not restate the lesson —
he is about to demonstrate it, which is stronger than repeating it.

> "colleague" — ¶133 ("the way you look at a colleague") and ¶143 ("finally
> found a colleague"), ten lines apart, both about Merrow. The word is also
> doing heavy service in ch1, ch7, ch8 and ch12. One of the two goes.

### Image plus gloss

> "So they are SOMEWHERE---" and here her voice did the thing that grief does
> when it has been on shift too long, which is nothing at all, "--- and this
> hall lights lamps until they walk back in."

The gloss is a narrator's generalisation about grief inserted into the middle of
the chapter's best line of dialogue, and it interrupts Merrow mid-sentence to do
it. Cut the gloss entirely; her voice not changing is more frightening
unexplained. (This also removes two em-dashes.)

Also a **formatting defect**: `SOMEWHERE` is set in capitals where the
manuscript's convention throughout is `\emph{}`.

> "something about a cat asking after the missing instead of the murder seemed
> to settle a question I had not known I was sitting."

"A question I had not known I was sitting" is a figure that does not resolve —
one does not sit a question. Plainer is better here.

### Narratorial throat-clearing

> "Think what that costs a guild that cannot spare the oil"

Direct instruction to the reader. State the cost; the reader can think.

### Ash's age — the counting-house hound

The fight is recently written and mostly good: the yard is correctly a box, the
hound correctly wins by geometry rather than speed, and "I record the exchange
without pride, because the arithmetic of it was not in my favor" is the right
register. Two problems.

1. **One athletic beat contradicts the premise.** "kicked off the rain-butt to
   change direction in the air, and hit the woodstack running" is a young cat's
   escape — a mid-air redirect off a barrel. It also makes the escape a *trick*
   rather than a piece of knowledge. The line immediately before it already
   states the correct principle: *I am small enough to go where a hound has to
   turn.* The escape should be that sentence executed — make him commit, go
   under the head on the side he must swing his whole body to answer, and refuse
   the gate because the gate is his. Bodkin's rule four paragraphs later
   ("Watch the paws, not the claws… the dog told you where it would be four
   moves before it got there") is a correction of *reading*, not of athleticism,
   and lands harder if Ash never had speed to lose.
2. **"took one of his ears away with me"** — Ash trading damage with a hound
   twice his mass reads as the bully register bible 04 warns about, and is not
   how a fifteen-year-old cat survives a yard. Losing the flank and taking
   nothing is both truer and more costly.

**The cost does not persist.** "I left a strip of my flank in that yard" is
never mentioned again, though the chapter goes on to a wisp fight and a piece of
public needlework. The flank must be present at both: it opens during the fight
on Merrow's round, and it makes getting up onto the meeting-board take two tries
in front of the whole guild. That second beat is worth more than the fight — it
is Ash being visibly old in public and doing the work anyway.

### Timeline error

> "They had been living inside a torn ward for a season"
> "because I had been practicing on grief for weeks by then"

Both are wrong by the calendar. The hall was emptied on the night of the murder
(bible 03), the funeral is ch1, ch2 is a handful of nights, and ch3 opens "at
the next dawn." A season has not passed; weeks have not passed. "Practicing on
grief" is also a strained figure — it names a feeling rather than a skill, and
what he actually practised on was a door frame.

### Merrow: the chapter's biggest missed opportunity

Bible 02 is explicit — *"Ash vs Merrow — the sharpest conflict in the book,
currently underused… Merrow's want must be established hard enough by chapter 3
that the refusal on the flats costs the reader something, not just Ash."* Ch9
spends her on the Mere's offer; ch13 gives her the fairest bill in the book. Ch3
is where the account has to be opened.

What is already there and works: the coats over the boots, "Not dead," the
filings coming back marked, the chalk tally, "this hall lights lamps until they
walk back in." That is a good scene. It is not yet a **debt**.

Three sharpenings, none of them length:

1. **A count.** She is procedural; procedural people give numbers. Eleven names,
   re-filed every week, coming back marked every week. (Deliberately not nine —
   nine is spoken for by lives, pockets, bells and seats, and a coincidental
   nine here would ring a bell the book does not intend.)
2. **The unpaid labour.** Their rounds are still walked, and she walks two of
   them herself. This is the exact thing the guild does again in ch13 ("the hall
   also walked the rounds while the seam was argued"), so planting it here makes
   her last speech an echo instead of an assertion.
3. **The day-side does not believe her.** *Added to brief.* The respectable
   half of Hollowmere has settled on a comfortable story: the lamplighters took
   contract work up the coast. It explains everything, it costs nobody anything,
   and the guild cannot disprove it — you cannot produce evidence that a man did
   *not* go somewhere. This is the load-bearing addition, because it converts
   her grief into a grievance with a mechanism:
   - it explains why the filings matter so much to her (paper is the only
     contradiction available, and paper comes back marked);
   - it makes the chalk tally an argument rather than a ritual — she is
     maintaining a record against a story;
   - it gives her fury a second floor. Bible 01 says she *needs* to accept that
     one of them will not come back. She cannot begin to, because accepting
     anything at all sounds, from the outside, like conceding the coast;
   - and it makes Ash's arrival matter: he is the first party in the chapter to
     treat the missing as missing. That is what buys the round, the shift-book
     and the knot at his chest — and it is why her bill in the epilogue is a
     bill and not a reproach.

   Her register stays procedural throughout. She does not editorialise about the
   day-side; she reports what was said, flat, and the flatness is the fury.

### Merrow's epigrams — strip

Checked line by line. "Rounds first. Paper after." "I answer what is asked. No
more." "The hall owes the cat." "That is Wick's hand… and that is the night
nobody signed." "Wisps do not learn a route. Somebody taught them the taste.
Find the teacher." All procedural, none witty, all correct. **The wit attributed
to her is in fact the narrator's** — the grief-on-shift gloss (cut above) and
the "grim satisfaction of a woman whose grievance has finally found a colleague"
tag. Removing those leaves her clean; no dialogue of hers needs rewriting.

### Protected, untouched

Bodkin's guest-right speech entire; the giving of his name; "Watch the paws, not
the claws"; "You fight like a housecat. — I was one. — Were."; Brindle's
half-second pause on the cart-slot seal; Merrow's "Wisps do not learn a route.
Somebody taught them the taste. Find the teacher."

## 2. Purpose of the chapter

**Job:** the case stops being about a thread and becomes about money — and Ash
acquires, in one night, the two relationships that will decide the end of the
book without either of them looking like it at the time.

By the end the reader must:

- **Feel** that Ash is out of his depth and being carried by other people's
  competence, and that both of them charge. Bodkin gives him a rule and a name
  and asks nothing, which should feel like luck and is not. Merrow gives him a
  round and a shift-book and ties a knot in his tether, and that *is* a bill,
  entered in front of witnesses.
- **Learn** three things: Wick bought every debt Elspeth owed in one week and
  called them due on one date; she had been sewing his warehouse shut for two
  years for nothing; and something taught a pair of wisps a lamplighter's route.
- **Believe** two contradictory things at once about the grey tom: that he is
  the best thing that has happened to Ash all season, and that the reader should
  not have been able to say why he was in that alley. Nothing in the chapter may
  wink at this. Bodkin never lies here and never will.

The chapter's emotional centre is not the ledger. It is a hall full of coats
over boots where the city has agreed on a comfortable story, and a cat who
asks after the missing before he asks for anything.
