# Fifth-edition revision plan

Source: four blind critics (mystery fan, fantasy fan, young reader, professional
writer — all 6/10) and one mythology/worldbuilding advisor, in `reviews-5e/`.
All five read the manuscript cold, with no access to prior reviews or notes.

---

## The one decision everything else hangs off

Four critics out of four named the same primary defect: **the reader identifies
Bodkin between chapters 3 and 5, and the narrator does not turn until chapter
11.** All four independently rejected the chapter 11 lampshade ("You have been
waiting chapters for me to turn around") as diagnosis rather than treatment.

The clues that give him away are the best construction in the book — the
mirror-stitch plant, "it travels like a cat," the name glossed on arrival. They
cannot be removed. So the answer is not to hide him better.

**The answer is to let Ash catch up, deliberately, and change what the last
third is about.**

New shape of the knowledge:

| Chapter | Ash's state |
|---|---|
| 3 | Meets Bodkin. Name given, filed, unread. |
| 7 | Parameters land (small, low, four-footed, tailed). Writes the name — and strikes it out. **One refusal, not three.** |
| 8 | Goes to his teacher with the parameters hot. Asks the question. Gets a true answer. Chooses not to hear it. |
| 9 | His own ward, lifted mirror-wise, a woman taken. Then the neap knot, kin to the knots on his own thread. **He writes the name and does not strike it out.** |
| 10–11 | A cat who knows, building a legal instrument, saying nothing to anyone — including a court that would act if he spoke. |
| 11 | The moonlight/shadow scene now reveals **mechanism, not identity**: nobody taught Bodkin the stitch, because the stitch teaches itself from the lap. Bodkin is not an imitator. He is Ash's elder brother in the same craft, from the same lamplit parlour. |

That last change is the load-bearing one. Chapter 11's discovery stops being
something the reader beat by eight chapters and becomes something the reader
could not have pre-empted: not *who* but *what he is to me*. The settlement
scene then arrives as inevitable rather than merciful.

Everything below serves that spine or repairs damage the critics found around
it.

## A. Structure

**A1. Compress three refusals of the name to one (ch. 7).** Currently ch. 7 ("a
name I would not write"), ch. 8 ("I did not write the name"), ch. 9 ("a name I
would not write") each perform the same coyness about a name the reader has held
since ch. 3. Keep ch. 7's. Replace the others with **behaviour** — the
grey-tom snare-hunt already dramatises the denial far better than narration
announcing it.

**A2. New scene: the roof, ch. 8.** Ash brings his teacher the parameters —
small, low, four-footed, tailed, breath held — and asks him what kind of
creature crosses a sentry's street. Bodkin answers truthfully, as he always
does, and the truth is a description of himself. Ash hears it, files it, and
walks home having decided not to have heard it. Converts dead who-suspense into
live dread; gives the middle its missing antagonist presence without breaking
cover.

**A3. New loss: the midwife, ch. 9.** The middle third has no attributed
atrocity — the enemy is off-page from the prologue to ch. 12, and the one
disaster (the Hake fire) is self-inflicted. Fix: Ash arrives too late at a
Mereside house whose door-ward *he himself deepened* in ch. 8. The ward did not
fail. It was **lifted, mirror-wise, by a hand that knew the stitch**, and its
keeper is gone. Irreversible, attributed, and caused by Ash's own labour. This
is the beat that makes him write the name.

**A4. Cut the triple-told backstory to two beats.** The eight lives are
currently delivered three times: the ch. 8 dream, the ch. 9 vault docket, the
ch. 11 confession.
- **Ch. 8 dream:** keep the nine bells, the barge, her walking on the black
  glass, the grey shape at the tactful corner. **Withhold the eight lights going
  out.** Cut the explicit accounting ("Eight spent. Nine owed").
- **Ch. 9 docket:** now detonates instead of confirming. *Write down that he
  offers* is read for the first time here.
- **Ch. 11 confession:** keeps its full load as the first telling in her voice.

**A5. Install the clock.** The neap deadline currently arrives in the same
paragraph it is won ("I want the reader to have spent the four days knowing
there was a race"). Post it at the end of ch. 9 — the blind postmaster already
hands over the mechanism ("the mender comes at the neap") — and run the
auditors' bulletins (*holding; holding; holding at the anchor only*) dusk by
dusk from ch. 10. Name Lady's Day in scene in ch. 11, with Wick watching from
the gallery as his own trap closes on the wrong quarry.

**A6. Compress the anthology middle.**
- **Ch. 4:** trim the pigeon election's procedural middle; compress the
  Fish-Giver caper (keep the lease-defect lesson entire — the finale stands on
  it); keep Tansy and the purr whole.
- **Ch. 8:** **cut the caulker's-shadow case.** Its thesis (*held is not spent*)
  is already carried by the purr escrow, the blind postmaster and the fog's
  offer. Transplant the wraith's pointing arm into ch. 9's Mereside descent.
- **Ch. 10:** halve the founder's-contract backstory and the auditor procedure.
  Keep the golden hour and the beat where Ash looks away from it at the Mere.

**A7. Give the finale resistance.**
- Bodkin **contests one line** of the stated account before he folds. Forty
  years should not dissolve in a single speech.
- The bell petition meets a real objection from the Court's own interest — a
  century of holdings is being disgorged, and institutions do not thank you.
- Restore the near-loss so the redemption is a countdown, not a recounting.

**A8. Weigh the earlier victims.** The Tatterstage lead and Widow Sarn get one
shared sentence in the settlement. Give each a line with the weight of the
candle's — Sarn's terrier, which waited two winters on a step, is the item at
which the one eye should close. Give the reopened files a scene in the epilogue,
not a clause.

## B. The metaphysics (advisor's Part A)

**B1. Bodkin's competing claim to the estate.** The advisor's sharpest catch: by
the Court's own logic — a bond tied by the decedent, severed by no recognised
instrument — the *first* familiar is the senior legatee, and his thread is in
better repair than Ash's. A courtroom containing a hostile rival, an
institutional objector and a broker holding paper on every party never mentions
him.

Fix, and it becomes the best beat in the hearing: **the Clerk asks whether any
other familiar of the decedent survives.** Ash, on the record, as an officer of
the estate, must answer. He answers *one*, and gives the name, and does not
accuse. The Clerk — who has been reading "close work, old-fashioned" on Ash's
own thread all season — makes the connection silently and lets it pass. Telling
the truth and betraying his teacher become the same act. The Court rules an
unpresented claim sleeps.

**B2. Guest-right, with teeth.** Plant in ch. 3, **in Bodkin's own mouth**, with
the rest of his roof-lore: nobody collects off a lit candle; oldest rule on the
night-side, older than the desk. Pay it in ch. 12. The beeswax stub stops being
the book's prettiest symbol and becomes the exhibit that damns him — and the
reader who remembers ch. 3 realises he told Ash the rule he broke, the same move
as giving him his name. It also turns "I have never once in forty years been a
thief" into a lie he has been telling himself.

**B3. Cutting is a standing, not a skill.** Plant in ch. 2 as Elspeth's
remembered teaching: anything can fray a thread; cutting one clean is a right,
and the Weft knows who held it. Repairs the ch. 11 ruling ("severed by no
instrument this Court recognizes"), which currently rests on an unstated
distinction between *cut* and *severed-in-law*, and adds a counted debt to the
ch. 12 account: every tether on Needle Lane, cut without standing.

**B4. Price Ash's stitching.** The book's largest unpriced transaction: Elspeth
pays for every stitch "at the pull," and Ash sews half the night-side for free.
Give him a currency — **sleep** — paid at the pull, plant it at the parlour-door
mend in ch. 2, and let it compound through ch. 3 and ch. 8. This converts the
Hake fire from a character flaw ("I was proud and tired") into a *ledger event*
the reader has watched accumulate, and gives the Clerk's "Sew less tired" the
force of an auditor reading a balance.

**B5. The pale does not refill.** State it once and let the level drop visibly
across the season, so "spent to the lining" in ch. 12 ends a countdown the
reader has been keeping. Ch. 2 already has the beat ("the bottom of that purse —
not empty, but findable"); it needs three or four more touchpoints.

**B6. Define banked shine** once, at the ch. 4 refusal: the residue the pale
leaves behind when it is spent, which is the only coin the desk takes from a
walk-in.

**B7. Split the sources of the Remembered Days.** The pale is *her* memory made
spendable, so it can only pay out days she lived. The deep-history dream (the
city before, the tithe, the Drowned in the market) must come from somewhere
else: **the seam itself**, slept on, remembering what it was made to refuse. One
paragraph in ch. 8. Better mythology and it closes the hole.

**B8. Name the tithe.** One line from the Grey Boss, who already remembers the
ninth bell: the city paid the water **the first of everything** — first catch,
first light of a new hearth, first child of a bad season. Makes the Mere's claim
legitimate *and* monstrous, which is the only reading that makes Elspeth's seam
a moral crisis rather than an unexamined civic good.

**B9. The water keeps everything but a bell.** Plant as a fishwives' saying in
ch. 3 or 4. The finale currently wins by paperwork alone; planted as folk truth
first, the redemption reads as the law catching up with what the quay always
knew.

**B10. Pay the paw-print stamp.** Noted three times, never explained. One
exchange with the Clerk: the first witness of the desk was a cat, because cats
are the creatures who see the appointment coming. Grounds the nine, the Clerk's
fondness, the Court of Whiskers' standing and the definition of a true death at
one stroke.

**B11. Why the lake waited forty years.** It cannot spend its holdings against
a seam that is whole — you cannot push on a wall with money. Only when the
anchor thinned did burning stock buy anything. One line in ch. 12.

**B12. Why the lake needed an agent at all.** Only a hand that knows the stitch
can lift it. One line, Bodkin's, ch. 12.

**B13. The Understudy's testimony.** She witnesses the one thing the technique
exists to prevent. Fix with a clause: she was already dying, mid-line, half out
of the schedule — *the purchase did not cover the dying*. Her testimony now
costs her her death, which is better than it being free.

**B14. The gargoyle's blink has a ledger.** A monument's watch *is* its account;
three centuries of attention banked in granite is the richest unguarded purse in
the city. One line from Sootbeard.

**B15. Nine: made, not allotted.** The Clerk should react to the *fact* — a
mortal woman minted life — not merely to the number. Establish that nines are
filed perhaps once a century, always a witch's work, always a debt paid forward.

## C. Consistency (caught by the professional writer and the mystery fan)

1. The stairs refrain references a rationing promise that was never made. Either
   plant the promise in the prologue or cut the reference; make the count
   self-consistent across prologue / ch. 8 / ch. 9.
2. Ch. 5's "the one uncut strand" — it was cut, on the page, in ch. 2. Rephrase
   (*the strand that kept its house end*, or similar).
3. Wick's "thirty years" against the forty-year chronology. Align or motivate.
4. Wick's unfinished sentence ("Out of — reach") needs its object: one clause
   explaining why a monopolist spends a premium to remove his own free
   ward-stitcher.
5. Tatterstage chronology and pronouns: "his breath" and "my lady" cannot both
   refer to the lead as written. The lead (a stitcher, Cardew's second victim)
   dies of the bought fog; the Understudy dies mid-line of the purchased silence
   the same night. State the two deaths in order.
6. Brindle's half-second pause on the cart-slot seal (ch. 3) is never cashed.
   One line in her ch. 11 testimony: she recognised favour-paper and held her
   tongue, because the Exchange does not price the Gentleman's stock aloud.
7. The parlour hour: ch. 1 reads the beeswax as an intruder waiting alone in the
   dark. Ch. 12 reveals she set it out and he lit it with her alive. Flag ch. 1's
   version clearly as Ash's *inference*, so the reversal is a re-reading and not
   a contradiction.

## D. Line and voice

Three critics complained the whole cast speaks in one mint, and the young reader
put it plainest: "20% less chewing."

1. **Strip the epigrams from Merrow, Cardew and Tansy.** Merrow's register is
   procedural terseness ("I answer what is asked. No more") and she is strongest
   when least witty. Cardew carries age through incompleteness. Tansy is
   concrete and literal, and her best lines already are. Aphorism stays with
   Ash, the Clerk, Brindle, the Gentleman and Bodkin — and gains contrast for
   free.
2. **Ration the formula phrases.** "Close work, old-fashioned" keeps only its
   four clue-bearing appearances (the Clerk's audit, the neap knot, the
   counterfoil, the ch. 12 candle); cut it everywhere decorative, including the
   vole. Break the "dead before I finished being —" mould after one use; ch. 8's
   earns it, ch. 4's does not.
3. **Cut simile density by roughly a third** in chs. 4, 8 and 10. Flag every
   "the way [clause]" and keep the best one per page.
4. **Reduce em-dashes by about a third**; break the eight-line paragraphs in
   chs. 5, 9 and 10.
5. **Gloss or cut the Latin.** *Amicus curiae* is already glossed beautifully
   ("which is Court for a friend of the court, in moth form"). *In situ* and
   *lepidopteral* are not. If a word would stop a bright twelve-year-old, either
   explain it in-world or use the plain one.
6. **Three scenes escape the ledger entirely.** Strip every
   account/debt/price/receipt word from the golden hour, the great sewing, and
   the missing walking home. The bookkeeping voice hits twice as hard on either
   side of unpriced wonder.
7. **Write the counting-house hound fight** (ch. 3) instead of summarising it
   into a joke. It is Bodkin's entrance and the book's first physical crisis. The
   young reader wants one real action beat in the middle; this is it, already
   in place and currently skipped.

## E. What must not change

- The prologue. Every critic bought the book on it.
- The kettle, in all three of its appearances.
- The Clerk, entire. Funniest character in the book by unanimous consent.
- Tansy's escrow line and her mirror-of-a-mirror hem.
- The shroud-seam on the quay, and "It took three tries."
- Fenn's lamp and Merrow's two-line bill in the epilogue — the fairest accounting
  in the manuscript.
- The chest-ledger page that stays blank.
- The ending's shape: Bodkin sews himself into the bottom of the world, by
  appointment, on the ninth stroke.
