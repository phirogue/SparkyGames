# Review by a Young Reader (age 12, reads a LOT)

I'm 12, almost 13. I've read all of Percy Jackson, Wings of Fire, Warriors, Nevermoor, and Amari and the Night Brothers, and I'm starting to read older books. I read this whole book about a cat detective in a foggy city, and this is the honest version of my review — the one I'd give my best friend if she asked "should I read this," not the polite one you'd say to the author's face.

**Verdict:** The truth? If a teacher hadn't assigned this, I don't think I would have finished it. The first chapter is genuinely amazing — the witch dies, the kettle is screaming, the cat takes it off the fire himself and it takes three tries and "nobody will ever know" — I was ALL in. And the last two chapters got me back and even made me tear up. But in between there is SO much talking about debts, ledgers, contracts, escrow, liens, dockets, and counterfoils that huge stretches of this book feel like a homework packet about banking that happens to have a cat in it. I skimmed real amounts of chapters 5, 6, 10, and 11 and I'm being honest about that because a review where I pretend I read every word of the clock-lawyer chapter isn't a real review. This book has a 9/10 beginning, a 9/10 ending, and a middle that kept daring me to quit.

**Rating: 6/10.** Amazing bones, buried under paperwork and sentences that never end.

---

## The Good

I'm keeping this section short on purpose, because the stuff that's good is REALLY good and doesn't need me padding it.

**The kettle.** Best thing in the book, full stop. "The kettle was still screaming. It had been screaming this whole time, and I did not hear it until now. She hates it when it does that." And it comes back — the guild hall's cold kettle, and at the very end, "it has not screamed once since the night it screamed, because there is always somebody in this house to take it off the fire in time. One paw. One try, these days." That's real writing. When Elspeth's note in chapter 12 says "The kettle is not your fault. I know you took it off the fire. I know it took three tries" — I cried. Actual tears. That's the moment the whole book exists for and it earns it.

**The scary stuff, when the book lets it be scary.** The rag-wraiths ("clothes whose owners are missing. Not dead — *missing*"), the empty coats hanging over pairs of boots in the Lamplighters' hall, and the one truly great horror scene in chapter 9: Fenn walking into the fog with nothing behind his eyes while Ash screams under his boots, and "his lamp went on burning where he left it." Then the fog talks to Ash in the stolen voices of the missing, including a child saying "And we walk home tomorrow." That page is the best scene in the book that isn't about a kettle.

**Tansy.** "This is my counsel. He is the best one." A kitten who sold her purr to save her brothers, gets it back, purrs so hard she falls off the rug. She's the only character my age in the entire book, she's the best character in the entire book, and the book barely uses her — which is a Bad, so see below.

**The Bodkin reveal, mechanically.** The idea that you can't be taught the stitch, you have to have LIVED next to the stitcher — "a familiar's paws do not learn the stitch from the needle's side. They learn it from the lap" — is genuinely clever, and "You fight like a housecat" landing differently on reread is a good trick. Credit where it's due.

That's it. That's The Good. Everything else people will praise about this book — the voice, the cleverness, the world — is also the exact stuff that made it exhausting, and that goes below.

---

## The Bad

**The adults never, ever stop talking.** Count the actual scenes in this book: most of them are Ash sitting somewhere while an older character delivers a speech. Cardew speeches. Merrow speeches. Wick gets a full page of monologue in chapter 5. The Quiet Gentleman in chapter 6 talks in riddles for basically the entire chapter — "It does not settle, it only ends," "Water does not ask," "the not-asking is what the quiet costs" — and I had to keep rereading his paragraphs to figure out what he actually SAID underneath the fancy. The Clerk speeches. Sootbeard speeches. The blind pigeon "speeches" in tap-code. In Percy Jackson, when an adult talks too long, Percy makes fun of them and something explodes. Here, the cat listens respectfully and then "files it in the ledger of his chest." Again.

**The vocabulary is not "challenging," it's a wall.** I'm a strong reader and here is a real list of words this book expected me to know: gravamen (they named a whole DISTRICT that), counterfoil, consignee, escrow, intestate, promissory notes, lien, surety, "residuary legatee," "amicus curiae," "in absentia," "in situ." The moth joke in chapter 11 — the Court of Whiskers files a dead moth as an "amicus brief" — is probably hilarious if you're a lawyer. I'm not a lawyer. I'm twelve. Half the jokes in this book are for grown-ups who already know what the words mean, and when the joke IS the word, kids like me are just locked out of it.

**The sentences.** So many sentences in this book have two dashes, a semicolon, a parenthesis, and a little side-comment before they let you out. Chapter 11 has a single paragraph — the one clearing the senior grey cat with "session-rolls" and "minuted" lives — that I read three times before I understood it just means "he has an alibi." When I have to decode a paragraph like a puzzle, and the prize inside is "this minor character you barely met is innocent," that's when I start skimming. And once I started skimming, the book made it easy to keep skimming, because —

**Every single fight is skipped.** This is the one that would make my friends quit by chapter 3. The book keeps winding up for fights and then going "anyway, I won." The hound in chapter 3: "I am writing this book, and that hound tells the story differently, and we are both entitled." Funny ONCE. But it's the goose (skipped), the dog (skipped), the wisps (skipped), the THREE rag-wraiths in chapter 6 ("the drover's coat came down on me like weather off a hill" — done, he's dead), and even the prologue fight with the actual murderer is like four sentences. Ash has four color-coded powers — red, green, black, and the pale Moonlight — which is the coolest system in the book, and it's used almost entirely for... paying tolls while walking through fog. Imagine Wings of Fire where every battle happens off-screen and gets replaced with a scene about invoices.

**Zero danger, and the book knows it.** Ash tells you in the PROLOGUE that he's writing this book. So he lives. He "dies" twice and both times I felt nothing about his survival, because obviously. The Toll — dying costs him a memory of her — is honestly a scarier idea than dying, and the scene where he reaches for the chart-table memory and "the room the fact was kept in is gone" is good and sad. But that's one moment doing the work that actual suspense should have been doing for thirteen chapters.

**Tansy vanishes.** The only kid-aged character disappears for basically chapters 5 through 9, which are exactly the chapters where I most needed someone to be young, ask dumb questions, and get the fancy words explained. That's not a coincidence. That's the book forgetting who could have saved its middle.

**The suspects get cleared by paperwork, three separate times.** Wick: cleared by his own ledgers (ch 5). Shear: cleared by a stitching demo, which at least is a scene (ch 11). The senior grey: cleared by MORE ledgers, in that unreadable paragraph (ch 11). By the third time I'd figured out the book's one move — "the answer is always in a document" — and detective stories aren't fun when you know the move.

**Timeline soup.** Forty years since the seam. Fifteen years of Ash. A parcel mailed thirty years ago, containing a spool ten years OLDER than the mailing, with a note NEWER than both, in chapter 11. I did actual subtraction, in the middle of what was supposed to be an emotional scene, to check the book's math. That's a design fail.

## The Ugly

This is the stuff I'd actually rant about to a friend.

**Chapter 10 nearly ended me.** The Horologists' section is a full explainer of what a "lien" is: "the taking-back declared, itemized, and spent in the open, before witnesses, with the balance struck." There are auditors. The auditors fall in love with bookkeeping. There is a sentence celebrating "exceptional bookkeeping." I put the book down here and only came back because I had to. Yes, the golden hour at the end of the section is genuinely beautiful — sunset running backward, cats on every roofline — but it's dessert after a plate of tax forms, and no kid I know eats the tax forms to get there.

**Chapter 11 is a courtroom episode, and the climax of the mystery is skimmable.** It literally announces its own format: "parties, testimony, exhibits, ruling." The ruling is written in real legalese — "is not the estate's property but its residuary legatee" — and I skimmed the RULING. Of the trial. That decides everything. Worse, the Gentleman's exhibit-dump paragraph crams about four huge reveals (the brokerage trail, the cart's cargo of "street-thread, harvested, spooled," the identity of the "gentleman" behind the cart, the payment in wound-down life) into ONE dense block, and I flat-out missed the cart answer the first time. When your biggest answers are hidden in your hardest paragraph, that's the book failing the reader, not the reader failing the book.

**The climax of the whole novel is a conversation, and the villain-lake is defeated by a filing.** Ash walks onto the flats, sits down in front of the murderer, and talks. The talk is good — "and put the kettle on," I said. "For two." is a killer line — but the giant ancient evil under the lake, built up for thirteen chapters, gets beaten by a foreclosure: "found in contempt in absentia, at the bottom of itself." That is a joke for adults, at the exact moment the book owed me fear. The flats rising "like the lid of an eye" and the Drowned closing in lasts about one paragraph, and then Bodkin handles the whole sacrifice himself while Ash's offer is politely declined. The hero of this book never wins a single physical confrontation on the page, including the last one.

**The book hides its own treasure.** Ten chapters build up Box 9-Crown, and when it opens, Ash says: "I am not going to transcribe her confession." Excuse me?? The chapter 12 note proves Elspeth's voice on the page is the most powerful weapon this book has, and the book WITHHOLDS the confession on purpose, for in-character reasons I understand and do not accept. I felt cheated, and "cheated" is the feeling that makes kids not recommend a book.

**The tics.** "The ledger of my chest." "I filed it." "In the small honest hand." "The stairs up are longer than the fall down." "Close work, old-fashioned." Each one is nice the first time. By chapter 9 I was spotting them like license plates on a car trip, which means I was outside the story counting instead of inside it feeling.

**Real talk about my friends:** of the five people I'd normally lend a book to, I think ONE finishes this. Two quit in chapter 3 when the second fight in a row gets skipped and the plot becomes about a warehouse's accounting. Two quit at chapter 10. The one who finishes cries at the end and then yells at me, because the ending proves the book could have been amazing the whole time.

---

## Specific Recommendations

What this book NEEDS so kids like me don't quit:

1. **Cut the lien-law lecture in chapter 10 by at least half.** Keep the golden hour, keep the dying clockmaker (that part has heart). The legal trick should be sayable in two kid-repeatable sentences: "They weren't stealing time. The city owed them a sunrise and they had the receipt." If a 12-year-old can't repeat the trick to a friend, the trick is too long.
2. **Put at least TWO full fights on the page, with the four strengths doing visible things.** The wax-keeper fight in chapter 5 (leading it around its own candles so the heat melts it) is the model — clever, visual, complete. Do that for the three rag-wraiths in chapter 6 and for something in chapter 12. Right now the coolest power system in the book is only used to pay fog tolls.
3. **Keep Tansy in chapters 5–9, and make her the question-asker.** Every time the book needs a word like "escrow" or "residuary legatee," let Tansy demand what it means so Ash has to say it plainly. That fixes the vocabulary wall AND the no-kids problem AND gives the middle a heartbeat, all in one move.
4. **Rewrite chapter 11's reveal paragraph as separate scenes or at least separate paragraphs.** One reveal per paragraph: the cart's cargo, the walker behind the cart, the client's payment. These are the answers to mysteries from chapters 2 and 4 — they deserve their own space, not a shared sentence with two semicolons.
5. **Show the confession.** Give us half a page of Elspeth's actual words from Box 9-Crown in chapter 11, not a summary with two quoted lines. The book already proves (chapter 12 note) that her voice is its nuclear weapon. Fire it twice.
6. **Add real, on-page danger to chapter 12 before the settlement.** Let the Drowned actually reach Ash. Let him genuinely lose a life at the flats fighting, not just crossing. THEN let the talking win. The conversation is the right ending — it just needs my heart rate up first, and it needs Ash to have won or survived at least one physical thing himself in this entire book.
7. **Front matter: a map and a one-page timeline.** Thimblefield, the Shambles, Wickrow, Gravamen, the Mere — I never knew where anything was. And "forty years ago: the seam / thirty years ago: the parcel / fifteen years ago: Ash" on one page would delete all the mid-scene subtraction.
8. **Break the mega-sentences, starting with chapters 6 and 11.** House rule: two dashes AND a semicolon in one sentence means split it. The senior grey's alibi should be three short sentences — or honestly, cut him entirely; he was never a suspect I cared about, and losing him loses nothing.
9. **Vary the detective work.** Three suspects cleared by documents is two too many. Wick clearing himself by opening his own books is good CHARACTER, so keep that one; give the others scenes, not files.
10. **Ration the catchphrases.** "The ledger of my chest" and "I filed it" get maybe three uses each for the whole book. Same for "the stairs up are longer than the fall down." A motif is a spice, and this book uses spice like it's soup.

---

**Would I recommend it?** To exactly one friend — the one who reads above grade level and loved the sad parts of Nevermoor more than the fun parts. To everyone else, not as it is. The kettle, the fog taking Fenn, and "Come home" are legitimately great, and that's what makes the 6/10 frustrating instead of boring: this isn't a bad book, it's a great book locked inside a filing cabinet, and somebody needs to unpick it.
