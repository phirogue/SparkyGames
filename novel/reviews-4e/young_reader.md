# Review by a Young Reader (age 12, reads a LOT)

I'm 12, almost 13. I've read all of Percy Jackson, Wings of Fire, Warriors, Nevermoor, and Amari, and I'm starting to read older stuff. I read this whole book about a cat detective in a foggy city, and here's my honest review.

**Verdict:** I actually loved this book, and I'm kind of mad about it, because parts of it made me feel like I was reading a homework packet about banking. The beginning grabbed me hard (the witch dies in the FIRST chapter and the cat takes the screaming kettle off the fire himself — three tries — I had to put the book down for a second). The ending made me cry, which almost never happens. But the middle has whole chapters where cats and ghosts talk about debts, contracts, escrow, liens, and "dockets" for pages and pages, and I had to push through. If you're a strong reader who liked the sad-clever parts of Nevermoor, read it. If you mostly read for battles, you'll quit around chapter 5.

**Rating: 8/10.** Would've been 9.5 with less paperwork and shorter sentences.

---

## The Good

**The prologue is one of the best openings I've ever read.** Ash is happy, hunting a vole as a present, and the narrator basically tells you "this is the last hour of my life in which the world is simple" — and then the lamps are lit in the wrong order, the pigeons are silent, and his witch's window is dark. The wrongness builds up piece by piece and it's SO creepy. And then the kettle. "The kettle was still screaming. It had been screaming this whole time, and I did not hear it until now. She hates it when it does that." That line destroyed me. The kettle keeps coming back the whole book (the guild hall's cold kettle, the ending where "it has not screamed once since the night it screamed") and every single time it hit.

**Ash's voice.** He's funny in a dry way — "She will say 'not on the table,' and mean *thank you*" — and he never gets mushy even when things are horribly sad, which somehow makes it sadder. The bit where he says nobody will ever know the first shroud stitch took him three tries, "except that I am writing it here, so now everyone will, and I find I do not mind" — that's the whole character in one sentence.

**Tansy.** Best character. A kitten who sold her PURR to save her brothers, sleeps through her own court hearing, then walks over and announces "This is my counsel. He is the best one." When Ash unpicks the contract and her purr comes home "at volume" and she purrs so hard she falls off the rug — perfect. And at the end she learns to stitch by watching, "mirror of a mirror," run the true way round. That's such a smart payoff.

**The Clerk and the cat-sized chair.** The death-office stuff could have been boring but the Clerk is great. The chair just appears at the fifth or sixth visit and "neither of us has ever mentioned it." The stamp is a paw print and nobody remarks on it. Funny AND sad AND creepy at once.

**The scary parts are actually scary.** The rag-wraiths ("clothes whose owners are missing. Not dead — *missing*"), the empty coats over boots in the Lamplighters' hall, and especially chapter 9 when Fenn walks into the fog with empty eyes and "his lamp went on burning where he left it." And then the fog talks to Ash using the STOLEN VOICES of the missing people, including a child's voice going "And we walk home tomorrow." That scene is the scariest thing I've read all year and I mean that as a compliment.

**The Bodkin twist works.** I guessed it — chapter 7 basically screams "the killer is a cat" (small, low, four-footed, tailed, held its breath) — but the reveal in chapter 11, where Ash is sewing his own shadow and realizes the mirrored stitch comes from watching *from the lap side*, is the good kind of twist: all the clues were there ("You fight like a housecat"), and it made me want to reread. And Bodkin isn't evil, he's OWED, which is way more interesting than a villain.

**The ending.** Bodkin sewing himself into the seam — "the walk-in apologizes for the filing" — and the missing walking home, and the last page where Ash keeps the window like she did, "Come home." I cried. The note from Elspeth in chapter 12 ("The kettle is not your fault. I know you took it off the fire. I know it took three tries.") is the single best moment in the book.

---

## The Bad

**Chapter 10, first half (the Horologists).** This is where I got bored for real. There's a whole page explaining what a "lien" is: "the taking-back declared, itemized, and spent in the open, before witnesses, with the balance struck." I read that three times and I'm still not 100% sure. The golden hour payoff is beautiful (sunset running backward, cats on every roofline), but the setup reads like a math word problem about clock lawyers.

**The vocabulary is brutal.** I'm a good reader and I still tripped over: gravamen (it's a whole DISTRICT named that), counterfoil, escrow, intestate, "residuary legatee," "amicus curiae" (the moth joke is funny but only if you know Latin??), "in situ," consignee, promissory notes. One or two fancy words is fun. This many made me feel like the book wasn't for me.

**The sentences go on forever.** Tons of sentences have three dashes and two semicolons and a parenthesis. Example from chapter 11: the paragraph clearing the senior grey cat of suspicion is ONE giant block about session-rolls and minuted lives, and I honestly had to read it twice to figure out it just means "he has an alibi." A lot of chapter 6 (the Gentleman's speeches) is like this too.

**The fights get skipped.** The book keeps setting up fights and then summarizing them. The hound fight in chapter 3: "I am writing this book, and that hound tells the story differently, and we are both entitled." Funny line! But that's like the fourth fight in a row that happens off-screen. The goose training, the wisp fights, even the THREE rag-wraiths in chapter 6 get about two sentences of actual fighting. The cat has four color-coded powers (red, green, black, and the pale Moonlight) which sounds AWESOME, and we almost never get to see them do cool visible things.

**Since Ash is writing the book, you know he lives.** He literally says "I am writing this book" in the prologue. So when he "dies" I never once believed he was in real danger. The Toll fixes this a little (dying costs him a *memory*, which is genuinely scarier than dying), but the tension in the fights is basically zero.

**The suspects get cleared in kind of the same way three times.** Wick gets cleared by his ledgers (ch 5), Shear gets cleared by a stitching demonstration (ch 11), the senior grey gets cleared by MORE ledgers (ch 11). By the third one I was like, okay, I get it, paperwork is truth in this city. Also the senior grey barely appears on-page before being a suspect, so I never cared whether it was him.

**Timeline soup.** Forty years ago the seam, fifteen years of Ash, a parcel mailed thirty years ago containing a spool ten years older than the mailing, with a note newer than both. I get why it's cool but I had to stop and do subtraction in the middle of an emotional scene.

---

## The Ugly

**The courtroom chapter (11) almost lost me.** I know, I know, the whole point is "paperwork is an appointment," and the Shear breakdown scene is honestly great. But it opens with "parties, testimony, exhibits, ruling," and then delivers exactly that, including a ruling written in actual legal language ("is not the estate's property but its residuary legatee"). I'm 12. I skimmed the ruling. If your big triumphant chapter is skimmable, that's a problem. The Gentleman's exhibit-dump paragraph in the middle (the brokerage-trail, the cart counterfoil, the "street-thread, harvested, spooled") crams like four major reveals into one paragraph so dense I missed the cart answer on first read and had to go back.

**The climax is a conversation.** Chapter 12: Ash walks out on the flats, SITS DOWN in front of the monster... and talks. And it works, emotionally — "and put the kettle on," I said. "For two." is an all-timer line — but the lake, the actual giant ancient evil, gets defeated by a foreclosure filing. "Found in contempt in absentia, at the bottom of itself" is a hilarious line for adults, but as the final defeat of the scary thing the whole book built up? I wanted ONE moment where I was scared Ash wouldn't make it. The flats rising "like the lid of an eye" was awesome and lasted about a paragraph.

**We don't get to read the confession.** Chapter 11: "I am not going to transcribe her confession." WHAT. The book spends ten chapters building to Box 9-Crown and then Ash tells us he's keeping it private except for quotes. I get that it's in-character, but as a reader I felt cheated of the thing I was promised.

**Almost no kids and almost no humans that matter.** The Fish-Giver is sweet, Merrow is cool, but every human is an adult with a job and a grievance. Tansy is the only young character, and she vanishes for most of chapters 5–9. My friends would ask "who's the kid character?" and I'd have to say "an orange kitten who's in like six scenes."

---

## Specific Recommendations

1. **Cut the lien-law lecture in chapter 10 by half.** Keep the golden hour, keep the auditors falling in love with the paperwork, but explain the trick in two sentences a kid can repeat: "They weren't stealing time. The city owed them a sunrise, and they had the receipt."
2. **Show one full fight, start to finish, with the four strengths used visibly.** The wax keeper fight in chapter 5 (using the candle drafts to melt it) is the model — it's clever and you can SEE it. Do that for the three rag-wraiths in chapter 6 instead of "the drover's coat came down on me like weather off a hill," done.
3. **Give Tansy scenes in chapters 5–9.** Even one page per chapter. She could be the person Ash explains things to, which also fixes the vocabulary problem — when the Court says "residuary legatee," let Tansy ask "what's that" so Ash has to say it in plain words.
4. **Break up the mega-sentences in chapters 6 and 11.** Rule of thumb: if a sentence has two dashes AND a semicolon, split it. The senior-grey-alibi paragraph in chapter 11 should be three short sentences, or just cut — he was never a real suspect to me anyway.
5. **Let us read more of the confession in chapter 11.** Even half a page of Elspeth's actual voice from the box. Her note in chapter 12 proves her voice on the page is the most powerful thing in the book — the confession scene deserves it too, and right now it's summarized.
6. **Add real danger to chapter 12 before the settlement.** Let the Drowned actually reach Ash, let him spend a life or nearly, THEN let the talking win. The talk is the right ending — it just needs the reader's heart rate up first.
7. **Put a short timeline (or a map of Hollowmere) in the front.** Forty years/thirty years/fifteen years/the seam/the bell — one little "the story so far in years" page would save so much mid-scene math. A map would also help; I never knew where Thimblefield was compared to the Shambles, Wickrow, Gravamen, and the Mere.
8. **Bring the Ratsmeet and the Grey Boss back at the end.** "I remember the ninth bell" is such a great line in chapter 4 — the Grey Boss should get one scene after the bell rings again. Right now the rats just disappear from the book.
9. **Vary how suspects get cleared.** Wick's clearing is great (he clears himself by opening his own books, which shows character). Give the senior grey a face-to-face scene instead of a ledger, or cut him entirely and let Ash's fear be about Bodkin sooner.
10. **Ease up on "the ledger of my chest" and "I filed it."** It's a good motif the first five times. By chapter 9 I was counting them instead of feeling them.

---

**Would I recommend it to my friends?** To my two friends who read everything: absolutely, and I'd tell them to push through chapter 10 because the ending pays off everything. To everyone else: probably not until it gets a little easier to read. But the cat, the kettle, and "Come home" are going to stay with me for a long time.
