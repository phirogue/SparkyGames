# Review: *The Nine Lives of Ashcat* (manuscript)
**Reviewer persona:** Devoted mystery/crime reader — 60+ mysteries a year, from Christie and Chandler to Tana French and Horowitz. I judge a book on the fairness of its puzzle, the placement of its clues, and whether the reveal earns the pages that led to it.

**Overall verdict: 8/10.** This is, underneath the fur and the fog, a genuine fair-play detective novel — and a surprisingly rigorous one. The clueing is honest to a degree most straight crime novels don't attempt: the murder method is planted in chapter 2, demonstrated on the page, and the reveal reframes it rather than inventing it. The narrator is a Watson and a suspect-adjacent figure at once, and the book plays the Ackroyd game ("one of them is that I tell it in the order I lived it") without ever actually cheating. Where it loses points: the killer is guessable very early — arguably named in chapter 3 and confirmed by chapter 8 — while the text continues to treat the chapter 11 reveal as a thunderclap; the middle is episodic in ways that stall the murder plot; one red herring is cleared by offstage paperwork; and the ending refunds almost every cost the middle so carefully charged. Still: I finished it in two sittings, and I annotated the margins the way I only do when a book is playing fair enough to argue with.

---

## The Good

**The method clue is one of the best-planted I've read in years.** In chapter 2, mending the parlor ward, Ash notes: "my paws wanted to run the line mirror-wise, right to left... I remember being irritated at my own hands and thinking nothing of it. *Nothing in this book costs me more*." That is a perfect fair-play plant — visible, flagged, and misreadable. When chapter 11 pays it off ("a familiar's paws do not learn the stitch from the needle's side. They learn it from the lap"), the entire mystery — why the killer's technique is Elspeth's stitch *mirrored*, why "Elspeth taught exactly nobody" doesn't matter — resolves through a mechanism the reader has physically watched the detective perform three times (the door ward, the warehouse re-sew, the shadow pockets). The murderer's signature *is* the detective's signature. That's Christie-grade construction.

**The evidence chain is real detection, not vibes.** The candle stub with the bee stamp (ch. 1), the cart riding "heavy toward the water and light away from it" (ch. 2), the wisps taught a route ("appetite does not read route-maps," ch. 3 — a genuinely elegant deduction), the seed "hauled *up*" to the rooftops ("Nobody hauls seed up. Seed falls; that is the entire economic principle of being a pigeon," ch. 4), the unpaid ward-shifts in Merrow's shift-book. Each chapter converts one anomaly into one inference, and the inferences compound. The reconstruction of the gargoyle's blink in chapter 7 — rain barrel, sleeping dog's dream, frost-ferns on the assay glass, converging on "small; low; four-footed; tailed; breath held for the crossing" — is the best sequence in the book: three witnesses without eyelids, forensically read, and the conclusion is left hanging in the silence where Sootbeard, correct to the last grain of him, refuses to state it.

**The suspect eliminations are structurally sound.** Wick is cleared in chapter 5 by motive logic that actually holds ("You do not kill the one needle holding your roof shut... Alive and ruined, she re-wards my wall from whatever gutter I chase her to"), and the scene where he lays his own true books on the desk is the best human scene in the novel — "a coward about everything except arithmetic" is a whole character in seven words. Shear's elimination in chapter 11 is even better: cleared not by alibi but by *demonstrated incapacity*, on the stand, needle in hand — "she did not make this. She merely wanted it. Wanting is not yet a filing category." Elimination-by-craft is rarer and more satisfying than elimination-by-timetable.

**The reversal of the first clue.** The beeswax stub is read in chapter 1 as an intruder's arrogance — someone who "waited here, in the dark, comfortable, unhurried." Chapter 12's dream reveals Elspeth set out that candle herself, "unlit, where a guest would see it": the good light you keep against a guest you owe. The book's very first exhibit was true evidence, honestly reported, and *wrongly read* — the correction comes via a forty-year-old receipt in the escrow box. That's the kind of thing I read this genre for.

**Chapter-end hooks are consistently strong.** "Then that is where we are going" (prologue, and echoed at ch. 9); "Find the teacher" (ch. 3); "It had blinked" (ch. 6); "I went to spend some lives" (ch. 8). The prologue itself is a superb cold open — a murder night narrated by the one witness nobody will interview, with the wrongness accumulating in a professional's ledger ("The ledger of things wrong tonight was becoming a page with writing on both sides").

**The cost mechanics create real stakes.** The Toll that takes a specific memory — "The case was all still there. The warm was not" (ch. 10) — is a better price than hit points, and the book honors it: the loss recurs, and the epilogue explicitly does not refund it. Fenn walking into the fog while Ash screams under his boots (ch. 9) is the darkest scene in the manuscript and earns the refusal that follows.

**The fog's temptation scene (ch. 9)** is a first-rate villain move: the Mere offers Ash *nothing for himself* — only every missing name walking home — "It had priced me correctly." And Ash's counter is detection, not willpower: he *audits the voices* and finds the one voice that cannot be in the inventory, because "Dead is not held; spent is spent." The detective defeats the temptation by applying the case's own rules of evidence. Lovely.

## The Bad

**The killer's name is a flashing arrow.** Chapter 3, one paragraph after Bodkin introduces himself: "A bodkin, she told me once... is the blunt needle. The one you use to unpick." The murder scene we've already witnessed is an *unpicking*. I wrote "the killer" in the margin at that line, on page one of his existence, and I was never given a serious reason to erase it. Fair play should whisper; this gloss shouts. Everything else about Bodkin's concealment is beautifully done — "I don't go in there" (ch. 5) reading as an old cat's honest fear when it's actually the perpetrator declining to enter his own crime-in-progress; the vole on the sill (ch. 10) described in the killer's fingerprint vocabulary, "clean, and old-fashioned, and close work"; the "four small drumbeats... going away" at the moment of Ash's drowning (ch. 9). The craft deserved a name that didn't confess.

**The solution arrives for the reader three chapters before it arrives for the narrator.** By the end of chapter 8's dream — eight lives drawn out of a grey familiar who "did not run," the familiar "missing from every record," the technique mirror-handed and forty years old — the puzzle is functionally closed. Chapters 9 through 11 are confirmation, and good confirmation, but chapter 11 stages the epiphany ("the whole book I have been writing you turned over at once") with a surprise-voltage the reader spent long ago. The narrator even tells us he's refusing to write the name (ch. 8: "Some entries, once made, cannot be unpicked") — which is honest and characterful, but it converts the last third from *whodunit* to *when-will-he-admit-it*, and the book never quite acknowledges that trade.

**The senior grey of the Court of Whiskers is a cardboard herring.** He's introduced in one sentence (ch. 7), never given a scene, never speaks, and is cleared in chapter 11 by a sealed ledger of session-rolls — elimination by paperwork we never see, of a character we never met. In a book this good at making its suspects *perform* their innocence (Wick's books, Shear's needle), the one pure decoy is dispatched like an accounting entry. He fooled nobody and cost nothing.

**Cardew's "twice before" speech is delivered twice.** At the parlor door in chapter 1 ("It has happened twice before. Both times a stitcher. Both times nobody looked. I am too old and too watched. You are neither") and then again, nearly verbatim with additions, on the quay walk in the same chapter. It reads as an editing seam, not a deliberate echo, and it blunts the quay version's new cargo (the Circuit closing the files; "You were her ninth").

**The inciting thread is metaphysically fuzzy.** The one uncut strand running "out from under the window... into the city" (ch. 1) is the engine of chapter 2 — but the book never explains what it actually connects to. Ash's tether ran to Elspeth; Elspeth is dead in the chair behind him; so what is the strand attached to, and why does it lead toward the Mere until someone cuts it "one clean bite" over open water? The implied answer — the killer walked off *wearing* her threads, and a live strand trailed him — is reconstructable, but I had to reconstruct it, and the trail that launches the whole investigation shouldn't require fan theory.

**Small ledger items never ticked off.** Who drove the cart (the Grey Boss is emphatic: "this driver we did not know and have not seen since")? Who scratched out the consignee line, and why be ashamed of a delivery commissioned blind? Who was the indebted day-sider who hauled the seed? Chapter 12's settlement scene explicitly promises completeness — "the account is stated entire, aloud" — and the account is stated almost entire. In a book that fetishizes reconciled books, three unreconciled lines itch.

## The Ugly

**The ending refunds too much.** This is my one real grievance. The middle of this book charges brutal, specific prices: a hall of empty coats over waiting boots, a child's sunday jacket "I will not describe further," Fenn folded away standing while his lamp burns. Then the epilogue returns *everyone*. The lamplighters walk home; the child of the sunday jacket walks home; the roosts refill; even the Drowned politely leave. The single unclaimed coat is "a fisherman's oilskin... older than living memory" — an anonymous loss, a stranger's, and therefore nearly free. Yes, the book keeps some debts unpaid (the Toll-taken memory, the blank page, Bodkin himself), and yes, "held is not spent" (ch. 8) was planted precisely to license the restoration — it's *fair*. But fair is not the same as costly. When every named victim of the villain's forty-year campaign is alive at the curtain, the campaign retroactively loses weight, and the flats scene I read with my heart in my throat becomes, in hindsight, a scare with a full refund. Tana French would have kept Fenn.

**Bodkin's strategic incoherence is gestured at, not resolved.** The murderer spends forty years on a plan of surgical patience — then personally saves the investigator's life (ch. 3), teaches him combat doctrine ("Watch the paws, not the claws"), *hands him the location of the counting room* that breaks the case open, and secretly mends his thread "eleven times between audits." The emotional reading — he loved her ninth stray and could not unpick him; some part of him wanted to be found and settled — is present ("You were the only thing in that house I could not unpick," ch. 12), and it's moving. But it is never *owned*. A creature this precise, who "did not spend four seconds looking" at the one moment looking would have stopped him, actively arms his own defeater for an entire season, and the settlement scene lets him off without stating why. One line — Bodkin admitting the sabotage was his, and deliberate, or that he needed a witness capable of stating the account — would convert the book's largest implausibility into its deepest character beat. As written, it's a hole wearing a heart.

**The prose never once stops performing.** I want to be careful here, because the voice is the book's fortune and most of the epigrams cash out ("patient as rent" is doing real work; "a coward about everything except arithmetic" is characterization). But thirteen chapters in which *every* witness is eloquent, *every* institution is a wry marvel (the Ratsmeet, the pigeon election, the moth entered as *amicus curiae*, the Court's terrible tea), and every third sentence lands a maxim — it's a rich meal with no bread. By chapter 10 (the Horologists), when the narration pauses to teach me lien law in plain words "so that the reader need never practice it," I felt the wit as a tax. Readers who bounce off this book will bounce here, not off the plot.

## Specific Recommendations

1. **Cut the bodkin-definition gloss from chapter 3** ("A bodkin, she told me once, is the blunt needle. The one you use to unpick") and move that etymology to the chapter 11 reveal, where it would detonate instead of forewarn. Let the name pass as texture on first meeting; genre readers treat a glossed name as a signed confession.

2. **Give the senior grey one real scene between chapters 7 and 9** — seen near the Mereside at a wrong hour, evasive when the Court of Whiskers serves papers, *something* performed on the page — so his chapter 11 clearing-by-ledger reads as an elimination the reader minded, not a footnote. Right now the suspect board has three working doors and one painted on.

3. **Close the strand's metaphysics in chapter 2 with one clause**: state that the surviving strand ran to the stolen wards the killer walked out wearing — which is why it leads toward the Mere and why he, noticing the tail, "turned and cut it one clean stroke." The inciting trail should not be the book's least-explained object.

4. **De-duplicate Cardew's "twice before" speech in chapter 1.** Keep only "Again" at the parlor door; deliver the full speech once, on the quay, where its new information (the closed files, "You were her ninth") can land clean.

5. **Make Bodkin own the self-sabotage in chapter 12.** After "You were the only thing in that house I could not unpick," add one exchange in which Ash names the contradiction — the counting-room tip, the eleven mendings — and Bodkin admits what they were: a creditor keeping his own witness alive, or a debtor hoping to be collected. The book's biggest logic strain becomes its best line.

6. **Let one named loss stand in the epilogue.** The cleanest candidate: Fenn does not walk home — or walks home wrong, his five borrowed minutes permanently short. The restoration of the city will survive one permanent grief; the flats scene will not survive a full refund. Alternatively, attach a name and a mourner to the fisherman's oilskin so the one unclaimed coat costs somebody something on the page.

7. **Trim chapter 10's Horologists sequence by a quarter and cut the lien-law paragraph** ("Here is the whole of the law of it in plain words, once..."). The golden hour is load-bearing — the precedent is the hinge of the finale — but the reader can be trusted to extract "declared, itemized, spent in the open" from the auditors' scene itself, which dramatizes it better than the lecture does.

8. **Tick off the last three ledger lines in the settlement scene (ch. 12).** Ash's stated account is the structurally perfect place to resolve the cart driver, the scratched consignee, and the seed-hauler in a sentence apiece — Bodkin can confirm with his "Put it all on the bill." The book's whole ethic is reconciled accounts; reconcile them.

9. **Either move the reveal earlier or let the narrator concede the reader's solve.** The narrator who says "I tell it in the order I lived it" and shows us his refusals ("I did not write the name," ch. 8) could, in chapter 11, grant the reader one honest sentence — that the reader has likely been waiting chapters for him to turn around — converting the surprise-gap into complicity, Ackroyd-style. As staged, the book is more surprised than its audience, which is the one posture a mystery can't afford.

10. **Give the second murdered stitcher a name and one human detail.** The Tatterstage lead gets a magnificent chapter; the other of Cardew's "twice before" remains a filing category from chapter 1 to the epilogue (where her file is reopened, still nameless). One paragraph — a trade, a district, one mourner — would make the forty-year pattern felt as three deaths rather than one death and two citations.

11. **Join the candle stub's two readings explicitly.** Chapter 1 establishes it "burned an inch down and pinched out"; chapter 12 reveals Elspeth set it out for her guest. Nobody ever pinches it out on the page. One clause in the settlement — Bodkin extinguishing her hospitality before beginning the work, or after — would close the book's first exhibit as cleanly as it was opened.

12. **Consider thinning the institutional whimsy in exactly one chapter — chapter 4 is the candidate.** The Ratsmeet and the pigeon election earn their pages (both produce hard clues). The Fish-Giver episode produces only theme; it's charming, and "they were going to *file* him out of it" ties to Wick's methods, but it's the one section a murder-focused reader skims. Either trim it by half or give it one evidentiary payoff (the defective lease as Ash's first taste of "paperwork is an appointment," explicitly recalled in chapter 9).

---

**Bottom line:** A fantasy whodunit that respects the compact better than most contemporary crime novels: every load-bearing fact is on the page before it's needed, the method reveal is planted in the reader's own hands, and the eliminations are earned in scene. Its sins are generosity — a name that confesses, a solution legible three chapters early, an ending that gives nearly everything back. All of them are fixable with a scalpel rather than a saw. I'd hand it to a mystery-reading friend today, with the warning that they'll solve it at the two-thirds mark — and the promise that, unusually, they won't mind staying for the settlement.
