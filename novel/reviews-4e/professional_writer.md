# Manuscript Review — *The Nine Lives of Ashcat* (working title assumed)

**Reviewer persona:** Professional novelist and developmental editor; twenty years in trade publishing, literary and genre lists, MFA workshop veteran, several hundred manuscripts edited. This is the candid internal reader's report.

---

## Verdict

**6/10 — reject with notes.** This manuscript has an exceptional narrative voice and one genuinely brilliant clue mechanism, and I want to be clear-eyed about why that is not enough. It is a murder mystery in which the mystery generates no doubt; a nine-lives conceit in which five of the deaths are spent off the page in a subordinate clause; a three-act structure whose middle act idles on episodic side-cases after its only deadline quietly dies in chapter 5; and a climax whose emotional transaction — the murderer forgiven, valorized, and given a hero's death — is never paid for on camera by the one character whose forgiveness would mean anything. The prose, meanwhile, is a monoculture of epigram that treats every paragraph as a curtain line, which is dazzling for thirty pages and numbing for three hundred. Against its natural comps — Terry Pratchett's Watch novels, Robert Jackson Bennett's *The Tainted Cup*, Frances Hardinge — it loses on exactly the axes those books win: propulsion, jeopardy, and the discipline to let plain sentences carry weight. What's here is a beautifully upholstered fix-up of novelettes wearing a novel's coat. The talent is obvious. The book, as structured, is not acquirable, and the fixes are not polish — they are load-bearing reconstruction of the middle third and the ending's moral economy.

---

## The Good

An exacting report still concedes the following, with qualifications attached.

**The voice, in bounded doses, is the real thing.** The prologue is close to flawless: fog "patient as rent," the city "buttoning its coat," and the thesis line — "I do not know how to be a cat that nobody is expecting" — answered by the epilogue's "Now I am the one expecting… Come home." The frame closes cleanly. The qualification: this voice is the book's single asset, and the manuscript leans on it to excuse structural debts it never repays. Voice sells thirty sample pages; it does not sell chapter 8.

**The mirror-stitch clue is genuinely first-rate.** Chapter 2 plants it — Ash's paws "wanted to run the line mirror-wise… and I remember being irritated at my own hands and thinking nothing of it" — and chapter 11 detonates it: a familiar learns the stitch mirrored because he learns it from the lap, watching the needle come toward him. Forensics converted into a statement about love. If the rest of the mystery were built to this standard, this would be a different report.

**Three set pieces would survive any cut.** The shroud-sewing in chapter 1 (Cardew's "Sixty years, and I don't know her hand," the cat finishing the seam); the three eyeless witnesses of chapter 7 (rain barrel, dreaming mastiff, frost-pane); and chapter 9 entire — the flats, the fog spending the voices of the taken, Fenn's lamp burning on the mud. Chapter 9 is the best chapter because it is the only one after the prologue in which the protagonist decisively loses.

**One cost is real.** The Toll taking a Remembered Day, and chapter 10's accounting of it — "The case was all still there. The warm was not." — is the honest version of what the whole book should be doing with its death economy. That the epilogue refuses to restore the memory is the manuscript's most disciplined decision.

**The kettle motif is handled correctly.** Screaming in the prologue, three tries to lift it, "the kettle is not your fault" in her note, "One paw. One try, these days" in the epilogue. Sparing, escalated, cashed. It proves the author *can* do restraint — which makes its absence elsewhere a choice, and a chargeable one.

---

## The Bad

**The middle third is a stalled engine, and the manuscript knows it.** The only live deadline — Wick calling Elspeth's debts due on Lady's Day, set with real menace in chapter 3 — dies the moment Wick is cleared in chapter 5, and nothing replaces it until the seam starts visibly failing in chapter 12. In between, chapters 4, 8, and 10 are built from side-cases: the pigeon election, Tansy's purr, the Fish-Giver's lease, the caulker's shadow, the Horologists' golden hour. Each "rhymes" with the debt theme; none advances the murder. Thematic rhyme is not propulsion, and an acquiring editor reading chapter 8 cold will not know, and will not be able to reconstruct, what question the protagonist is currently trying to answer. That is a fatal condition in a mystery, not a note.

**The suspect structure never once creates doubt.** Wick is introduced and exonerated inside chapter 5, by his own confession. The Gentleman is dismissed by the narrator's own logic in chapter 6 ("a being made entirely of open accounts does not spend the one coin that closes them"). Shear is incriminated in chapter 8 and folds without resistance at the chapter 11 hearing. The fourth candidate, the senior grey of the Court of Whiskers, is a red herring made of paperwork — suspected in two paragraphs, cleared by an offstage ledger, never given a scene. Meanwhile the actual killer is flagged by chapter 3 at the latest: a one-eyed grey tom whose name is glossed on the page as "the blunt needle. The one you use to unpick." The reader spends six chapters ahead of the narrator while the text stages itself as if the answer were open. A mystery that is neither a surprise (the reader knows) nor a committed dramatic irony (the text won't admit the reader knows) is a mystery that fails both ways.

**Five of nine deaths are spent in a subordinate clause.** Chapter 7: "I have stood in the Clerk's corridor six times this season — once on the docket, five times REFUSED." The reader has seen two. The nine-lives economy is the book's marquee conceit and its best set — the cat-sized chair, the pen that sounds like winter — and the manuscript burns five visits as a retroactive tally. This is simultaneously a trust break, a waste of the strongest material, and proof that the middle chapters had no room for the A-plot's own currency because they were busy with lease disputes.

**Scene-versus-summary imbalance runs chapter by chapter, always in the wrong direction.** The book dramatizes its digressions and summarizes its turns. Chapter 4's Fish-Giver rescue: all summary. Chapter 5's public ruin of Wick — the biggest civic reversal in the novel — is one sentence of report ("Merrow read the ward-shift page aloud from the hall steps, twice"). Chapter 8's caulker case: narrated at arm's length. Worst is chapter 11, where a single unbroken paragraph clears the Gentleman, reveals the cart's cargo ("street-thread, harvested, spooled"), and identifies the unbookable walker — three chapter-old questions resolved in one fire-hose block of exposition folded on a bench. When your climactic hearing resolves its evidence in a paragraph of summary and your lease dispute gets a full scene, the architecture is inverted.

**The prose is an epigram monoculture.** "Quiet is against their religion." "A rat's silence is a price list." "A cat is sincerity armed." "Paperwork is an appointment." Every one of these is good; the problem is that there is one on every page, in the same syntactic mold — concrete image, dash or colon, aphoristic landing — until emphasis stops registering. The em-dash appositive cascade is the default sentence shape of chapters 6 and 10; some paragraphs carry three parenthetical insertions and a hundred and fifty words before the verb resolves. Dialogue has the same disease: Ash, Brindle, the Clerk, the Gentleman, Bodkin, and even the fog all speak in the same polished, load-bearing epigram. Exchanges read as duels of curtain lines, not people talking — compare Wick's confession in chapter 5, the one scene where a character is allowed to sound unlike the narrator, and notice how much air comes into the book.

**Secondary characters are functions in costume.** Brindle is The Fence, Merrow is The Grieving Foreman, Sootbeard is The Duty Joke, the Clerk is The Bureaucrat Softening, Tansy is The Heir Prefigured. Each is executed with charm; none has a want that conflicts with the protagonist's, which is why every scene with them resolves in Ash's favor and usually in his praise. The only characters with agendas that push back — Wick, Shear, Bodkin — produce the book's three best dialogue scenes, and two of the three are dispatched within a chapter of arriving. The cast is a receiving line.

**The retrospective frame bleeds somatic tension.** The narrator repeatedly time-stamps his own survival and curates suspense by announcement: "Nothing in this book costs me more" (ch. 2), "I have promised to tell this in the order I lived it" (ch. 5), "what came later has made the memory expensive" (prologue). By chapter 6, the reader has internalized that every fight is survivable, every defeat refundable, and every revelation pre-chewed. The one countervailing device — the Toll — is used to full effect exactly once (ch. 9–10). A death economy this forgiving needs its costs foregrounded every time; instead the frame keeps reassuring us the accountant survived to write the book.

## The Ugly

These are the problems that sink the manuscript with an acquiring editor, not the ones a line edit reaches.

**1. The climax's mercy is unearned sentiment, and the book's moral ledger doesn't balance.** The last act reveals the victim spent eight of her familiar's lives without consent, recasts her as the true debtor ("HE IS OWED — write it at the head of every page"), and gives her murderer — who also killed the Tatterstage lead and a second stitcher, emptied the Lamplighters' hall, and fed a city's missing to the lake as "down-payments" — a valorized, elegiac hero's death. Ash's on-page rage is directed at *Elspeth* ("I was at the doorpost with my claws, and it was not sharpening," ch. 11). At Bodkin he directs nothing, ever. Between writing the name at the head of the page and sitting down peaceably in front of the killer in chapter 12, the entire movement from grief to advocacy happens off the page. Forgiveness chosen over a live, dramatized alternative is a climax; forgiveness that costs nothing on camera is authorial fiat wearing a mourning band. The blank-page device (ch. 11, "the woman at the window and the woman on the water") is honest about Elspeth — and its very honesty exposes that no equivalent reckoning exists for Bodkin. Reviewers will say the book lets the murderer off because he was sad, and on the page as written, they will be right.

**2. The mystery fails as suspense and refuses to be irony.** Any attentive reader has the killer by chapter 3 (the name glossed as "the blunt needle… you use to unpick"), confirmed by chapter 5 ("I don't go in there"; fog holding "the shape of a sitting cat"), sealed by chapter 7 (small, low, four-footed, tailed). The narrator half-admits foreknowledge ("What I see now — but this account has rules") yet stages chapters 8–11 as open investigation, complete with a suspect board and a paperwork red herring. Six chapters of a narrator refusing an answer the reader holds is only tolerable if the text commits to that refusal *as the drama*. It doesn't; it keeps performing detection. The result is the worst of both engines, and it is the structural reason the middle feels slack no matter how good the sentences are.

**3. Chapter 12 runs two incompatible clocks.** "The seam… was down to its last threads at the anchor; the fog stood in the streets at noon" — and in the same chapter, the bell's redemption is a leisurely four-day audit played for institutional comedy ("exceptional bookkeeping"). Either the collapse is hours away and the montage is absurd, or it is weeks away and the sprint to the flats is unearned. The reconciling device (the anchor only reachable at the neap) is sitting in the text unused. Worse, the bell's climactic function — a scheduled ninth stroke makes Bodkin's death an appointment, hence a true death, hence a valid anchor-price — must be assembled by the reader from two half-lines a hundred pages apart (ch. 9's "the hours kept the appointments"; the note's "nothing anchors without a measure"). Load-bearing mechanics on that little scaffolding is a crash waiting for a reviewer.

**4. The market problem is real and unaddressed.** Too adult in grief and length for middle grade; too whimsical, talking-animal, and low-jeopardy through the middle for the adult crime-fantasy shelf where *The Tainted Cup* lives; too slow for the cozy-fantasy impulse buyer, who will not cross chapter 8. Pratchett solved this with relentless plot momentum under the wit; Hardinge with escalating danger. This manuscript, at what reads as 110–120k words of dense, self-savoring prose, offers an editor no clean answer to "who is the reader, and what keeps them past page 150?" — and in the current market that question is the acquisition meeting.

---

## Conditions for Publishability

Not suggestions — conditions. Prioritized. The first five are the difference between reject and revise-and-resubmit.

1. **Rebuild the middle third around a live clock.** Chapters 4–10 must run against a visible, escalating countdown — the seam's decay made legible one concrete symptom per chapter (a ward failing, the fog holding an hour longer, the Drowned one terrace higher), each logged in Ash's ledger. Either cash the Lady's Day deadline (ch. 3) into the back half or cut the date; a deadline set with menace and silently dropped in chapter 5 is a broken promise the reader files against the author.

2. **Commit to the dramatic-irony engine and retool chapters 6–11 accordingly.** Cut the pretense of open detection: eliminate or fully dramatize the senior-grey red herring (currently two paragraphs and an offstage ledger, ch. 7/9/11), and rebuild the middle's tension as the reader's certainty grinding against Ash's refusal to see — the register of "I had been *afraid* of where it went" (ch. 6). Every beat that pretends the answer might be a rival witch must go or be re-aimed.

3. **Put the missing deaths on the page.** The "six times this season" tally (ch. 7) is unacceptable as written. Dramatize at least two of the five elided refusals in chapters 4–8 — one for comedy (the cat-sized chair must debut in scene, not parenthesis), one at real cost, with the Toll's threat escalating each visit. The nine-lives economy is the marquee conceit; spend it where the reader can watch.

4. **Earn the mercy.** Insert a scene between the chapter 11 revelation and the chapter 12 settlement in which Ash wants Bodkin dead — physically, on camera, at real risk of acting on it — and chooses the account-stating instead. Without a live alternative, the climax's forgiveness is sentiment; with it, the settlement scene's existing dialogue ("You'll take tea, then") becomes devastating instead of convenient.

5. **Reconcile chapter 12's clocks and shore up the bell mechanics.** State on the page that the anchor is workable only at the neap, so the four-day audit races a tide-table rather than ignoring a collapse. Expand the appointment-mechanics plant in the chapter 9 vault scene from an aside to a beat Ash actually chews on; the climax's entire causal chain currently hangs on two half-lines.

6. **Break the chapter 11 evidence-dump paragraph into a staged scene.** The Gentleman's exhibit resolves three long-held questions (his innocence, the cart's cargo, the unbookable walker) in one block of summary. Three beats, three reveals, each allowed to land — this is the hearing's evidentiary climax and is currently disposed of like housekeeping.

7. **Dramatize the Wick fallout; compress the second wax-golem fight (ch. 5).** Merrow reading the ward-shift page to a silencing street is the novel's biggest public turn and gets one sentence; the Guildhall keeper is the second wax-construct set piece in three chapters and gets a page. Swap the weights.

8. **Cut or fold one full episode from chapter 4 and tighten chapter 8.** The Fish-Giver rescue duplicates the chapter's thematic work twice over; fold it into chapter 8 or reduce it to a paragraph. Seed Tansy earlier so her adoption is not a mid-book swerve. The caulker's shadow (ch. 8) earns its place only if its "held is not spent" finding is made to *do* plot work sooner than chapter 12.

9. **Run an epigram cull of 20% minimum, chapters 4–10 hardest.** One aphorism per page survives; the rest convert to plain narration. Break the em-dash appositive default; let paragraphs end on ordinary sentences so the real curtain lines ring. Prologue and chapters 9, 11, 12 need the lightest touch.

10. **Differentiate the dialogue.** Every speaker currently competes in the narrator's idiom. Give Merrow shift-floor bluntness without polish, Brindle trade patter with dropped grammar, Tansy actual kitten syntax. Wick's confession (ch. 5) is the existing proof of concept — the one voice that isn't Ash's — and the book needs five more like it.

11. **Ration the refusal-to-narrate tic to two uses.** "I will not describe / I am not going to transcribe / the file may show a redaction" appears six-plus times (prologue, ch. 2, ch. 7, ch. 11 twice, ch. 12). Keep the confession and the redacted gargoyle ride; write through the rest. A narrator who keeps declining to narrate is dodging, and readers feel it.

12. **Give the middle third one unrecoverable loss.** Between chapters 3 and 9 Ash wins every engagement and every institution falls in love with him — goose, hound, keepers, courts, guilds. Move the failed fish-market ward (ch. 10) earlier and attach a visible casualty to it, so competence has a price before the flats.

13. **Let one cleared suspect stay dirty longer.** Wick, the Gentleman, and Shear are each neutralized in their debut chapter. Hold Shear's clearance until the hearing but give her one intervening scene of genuine menace, or let the Gentleman's "bait" (ch. 6) actually cost Ash something on-page instead of being flagged and forgotten until the ch. 11 note.

14. **Cut 15–20k words overall and take a market position.** Target under 105k. Query as adult fantasy-mystery crossover (Pratchett/Bennett shelf), lead with the hook — a murdered witch's cat inherits her unfinished magic and spends his nine lives as case expenses — and make chapters 1, 9, and 12 the proof pages. Do not let the package drift cozy; the middle as written invites it, which is one more reason the middle must change.

The voice and the mirror-stitch reveal are the work of a real writer. The structure is the work of a writer who has not yet been made to choose between what the book enjoys and what the book needs. Until that choice is made — middle rebuilt, deaths spent on camera, mercy earned — this is a pass.
