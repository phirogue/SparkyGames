# Manuscript Review — *The Nine Lives of Ashcat* (working title assumed)

**Reviewer persona:** Professional novelist and developmental editor; twenty years in trade publishing, literary and genre lists, MFA workshop veteran, several hundred manuscripts edited.

---

## Verdict

This is the rare manuscript where the sentence-level voice, the world's internal logic, and the theme are all running on the same engine — here, an economy of debt, thread, and grief — and the final three chapters cash nearly every promissory note the first three write. It is a murder mystery narrated by the victim's cat, told as a retrospective case file, and it is genuinely moving, frequently funny, and structurally ambitious in a way most cozy-adjacent fantasy never attempts. Its problems are real but tractable: a middle third that idles the murder plot while the narrator wins episodic side-cases, a suspect structure that never once puts the reader in genuine doubt, five of the hero's nine deaths spent off the page, and a prose style so relentlessly aphoristic that the reader's ear starts flinching around chapter 6. **8/10** — a strong revise-and-resubmit away from being the kind of book that gets hand-sold for a decade. **Comps:** Terry Pratchett's City Watch novels for the civic-institutional wit; Robert Jackson Bennett's *The Tainted Cup* for the fantasy-procedural spine; Frances Hardinge for the tone of tender menace and the crossover adult/YA-upper shelf where this would sit.

---

## The Good

**The voice is the asset, and it is a real one.** Ash's narration — first-person retrospective, dry, exact, keeping "accounts" — is sustained with remarkable discipline across fourteen chapters. The prologue alone earns the price of admission: fog "patient as rent," the city "buttoning its coat," and then the line that quietly announces the whole book's emotional project: "I do not know how to be a cat that nobody is expecting." When the epilogue answers it — "Now I am the one expecting… Come home." — the frame closes with the click of a well-made box. That is arc engineering of a high order: the want (find the killer) resolves in chapter 12, and the need (learn what the woman at the window was actually doing there, and become it) resolves in the last two pages, in the correct order, without the narrator ever stating it as theme.

**The mystery plays fair, and the reveal mechanism is original.** The plant in chapter 2 is close to perfect: mending the door-ward, Ash notes "my paws wanted to run the line mirror-wise, right to left… and I remember being irritated at my own hands and thinking nothing of it. Nothing in this book costs me more." That is the murder method, the killer's identity, and the narrator's own implication, laid down in one image a hundred pages before anyone can use it. The chapter 11 detonation — a familiar's paws learn the stitch mirrored *because they learn it from the lap, watching the needle come toward them* — is the best clue-logic I've seen in a fantasy manuscript in years, because it converts a forensic detail into a statement about love. "It is not a technique. It is a *life*."

**Individual set pieces are superb.** The shroud-sewing in chapter 1 — Cardew's courage failing four stitches in ("Sixty years, and I don't know her hand"), and the cat climbing onto the bier to finish the seam in the dead woman's own stitch — is the strongest early-book emotional beat, and it doubles as evidence entered at the chapter 11 hearing, which is exactly how scenes should be made to work twice. Chapter 7's three eyeless witnesses (rain barrel, dreaming mastiff, frost-pane) is the most elegant procedural sequence in the book, and the Understudy — a ghost who cannot exit because she remembers her death wrong, freed when Ash stands in the true blocking and lets her line land — is a self-contained short story any magazine would take. Chapter 9's drowning on the flats is the book's best chapter, full stop: the fog paying out the voices of the taken one sentence at a time, the offer priced perfectly ("It did not offer me a single thing for myself"), and Fenn's lamp burning on the mud.

**The costs are real, and the best one is structural.** In a book where the hero has nine lives, jeopardy has to live somewhere other than survival, and the Toll delivers: the Court takes a Remembered Day, and chapter 10 dramatizes the loss with terrible precision — "The case was all still there. The warm was not." My own file says there was a voice; the room the fact was kept in is gone. That the epilogue refuses to restore it ("some seams are finished open") is the most honest decision in the manuscript.

**The climax's dialogue earns its weight.** "You fight like a housecat." / "I was one." / "Were." — planted as alley banter in chapter 3, returned at the anchor of the world in chapter 12 — and then "She said, 'You'll take tea, then'… I did not spend four seconds looking, because if it was welcome, I could not have collected." Followed by Ash's quiet, brutal completion: "—and put the kettle on… For two." The kettle motif (screaming in the prologue, three tries to lift it, "the kettle is not your fault" in her note, "One paw. One try, these days" in the epilogue) is motif discipline done right: said sparingly, escalated each time, cashed at the end.

## The Bad

**The middle third idles the A-plot.** Chapters 4, 8, and 10 are built substantially from episodic side-cases — the Ratsmeet and pigeon election (which do advance the case), but then Tansy's purr, the Fish-Giver's lease, the caulker's shadow, the Horologists' golden hour. Every one of these rhymes thematically (debt, escrow, restitution) and several plant material the finale needs (the golden hour establishes the debt-of-light precedent chapter 12 stands on; the purr case introduces the Gentleman's paper). But *rhyming is not propulsion*. From the end of chapter 5 (Wick cleared) to chapter 9 (the flats), the murder investigation advances mostly by rumination, and the manuscript's one live deadline — Wick calling Elspeth's debts due on Lady's Day, established with real menace in chapter 3 — simply evaporates once Wick is exposed. Nothing replaces it as a clock until the seam begins visibly failing very late. An agent's reader will feel the sag around chapter 8 even if they can't name it.

**The suspect structure never generates doubt.** Wick is developed and cleared inside one chapter (5), by his own excellent confession scene. The Gentleman is flagged by the narrator himself as an "elegant impossibility" the moment he's suspected (chapter 6). Shear is introduced, incriminated, and cleared in a single hearing (chapters 8 and 11) — her folding at the demonstration is a fine scene, but she never had a chance with the reader, because the prologue's antagonist is self-evidently a night-side creature and none of these candidates is one. The fourth name, the senior grey of the Court of Whiskers, is a red herring made entirely of paperwork: suspected in two paragraphs, cleared by a ledger delivered under seal, never given a scene. A mystery needs at least one interval where the reader genuinely believes the wrong thing. This one never provides it; instead it runs on dramatic irony (see The Ugly), and it should commit to that openly.

**Five of nine deaths happen off the page.** Chapter 7 states, in passing: "I have stood in the Clerk's corridor six times this season — once on the docket, five times REFUSED." The reader has seen exactly two of those visits (the parlor; the rag-wraiths in chapter 6). The Clerk's desk is the book's single most striking invention — the cat-sized chair, the pen that sounds like winter, CLERICAL ERROR — and the manuscript spends five visits to it as a retroactive tally. This is a trust break (the narrator has been keeping cards off the table that the conceit promised us) *and* a waste of the best room in the book.

**Key turns are summarized that beg to be scenes.** Wick's public ruin — Merrow reading the ward-shift page from the hall steps "twice, the second time slower, to a street that got very quiet" — is one sentence of report, and it is one of the biggest civic turns in the novel. The Fish-Giver rescue (chapter 4) is entirely narrated summary. Worst: the single enormous paragraph in chapter 11 that clears the Gentleman, reveals the cart's cargo ("street-thread, harvested, spooled"), and identifies the Grey Boss's unbookable gentleman — three major case resolutions delivered as one breathless block of exposition folded on a bench. That paragraph is doing a chapter's work in a fire-hose, and the reveals arrive devalued.

**Aphorism fatigue.** The style's signature move — concrete image, colon or dash, epigram — is glorious in the prologue and wearying by chapter 6, because nearly every paragraph lands one: "Quiet is against their religion." "A rat's silence is a price list." "A cat is sincerity armed." "Paperwork is an appointment." Each is good; the density is the problem — the ear stops registering emphasis when everything is emphasized, and the em-dash-appositive sentence shape becomes a monoculture. Chapters 6 and 10 are the worst offenders. A 15–20% epigram cull would make the survivors ring.

## The Ugly

**1. The moral engineering of the ending is missing one load-bearing beat: Ash's anger at Bodkin.** Consider what the last act actually does: it reveals that the victim spent eight of her familiar's lives without consent, recasts her as the true debtor ("HE IS OWED — write it at the head of every page"), and gives her murderer a valorized, elegiac hero's death sewing himself into the seam. The manuscript *knows* this is dangerous ground — chapter 11's blank ledger page ("I could not write them together — the woman at the window and the woman on the water") is the most honest grief-writing in the book, and the epilogue rules the page "finished open." But notice where Ash's on-page rage goes: at Elspeth ("I was at the doorpost with my claws, and it was not sharpening"). At Bodkin — the creature who murdered her, emptied a hall, and killed at least two other stitchers decades ago — he directs *nothing*. Between writing the name at the head of the page (end of 11) and sitting down peaceably in front of him (12), the transition from grief to advocacy happens entirely off the page. The settlement scene is magnificent, but forgiveness that costs nothing on camera is sentiment, not payoff. One scene — even half a scene — of Ash wanting to open Bodkin's throat, and choosing the account-stating instead, would make chapter 12 twice as strong and armor the book against the charge (which some reviewer will otherwise make) that it lets the murderer off because he was sad.

**2. The book cannot decide whether its reveal is a surprise or an irony, and the indecision costs the middle chapters.** Any attentive reader has the killer by chapter 5 at the latest: chapter 2 files "it travels like a cat" and turns the page; chapter 3 introduces a one-eyed grey tom whose name is glossed, on the page, as "the blunt needle. The one you use to unpick"; chapter 5 gives him "I don't go in there" and fog holding "the shape of a sitting cat"; chapter 7 delivers small-low-four-footed-tailed and the narrator "reaching for" an alternative "the way a falling man reaches for a gutter." The retrospective narrator half-admits he knows ("What I see now — but this account has rules"). Yet the manuscript still stages chapters 8–11 as though the identity is in genuine question — the suspect board, the senior grey, the not-writing of the name — and the reader spends four chapters waiting for the narrator to catch up. This is a solvable problem, but only by commitment: this is a *dramatic-irony* engine, a "when will he let himself see it" book, and the middle should be tuned as such — tightening the screws of the reader's certainty against Ash's refusal, and trimming any beat that pretends the answer might be a rival witch or a court cat. Played as suspense, the middle is slack; played as watching a bereaved creature refuse the truth, it's devastating. Choose.

**3. The climax runs two clocks at incompatible speeds.** Chapter 12 tells us "the seam… was down to its last threads at the anchor; the fog stood in the streets at noon; the Drowned walked the Mereside terraces in numbers the day-side could no longer unsee" — and in the same chapter, the bell's redemption is a leisurely four-day audit played substantially for institutional comedy ("exceptional bookkeeping"). Either the seam is failing by the hour, in which case the paperwork montage reads as absurd (and not intentionally), or the collapse is weeks away, in which case the sprint to the flats is unearned. The neap tide is sitting right there as the reconciling device — the anchor can only be reached, and therefore only be attacked or re-sewn, at the neap — but the text never says so, and one sentence would fix it. Relatedly, the bell's climactic function (a *scheduled* ninth stroke makes Bodkin's death an appointment, hence a true death, hence a valid anchor-price) must be assembled by the reader from Elspeth's one-line note ("Nothing anchors without a measure") and the Clerk's aside ("the hours kept the appointments"). Load-bearing mechanics deserve more than two half-lines a hundred pages apart.

## Specific Recommendations

Prioritized, highest leverage first.

1. **Install a visible clock across chapters 4–10.** The seam's decay is the book's real countdown; make it legible and escalating — one concrete symptom per chapter (a ward failing here, the fog holding an hour longer there, the Drowned seen one terrace higher), each noted in Ash's ledger. The episodic cases can stay if each one visibly costs time the seam doesn't have. Right now the only clock (Lady's Day, chapter 3) dies with Wick's clearance in chapter 5 — either cash Lady's Day (the hearing, the reading, or the collapse landing on it) or cut the date entirely.

2. **Dramatize at least two of the five elided refusals.** The chapter 7 line "six times this season" retro-counts deaths we never saw. Put two on the page in chapters 4–8 — one comic (the cat-sized chair should *appear* in a scene, not a parenthesis), one costly. This fixes the tally trust-break, keeps the book's best set (the Clerk's desk) warm through the middle, and lets the Toll escalate visibly.

3. **Add the missing anger beat at Bodkin.** Between the chapter 11 revelation and the chapter 12 settlement, give Ash one scene (even a page) of wanting vengeance and setting it down deliberately — ideally at the board, or at Number Twelve's fence, somewhere physical. The settlement's mercy must be chosen over a live alternative, on camera, or it reads as authorial fiat.

4. **Break the chapter 11 evidence-dump paragraph into a scene.** The Gentleman's exhibit — the brokerage trail, the "street-thread, harvested, spooled" counterfoil, the walker the rats couldn't book — resolves three chapter-old questions in one unbroken block. Stage it: the folded papers, Ash reading, the Court reacting, the note last. Three beats, three reveals, each allowed to land.

5. **Commit to the dramatic-irony engine (see Ugly #2).** Retune chapters 8–11: cut or radically compress the senior-grey red herring (or give him one real scene — an interview on the ropewalk roof — so the herring has flesh), and sharpen every moment where Ash's refusal to see is the tension. The line "I had been *afraid* of where it went" (chapter 6) is the right register; build the middle on it.

6. **Reconcile the climax's two clocks.** One sentence establishing that the anchor is only workable at the neap — so the four days of paperwork are racing *toward a tide-table*, not away from a collapse — turns the audit montage from a pacing hole into a countdown. While there, give the bell's appointment-mechanics one clean early plant: the natural spot is the chapter 9 vault scene, where the Clerk's "the hours kept the appointments" can be expanded by a line Ash actually chews on.

7. **Convert the Wick-exposure aftermath into a half-scene (chapter 5).** Merrow reading the ward-shift page from the hall steps to a street going quiet is the novel's biggest public reversal and currently occupies one sentence. Put Ash on a wall across the street for a page. The chapter can lose the same length from the keeper fight, which is the second wax-golem set piece in three chapters and can be compressed hard.

8. **Cull epigrams by roughly a sixth, chapters 4–10 especially.** Mark every colon-or-dash aphorism in a pass; keep the best per page; convert the rest to plain narration. Vary sentence architecture — the em-dash appositive cascade appears on nearly every page of chapters 6 and 10. The prologue and chapters 9, 11, 12 should be touched least; their density is earning its keep.

9. **Tighten chapter 4 by one episode.** The Fish-Giver rescue is charming and thematically on-mission ("no monsters… a human-sized catastrophe"), but it is the third consecutive episode in a chapter that already contains the Ratsmeet and the pigeon election. Fold it into chapter 8 (which is shorter and also episodic) or trim it to a third of its length. Tansy's case must stay — it plants the Gentleman, the purr, and the household — but consider seeding Tansy one chapter earlier so her adoption doesn't read as a mid-book swerve.

10. **Ration the "I will not describe it" gesture.** The refusal-to-narrate move (the vole scuffle, the wisp on the thread, the confession, the final working, the redacted gargoyle ride) appears six-plus times. Twice it's powerful (the confession; "the file may show a redaction here"). By the fifth use it reads as a dodge. Keep two, or three at most; write through the rest.

11. **Let one victory fail in the middle third.** Between chapters 3 and 9 Ash wins essentially every engagement — goose, hound, keeper, keeper again, wisps, election, purr, lease, court after court falls for him. The book notices ("the city had begun… to keep its cat") but noticing isn't fixing. The fish-market ward that fails in chapter 10 is the right idea in the wrong place — move that failure earlier (chapter 7–8 range) and let something visible be lost because of it.

12. **Check the promise of chapter 6's free information.** The Gentleman answers everything unpaid, and Ash flags it: "the conversation was bait of another kind." The manuscript never cashes what the bait *was* — chapter 11's note ("You now owe the house") attaches to the hearing exhibit, not the chapter 6 conversation. Either tie the two explicitly (the conversation created the debt's occasion) or trim the "bait" flag; an unfired gun in this book, of all books, is conspicuous.

13. **Positioning note for submission.** This is adult fantasy with full crossover reach — shelve it beside Pratchett, Hardinge, and *The Tainted Cup*, and lead the query with the hook (a murdered witch's cat inherits her unfinished magic and investigates, spending his nine lives as case expenses). Do not let anyone package it as a cozy cat book; the flats scene and the Toll are the proof it's more, and the sample chapters sent out should include chapter 9 or the prologue, not the mid-book episodes.

The bones here are excellent and the heart is real. Tighten the middle, spend the deaths on the page, and make the mercy cost something — then this is a book people press into other people's hands.
