# Review: *The Nine Lives of Ashcat* (manuscript)
**Reviewer persona: the Fantasy Fiction Fan** — shelves full of Pratchett, Le Guin, Susanna Clarke, Hobb, Gaiman, Jemisin; judges a book on worldbuilding depth, magic-system rigor, and whether the wonder is earned or decorative.

## Verdict — 9/10

This is the best-costed magic system I've read in years, wrapped around a fair-play murder mystery narrated by a cat who keeps accounts the way Vimes keeps boots. Magic is needlework; debt is the physics of the world; death is a bureaucracy with a brass rule set into the desk — and, crucially, *every one of these rules holds under pressure*. The book states its iron law early ("spent is spent"), tempts three separate characters to break it, and lets the refusal of a refund be both the mystery's solution and the tragedy's engine. It reads like Susanna Clarke's texture at Pratchett's tempo with Hobb's willingness to hurt you. It is not flawless — the cosmic antagonist stays a fog-shaped abstraction, one crucial rule is minted in the same scene that spends it, and every creature in Hollowmere speaks in the same polished aphorism — but I finished it at two in the morning and immediately reread the prologue to watch the trick being set up. That's the review, really. The rest is itemization, which feels appropriate for this book.

---

## The Good

**The magic system is genuinely airtight, and its costs actually bite.** "Magic here is needlework. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling candles" (Prologue) is a thesis the book never betrays. Wards are stitches; stitches are *paid* at the pull; "thread that has become stitch is not thread any more" (Ch. 11) — and that last rule is not flavor, it is the entire case: Bodkin's forty-year crusade is founded on the lake's lie that unpicking the seam will refund his eight spent lives, and Ash's proof — "every stitch you lifted, coming off the seam as slack" (Ch. 12), planted as physical evidence back in Ch. 5's warehouse — is what breaks him. Sanderson's Laws, executed with more grace than Sanderson.

**The costs are permanent, and the epilogue honors them.** The Toll at the mud-flats (Ch. 9) takes not "a memory" in the abstract but *the* memory — the chart-table, the lamplight, "that winter I tried eight strays" — and Ch. 10 makes you sit with what that means: "The case was all still there. The warm was not." The epilogue confirms it's never coming back. When a book refuses to refund its own protagonist, I trust everything else it tells me.

**The economy of the world is the worldbuilding.** Favor-knots tied where "the whole Weft could see it"; a magpie who won't fence grief because "it only ever comes back on the fencer" (Ch. 2); the Locksmiths' escrow whose honesty is guaranteed by the horror of the midnight reading-aloud (Ch. 10); the ninth bell *pawned by the city itself* against a plague-season's Tolls (Ch. 9). This is a city where every institution runs on debt, which makes the climax — winning by audit, the bell's accrued century of hours discharging the pawn — feel not like a lawyer's gag but like the world's physics vindicated. "Against a thing like that, claws are a rumor. *Paperwork* is an appointment" (Ch. 9) is the book's whole method, and it's set up three chapters in advance by the Horologists' golden hour, which establishes the precedent ("proved, in gold, over Gravamen") the finale spends. Chekhov would weep.

**The mystery plays fair on the reread.** The mirror-wise stitch is planted in Ch. 2 as Ash's own irritation at his paws ("nothing in this book costs me more"), confirmed at the warehouse wall (Ch. 5), and detonated in Ch. 11 when Ash mends his own shadow and realizes the mirrored hand isn't a technique, "it is a *life* — the shape left in a body by years of lying against someone else's work." Nobody taught the murderer her stitch; the stitch teaches itself to anything that loves the stitcher from close enough. A murder method that is literally a proof of love is the best clue I've encountered in genre fiction this decade. And Bodkin is seeded honestly: "You fight like a housecat." / "I was one." / "Were." (Ch. 3) reads as kindness the first time and as autobiography the second.

**Chapter-end hooks are consistently excellent.** "Then that is where we are going" (Prologue, and again Ch. 9, as a deliberate bookend); "It had blinked" (Ch. 6); "and then I went to spend some lives" (Ch. 8). The Gargoyle-and-Ghost chapter (Ch. 7) is a standalone masterpiece — forensics via rain barrel, dreaming mastiff, and frost-ferns; the Understudy's "The performance did not fail. It was *robbed*. And so, small colleague of the wings — so were you" is the book's emotional hinge and earns every line.

**Elspeth is the best dead character I've read since *The Fifth Season*'s prologue victims.** She is revealed almost entirely through paperwork — a shift-book of unpaid ward-fees, a forty-year-old escrow box, a candle bought and never burned for a guest she owed — and the last Remembered Day (Ch. 12), in which she folds the kerchief and carries the sleeping cat off her work before walking out to die, is devastating precisely because it is housekeeping. "The kettle is not your fault. I know it took three tries" broke me.

## The Bad

**The Mere is a mechanism, not a being.** For nine chapters the lake is magnificent dread — "something vast lay against the underside of the world with the patience of owed money" (Ch. 9) — but it never acquires a history, a nature, or a want beyond "open the seam." What is it? Where did it come from? Why does a city sit on top of it? The day-side children's story is gestured at in Ch. 2 and never told. Le Guin would have given us the shadow's name; Clarke gave the Gentleman with the Thistle-down Hair an aesthetic and an appetite. This book's ultimate antagonist is *load-bearing fog*, and the closing joke — "found in contempt *in absentia, at the bottom of itself*" (Ch. 12) — is funny at the exact moment the book can least afford to deflate its one cosmic horror.

**The anchor arithmetic is minted at the moment it's spent.** Elspeth's anchor cost *eight* lives (Ch. 8). Bodkin closes the same seam with *one*, justified by a rule — "An anchor wants a life that is *finished paying*" (Ch. 12) — that appears for the first time in the sentence that uses it. Everything else in this magic system is established before it's needed; this, the single most load-bearing transaction in the book, is asserted mid-climax by the person it kills. In a book this rigorous, that's not a quibble, it's the one visible seam.

**Voice homogeneity.** Ash's ledger-voice is a triumph, but Brindle, Merrow, the Clerk, the Grey Boss, Sootbeard, Wick, and Bodkin all speak in the same cadence: setup, reversal, epigram. "Claws are a rumor. Paws are an appointment" (Bodkin, Ch. 3); "we are a court, not a revolving door" (Clerk, Prologue); "warm the way his hall is warm, which is to say: by the burning of something" (Ash on Wick, Ch. 5). Individually these lines are superb. Cumulatively, by Ch. 10 I could no longer tell witnesses apart with my eyes closed, and in a book *about* recognizing individual hands — where the whole plot turns on knowing a stitch's signature — that's an ironic failure.

**The middle is episodic, and it shows.** The Fish-Giver rescue (Ch. 4) and the caulker's shadow (Ch. 8) are lovely miniatures — and "held is not spent" (Ch. 8) does become climax-critical — but both read as detachable side-cases: arrive, solve, collect a thematic rhyme, move on. Between the Wick exoneration (Ch. 5) and the Locksmiths' box (Ch. 10), the murder investigation itself advances mostly in the seams around these vignettes, and I felt the drag.

**The reader laps the narrator.** By the end of Ch. 7 — cat-shaped crossing, mirror-hand, one-voiced familiar-thread — any attentive reader has written the name Ash refuses to write. The book knows this ("the ledger and I did not discuss it") and turns the refusal into characterization, which half-works; but the gap between reader certainty and Ch. 11's reveal is three full chapters, and the decoy suspect meant to fill it — the senior grey of the Court of Whiskers — is introduced late (Ch. 7), never given a scene, and cleared off-page by a sealed ledger (Ch. 11). A red herring you never meet is not a red herring; it's a footnote.

**Small unpaid invoices.** Ash is fifteen years old — elderly for a cat — yet trains against geese, out-fights counting-house hounds, and sprints tidal flats; with nine new-made lives in play, one line about what the lives do to a body's span would settle it, and no line comes. Fenn, whose taking is "the worst thing I have watched in this city" (Ch. 9), is never explicitly among the returned in the epilogue — the one named victim whose homecoming I needed confirmed and didn't get. And the Gentleman answering Ash's questions gratis in Ch. 6 is explained only as "the conversation was bait of another kind," a check the book gestures at (the knot-debt of Ch. 11) but never quite cashes on the page.

## The Ugly

**The ending refunds the tragic choice.** Chapter 9 is the moral summit of the book: the fog offers every missing soul home — the coats filled, the roosts loud, the child's jacket — for one cat standing aside, and Ash says *no* and pays for it with a death and a memory. It is magnificent. And then Ch. 12–13 deliver... everything anyway. Seam sewn *and* the missing "ordered disgorged"; the lamplighters walk home, the child comes back, the roosts refill. The sole permanent casualty of the refusal is one anonymous fisherman's oilskin (Epilogue). I respect that the book names that cost and pins it on Ash — but one unclaimed coat is a token against a chapter that promised a real price. This is the have-your-city-and-save-it-too ending, and it's the closest the book comes to breaking my trust. If I warn a friend about anything, it's this: the darkest bargain in the book turns out to be a bluff the plot was always going to cover.

**Everything is clever, always.** This is the voice problem at system scale. Every paragraph closes on a mic-drop; every institution is a delightful irony; every grief is immediately upholstered in wit. Mostly the book earns it — the kettle, the blank page in the chest-ledger, Shear laying down the needle "the way you lay down something you have decided not to carry any further" (Ch. 11) are wit *in service of* hurt. But there are stretches (the Ratsmeet, the pigeon election, the auditors falling in love with a filing) where Hollowmere stops feeling like a city and starts feeling like a revue — thirty brilliant sketches wearing a trench coat. Pratchett's Ankh-Morpork got away with this because Vimes was allowed to be boring and angry sometimes. Ash is never allowed to be less than quotable, and by Ch. 10 I was occasionally exhausted by a book I loved.

---

## Specific Recommendations

1. **Give the Mere one page of history.** The Ch. 8 dream of the forty-year working is the natural slot: let Ash glimpse *what the city was before the seam* — one concrete image of what the lake did when it was loose, or one line of the Grey Boss's or the Ratsmeet's oldest story about it. The antagonist needs a face on the underside of the glass, not just "hoping."

2. **Plant the "finished life" anchor rule before the climax.** Move it into the Clerk's mouth — he's already the rules-explainer — during the Ch. 9 thread-audit ("an anchor takes a settled account; yours will not settle for years, sir"), or into Elspeth's confession in Ch. 11. Then Bodkin's "your own law, kit" in Ch. 12 lands as recall instead of invention.

3. **Differentiate the secondary voices.** Give Merrow flat, unfigurative shift-boss speech (she's closest already: "I answer what is asked. No more" — keep her there and strip her remaining epigrams); let the Grey Boss be genuinely oblique rather than quotably oblique; let Wick babble without landing his own best lines. Reserve the aphorism for Ash, the Clerk, and Bodkin — the three ledger-keepers — so the style becomes characterization instead of wallpaper.

4. **Confirm Fenn.** One sentence in the Epilogue's homecoming paragraph — Fenn walking back up the terraces to his lamp, or explicitly *not* — because he is the named face of the Mere's taking and the reader is owed the line either way.

5. **Either stage the senior-grey suspect or cut him.** As is (Ch. 7, 9, 11) he's cleared by paperwork we never see dramatized. One actual scene — Ash interviewing him on the ropewalk roof, reading his gait, being *wrong* about something — would give the Ch. 8–10 gap a live suspect and make the Ch. 11 ledger-clearing a relief instead of an aside.

6. **Make the Fish-Giver episode carry case-weight.** It's the most detachable sequence in the book (Ch. 4). The bank already "banks for the Chandlers" — let the reviewed paperwork surface something Ash carries into the Wick confrontation in Ch. 5 (the cart-slot's ledger trail, a Gravamen counter-signature), so the kindness is also a clue and the chapter earns its acreage twice.

7. **Raise the price of the ending.** Options in ascending order of nerve: name more than one unreturned soul; have the "escrow reached back only so far" line cost something the reader has met (a roost elder, one lamplighter of the four); or make Ash's contested weeks measurably lengthen the missing's absence in a way someone *says to his face*. The Ch. 9 refusal deserves a scar the Epilogue can't polish.

8. **Give Ash one un-clever chapter beat.** Somewhere in Ch. 8–9, let the narration go flat and stay flat for a page — no epigram, no reversal — the way the blank chest-ledger page works in Ch. 11 (that blankness is the single most affecting device in the book precisely because the voice *fails*). Once more, earlier, would inoculate the whole middle against revue-fatigue.

9. **One line on cat-years and new-made lives.** The Clerk's desk is the place: he measures thread; let him remark, dryly, what nine new lives do to an old cat's arithmetic. It closes a hole and it's free characterization.

10. **Cash the Gentleman's "conversation was bait" explicitly.** When his note in Ch. 11 names the price ("One knot, of my choosing"), add half a sentence tying it back to the free answers of Ch. 6 — that the answers were the loan and this is the interest — so his one violation of his own no-free-lunch nature is retroactively priced rather than waved at.

— *Filed with admiration, two pots of tea, and a genuine hope for the sequel the thin active folder promises. The kettle line got me. Twice.*
