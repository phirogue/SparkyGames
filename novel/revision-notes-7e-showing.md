# Seventh edition: stop explaining the world

The owner's note, in full:

> the novel right now has a tendency of telling things, rather than showing
> them. there are a lot of exposition dumps.

With the example given:

> Fifteen. It means I am stiff in the mornings until the sun gets into me, it
> means I take the stairs and not the banister, and it means I retired some
> while ago without telling anybody, there being nobody to tell.

That paragraph is the whole diagnosis in miniature, and the reason is worth
stating exactly, because it is not "too much telling" in the abstract.

**Everything that paragraph asserts, the prologue then dramatises anyway.**

- *stiff in the mornings* → "I wait longer than I once needed to, because
  waiting is cheap and the other thing is not."
- *take the stairs and not the banister* → the gap over the alley at Cooper's,
  looked at, counted, and gone round.
- *retired* → "I sat afterward longer than the work deserved, and did not
  mention it to anybody."

The showing is already there and it is good. The telling is a *summary placed
in front of it*, which spends the surprise before the scene can earn it. The
reader is told the conclusion, then walked through the evidence for a
conclusion they have already accepted. Both halves work; only one can be kept.

This is the governing insight of the pass: **this book does not have an
exposition problem so much as a redundancy problem.** In most cases the scene
that would replace the dump is already on the page, a paragraph or two later.
The edit is nearly always a deletion, not a rewrite.

## The five shapes

### T1. The definition before the experience

The narrator names a thing and glosses it *before* the reader has met it, and
then the meeting itself gets summarised.

> A gutter-wisp: a swallowed lantern of a thing, all glow and appetite. They
> eat small lies, and the city breathes out enough of those to keep a great
> many of them fed.
>
> [...] I would like to tell you I handled it with grace. What happened is that
> a cat and a lit appetite had a disagreement in a foggy gutter.

Exactly backwards. The creature is explained at length and the fight — the only
part the reader could have *seen* — is waved off in a clause.

The worst instance in the book is the rag-wraith, because the image immediately
before it is perfect and the definition immediately after adds nothing:

> It has sleeves. It has a collar. It has the shape of a person who is not in
> it.
>
> They have a name, things like this. Rag-wraiths: clothes whose owners are
> missing.

The second paragraph is the first paragraph with the poetry taken out. Cut.

**Detection:** a colon introducing an apposition that defines a noun; "they
have a name"; "which is"; any sentence that would sit comfortably in a
glossary.

**Repair:** let the thing act first. Name it late, in passing, or not at all.
Trust the image that already landed.

### T2. Tell, then show the same thing

As the owner's example. A stated conclusion followed by its own dramatisation.

**Detection:** a paragraph of assertions, then a scene that proves them. Look
especially at the paragraph *before* any good concrete beat — the dump is
usually sitting right on top of it.

**Repair:** delete the assertions. Keep the beat. If a fact truly has no scene
anywhere, give it one line at the moment it first *matters*, not on
introduction.

### T3. The curriculum vitae

A character introduced by résumé instead of behaviour.

> Her name, for the record --- because tonight of all nights the record matters
> --- is Elspeth Vane. The city calls her the Needle of Hollowmere, mostly
> behind her back, always with respect. She is a witch, which in this city is a
> trade like any other: she sews. Wards, hems, bindings [...] Magic here is
> needlework.

Six facts, no scene, before we have watched her do one thing. The magic system
is announced in a sentence in chapter zero — and then chapter two dramatises it
properly, in her own remembered teaching voice, which is where it belongs and
where it is good.

**Repair:** name, and one action. Everything else waits until a scene needs it.
Where the trade must be explained, explain it in *her* voice, in memory, at the
point of use.

### T4. The chapter that announces itself

> Before the dreams, the living. A suspect enters the file in this chapter,
> properly pinned, on evidence.

This tells the reader what the chapter is about to accomplish. It is also a
standing prohibition already on the books — *narration reports what happened,
never what the book will do* — which this line breaks in its second sentence.

**Repair:** open on the scene. The file-keeping voice is welcome; the table of
contents is not.

### T5. The gloss on a landed image

One figure, then a clause explaining how to take it. Already prohibited in the
line rules; it has crept back.

## What is deliberately NOT touched

This is a noir, narrated in retrospect by a detective who keeps a file. Summary
is not a defect in that voice — it is the voice. The pass does not touch:

- Ash weighing, judging, or refusing to decide ("I have had a long time to
  decide which one I was obeying, and I have not decided").
- Case-status reasoning where the reasoning *is* the action.
- The Clerk's dialogue, except to shorten the one terms-of-service speech.
- Anything on the line-rules protected list.

The test is not "is this summary?" It is: **does a scene elsewhere already do
this work?** If yes, the summary goes. If no, the summary stays and earns its
place.
