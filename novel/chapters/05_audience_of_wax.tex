\chapter{The Ward That Failed}

The Wickhouse squats on the Mere-edge, fat as a tick, and every stone of it is warded in her stitch.

I had known the building my whole life the way you know a rich man's back: something large that faces away from you. Now I stood on the sea-wall at dusk with Merrow's shift-book tally in my memory and Wick's ledger-page in my shadow, and I read the place with my new eyes, and what I read stopped my breath. Her seam ran the whole water wall --- foundation to chimneys, stitch upon stitch upon year upon year of stitches, the biggest single working I had ever seen outside the one I had not yet seen. Two years of unpaid ward-shifts, the tally said. The tally was modest. That wall held a \emph{decade} of her evenings, and I could name the seasons of it: that hard winter; that flood-spring; that autumn she came home smelling of pond-water and would not say why and sat closer to the fire than the fire required.

Bodkin was on the sea-wall.

I had not seen him arrive, which by then I understood to be the only way he arrived. He sat looking at the water --- not the warehouse; the water --- with his one eye half shut, an old grey stone among the granite ones.

``Keep your feet dry and your business short,'' he said, without preamble, as I came up. ``And go in by the wax door, not the water door.''

``You know the building.''

``Everyone on this side of the wall knows the building.'' He looked at it then, briefly, the way you glance at a debt. ``Nothing good ever came off that water. You are going in anyway.''

``I am.''

``Then that is the geometry of it,'' he said, and settled deeper into his coat, and stayed exactly where he was, on the stone. ``I don't go in there.''

I have promised to tell this in the order I lived it, and in the order I lived it, that was an old cat's sensible fear of a bad building, and it made me trust him more, not less. An adviser who shares your risks is a comrade; an adviser who names the risks he will not share is honest. I put him down in the ledger as honest. I want the reader to see me do it --- see me write \emph{honest}, in my own careful hand, about the sentence \emph{I don't go in there} --- because this book is a case file, and a case file must show its errors, and that entry is not an error. Every word he said to me on that wall was true. It took me the rest of my lives to learn what the truth is for.

\scenebreak

Getting to the wax door is the Mereside's own toll.

The fog on those terraces has residents. I paid my way across --- patience at the curbs, shadow in the long stretches, and at the last crossing, where the cold came up through my pads like a hand closing, the pale strength again, spent freely --- and even so I did not arrive unmet.

One entry for the \emph{WATCH THIS} page before anything else, taken on the way down: the Mereside steps wear a white stain where the fog's breath has reached, every night, for longer than the stone remembers --- the fog's own tide-line, and every cat on this side of the city knows it the way you know where the ice is thin. The wet that night stood a full step above the stain. The line had moved. Nobody had rung a bell about it, because the day-side does not read steps, and the night-side had a murder to think about.

Between me and the door handle, dry-footed on a street glazed with wet, stood one of the Drowned.

I had heard of them all my life and seen them never: the Mere's patient people. It did not lunge and it did not stalk. It \emph{arrived} --- that is the whole motion they own; they are always arriving, the way the tide is always arriving, and the horror of it is precisely that there is no hurry in it anywhere. It regarded me with the mild interest of deep water, and it stood where it stood because something under the lake preferred that door unopened, and I understood for the first time, in my pelt and not merely in my ledger, that my case had two sides, and that one side was a murder, and the other side was \emph{old}, and wet, and did not have habits so much as tides.

What does a cat do against the patience of water? What she taught me. You do not outwait the tide; you use its schedule. The Drowned arrive; therefore they are never quite \emph{here}. I spent the red all at once --- not at the thing, past it, one committed burst through the gap its arriving had not yet closed --- and I was through the wax door with the fog's fingers still turning where my tail had been.

Inside: wax to the rafters.

White hills of it, tallow-bergs and beeswax cliffs, aisle upon aisle in the gloom, a hundred winters of light not yet sold. The merchant smell lay over everything, honey and render and coin. And under the merchant smell, the way a signature lies under a seal, the other smell. Pond-bottom. Old rope. Patience.

The wax was not stock. The wax was \emph{feed}.

I walked those aisles the better part of an hour, and the sums assembled themselves around me like the case-board come to life. The Chandlers' fortune says the city buys its light out of fear of the dark. But here was the reserve, the deep cellar of the trade, stacked against a wall the Mere touches --- and along that wall, her seam, and against her seam, on the waterside, something that fed. The guild's ledgers would call it spoilage: so many hundredweight of stock written off each quarter, damp-lost, rat-lost --- the rats, who book everything, would laugh their transparent grey laughter at that line. The wax went to the wall and the wall's tenant took it, and her stitching held the tenant to its side of the bargain, and Ellery Wick's entire empire stood on a seam he got for free from a witch who could not afford to let it fail.

Her ward did not fail. I am ahead of myself. But so was she; so was everyone; the whole city was ahead of itself and did not know it.

Halfway down the water wall I found the place where the seam was wrong, and before I tell you what I found I must tell you where I went, because in the middle of that cold warehouse, with my nose an inch from her stitching, I fell into a Remembered Day, and it was the first one, and I did not choose it.

They come from sleeping in dangerous places --- some economy of the pale strength, spending itself as memory when the body will not spend it as anything else. I had not slept in days. I stood at the wall with her thread-scent in my head, and between one breath and the next it was years ago, and warm, and her table was under me instead of stone.

The same chart. That is what undid me: the chart of this same water wall, half-sewn, spread across her table in lamplight --- and a much smaller cat deployed on top of it as a paperweight, asleep, entirely certain of the world. Her voice above me, amused, talking to her needle the way she did when the work was long:

``That winter I tried eight strays. Every one declined the interview. Wise animals. The ninth fell asleep on my work---'' the needle going down, through, drawn, the little tug, a word ending, ``---and I took it for a signature.''

I came back to the cold with my paws on the actual wall, on the actual stitches the dream had shown me half-sewn, and I stood there while the warehouse dark leaned in, doing the sum I had been refusing all season. Eight strays declined. The ninth signed. She kept count of strays the way she kept count of nothing else in her cheerful shambles of a house, and Cardew's voice on the quay: \emph{you were her ninth, you know. She would never say what she meant by keeping count.}

Nine of us. There were nine of something else, too, and the Clerk had not filed one since he sat at a much smaller desk. I wrote the two nines side by side in the ledger of my chest and drew no line between them yet. But I felt the line wanting to be drawn, the way you feel weather.

\scenebreak

Now the seam.

At the near end, man-height and below, her work was \emph{wrong}, and the wrongness was of a kind I had no word for and have had to build one. Not torn --- tearing leaves violence in the thread, and there was none. Not burned, not rotted, not the Mere's slow patient chewing, which I could see and recognize further down, held off by every stitch. \emph{Undone.} Each stitch lifted out clean and whole, the way she herself lifted stitches when a hem displeased her --- her exact movement, her exact order, the little releasing twist at the top of each --- but run \emph{backward}. Unsewn in the precise reverse of the sewing, as if time had been persuaded to reread a page from the bottom.

I did the only thing that would let me be sure. I re-sewed a hand's length of it, her pattern, my own paws --- and to re-sew what was unsewn I had to follow the unsewing, stitch into the ghost of each lifted stitch, and my paws, following, \emph{ran the line mirror-wise}. Right to left. The way my paws had wanted to run her door-ward, when I stood on the frame irritated at my own hands and thinking nothing of it.

Whoever unpicked this wall did it in her own stitch, mirrored, faster than any needle honestly goes. Perfect technique. And Elspeth Vane taught exactly nobody --- the Circuit begged her for apprentices for thirty years and she turned away every one; ask Cardew; everyone asked Cardew.

The lifted thread told me the second finding of the wall. It lay where the unpicking had dropped it, loose coils along the foot of the stone, and when I took it up to work with it there was nothing in it. Dead slack. A stitch of hers, standing, holds what she paid into it --- an evening banked in every hand's length; that is the whole trade of warding. These coils were spent thread, empty the full length of them, nothing of what was paid in coming back out at the lifted end. Whoever ran her seam backward had unlocked the wall and recovered not one evening of the decade. Undone is not refunded.

I took the hand's length of her thread away with me --- evidence: hers, then mine --- and I went out by the wax door with the warehouse dark at my back, and Bodkin was gone from the sea-wall.

A case wants stating. She talked to her needle when the work was long, and I had taken it for company; it was method. A finding kept in the chest is a finding you can still argue with. So I stated mine aloud, to the empty wall, in the flat voice you keep for entering evidence, there being nobody on the stone to hear it: ``The slack comes off dead. Coils of spent thread, nothing left in them. What was paid into the stitching does not come back out at the unpicking.''

And the fog over the stone held the shape of a sitting cat for one second longer than fog holds anything, and in the order I lived it, that was only the fog.

On the wall where he had sat, the sum of the chapter sat instead, and it said: the wax feeds a thing the guild will not name. Her ward holds the thing. She held the ward unpaid, for years, in secret. And the man who profits by all of it \emph{bought her debts and called them due}.

Coincidence, she used to say, is what mice call carelessness.

It was time to meet Ellery Wick.

\scenebreak

The invitation, when it came, was a candle.

That is how the Chandlers grant audience: a beeswax taper arrives, trimmed by the guild's own chandler to burn exactly one hour, stamped with the bee at the base. You light it and start walking, and if it dies before you reach the Guildhall, so does the appointment. It is a rich man's way of pricing your eagerness, and it is also --- I admit it with the reluctance owed to an enemy's craft --- rather beautiful.

They had sized the taper for a man's stride. I carried it lit through the evening streets in my teeth.

The image deserved a dignity it did not receive: a black cat crossing three districts at speed with a live flame borne before him like a comet with dignity problems, fog curling off the wick-heat, wax dripping --- I will say it --- onto the whiskers, which took a week to feel right again. Children pointed. A watchman blessed himself.

I arrived with a quarter-inch to spare.

The Guildhall of the Chandlers is a chapel to the bee. Ten thousand lit votives line its hall, tier upon tier, and the heat stands in the room like a body --- you walk into it as into surf. Above, the great combs of the guild's own hives mutter behind bronze grilles. And between me and the stair, the keeper of the hall unfolded from beside the votive-tiers: a tower of poured wax with a gentle face --- cousin to my counting-room acquaintance, but built to another scale entirely, a cathedral of a creature, warm as the room and patient as the room, with hands like dripping bells.

``The Guildmaster will see you presently,'' it said, in a voice like a wick drowning, and \emph{presently}, in the grammar of such keepers, meant: \emph{when you have shown the hall what you are}.

I will not make the fight longer in the telling than it was in the doing; it shames a warm room to be fought in. The keeper was slow, and enormous, and lit --- and that was the reading of it, in four seconds of looking: a thing of wax serves the flame it carries, and ten thousand flames in tiers make a weather of rising air, and a cat who cannot out-muscle a cathedral can out-\emph{draft} one. I led its gentle bulk on a tour of its own votive-tiers, close and fast, and the wind of its turning did what I could never do: bowed a hundred flames toward its own body, softened it at the shoulders, at the knees --- until it stopped, mid-reach, considered the puddling of its own wrists with that unfinished face, and withdrew with immense courtesy to its alcove, conceding the stair.

She would have laughed until she needed the chair. I climbed the stair and was seen presently.

\scenebreak

Ellery Wick is a small man behind a desk built to make everything else in the room smaller, and he was already talking when the door finished opening.

``The ward-cat! The seam-mender --- the hall talks, the whole row talks, sit --- no, not there, the good chair. The good chair, I insist.'' Warm, voluble, quick-eyed, in guild black with beeswax-colored facings; warm the way his hall is warm, which is to say: \emph{by the burning of something}. I took the good chair. It is a mistake to refuse a frightened man's hospitality; it tells him you have already decided.

And he was frightened. Under the patter, under the desk and the facings, Ellery Wick was a man who had been doing sums at night, and I had come to check his working.

I laid the whole case on his desk, item by item, in the order a cat lays out prey. The ledger page in his own hand: buy Vane's paper, all of it, due Lady's Day. Merrow's tally: two years of ward-shifts at his warehouse, paid to nobody. The hand's length of her thread from his own water wall, unpicked in her mirrored stitch. The docket-scrap from the gutter of Needle Lane, consignee scratched out. And I watched him meet each exhibit, and here is my testimony, for what a cat's reading of a man is worth in whatever court finally hears all this: at the ledger he flinched like a man touched on a bruise he knew he had. At the thread he went \emph{blank} --- honest, ignorant blank, the blank of a man shown a wound in a language he cannot read.

Then he did the thing I had not priced. He got up, opened his own strong-cabinet, and laid his books on the desk beside my exhibits. All of them. True books --- I know true books now; I have been schooled by rats and magpies, the finest forensic faculty in the city --- and the true books said what the stolen page had said, and worse, and he narrated his own disgrace item by item in a voice that got steadier as it got more damning, because Ellery Wick is a coward about everything except arithmetic.

``I wanted her evicted,'' he said. ``I wanted her \emph{out}, cat. Out of the district, out of the trade, out of --- reach. Her lease, her debts, her standing --- I bought the lot, and I timed the calling for Lady's Day because the Circuit meets at Lady's Day and the humiliation would be public and she would have to \emph{go}.'' He looked at the thread lying on his blotter, and his hands stopped. ``You do not kill the one needle holding your roof shut. You do not kill the needle. Whatever you have decided I am --- and I can read a cat's face better than my clerks think --- follow the sum. Alive and ruined, she re-wards my wall from whatever gutter I chase her to. She was proud like that. She'd have done it to spite me. Dead---'' and his voice went, at last, where his sums had already gone, ``---dead, my wall is a larder with the pantry door coming open, and I am the man who fed the thing behind it for thirty years. So ask me your question. I have been asking it myself since the bells rang eight that night and I sat here \emph{hoping} it was me she'd ruined herself for. Who else knew the ward was hers?''

I looked at him a long time. Then I put my last question in front of him, flat as a docket: \emph{a cart went down Needle Lane that night. Your cart-slot. Who signed it out?}

And Ellery Wick, guildmaster, monopolist, terror of leaseholds, went and fetched the counterfoil like an errand boy, and laid it down, and it was worse than anything in his books.

The cart-slot had been signed out against \emph{favor-paper}. Not coin, not credit: a favor the Chandlers' Guild itself owed --- owed for years, incurred by his own father's generation, the sort of debt that sits in a strongroom and is never, ever called --- called in that week, blind, no name given, no name \emph{asked}, the guild's own seal pressed beside a signature line that held no signature at all. Just a small, neat knot, drawn in ink. A knot of thread, on paper.

``Everything in this city comes through the usual quiet channels,'' Wick said, very quietly, for a man built of noise. ``You do not ask. The not-asking is what the quiet \emph{costs}. I have paid it my whole life. I never once asked what I was buying.'' He pushed the counterfoil across the desk to me with one finger. ``Take it. Ruin me with it if you like. I find I would rather be ruined than be next.''

\scenebreak

I could have squeezed him. The choice existed, and choices are the spine of any honest account: a guildmaster's knot tied into my thread where the whole Weft could see it would have opened every warded door in Wickrow to me for a year. He watched me decide what to do with him --- which was, of all the fear in that room, the most honest fear in it.

I exposed him.

By eight bells the next evening the Lamplighters had his books.

I was on the hall steps when Merrow read them. She came out with the shift-book under her arm and her ladder left inside, which for a lamplighter is shirtsleeves, and she read the requisition page to the street in her end-of-shift voice, flat as a level. \emph{Ward-shifts, Wickhouse, two years. Escort's fee: entered, paid.} \emph{Stitcher's fee:} --- and here she turned the book outward, so the street could see the column, and read the emptiness aloud --- \emph{blank.} Page after page, season after season, \emph{blank, blank, blank}, in that voice that has called rounds over wind off the Mere for twenty years and does not perform, until a man at the back who had bought his light from the bee all his life said something thick and went home with his coat buttoned wrong. She read the whole tally once. Then she read it again, slower, and the second reading was the one that did the work: the street went quiet the way a lane goes quiet when the lamp at the end of it dies.

By nine bells the criers had it. The terror margin --- the fat quarter of every candle's price that was fear and not wax --- died on the counting-room floor within the week, and a district that had bought its light out of fright for a generation learned exactly what its fright had been worth, and to whom. Wick kept his desk. Barely. The guild's own arithmetic saved him: he was the only man in the building who understood the water wall's true accounts, and now every soul in Wickrow knew there \emph{were} true accounts, and that the cat had them. He needs me now --- the cat who ruined him --- which she would have called the correct way round for a rich man to need anybody.

And so ended, formally, the first file of the case. Ellery Wick: guilty --- of debts bought quiet, of a witch's decade taken free, of feeding a nameless thing on the Mere-side of his wall and pricing the city's fear of it --- and innocent, provably, structurally innocent, of the murder of Elspeth Vane. You do not evict a woman you have had killed. I crossed him off, and I confess the crossing-off cost me: a suspect crossed off is a door closed, and the room behind the remaining doors gets darker by exactly that much light.

Home, then. The board on the chimney breast received the last pin of the season: the counterfoil, with its inked knot in the signature line, and from it a length of grey wool I ran with my own paws --- past the candle stub, past the docket, past the blank calling card that smelled of fog --- off the board's edge entirely, toward the window, toward the south of the city, where the favor-paper of every guild drains when it is called, where the carts change hands and the quiet channels run to ground.

Gravamen. The undertakers' district; the Court-adjacent shadow-market where debts go to be owned.

The quiet channels had an address now. Tansy slept on my paws while I sat up over the board, and the one uncut strand of her stirred in the draft under the window, and for the first time since the kettle I felt the case \emph{move} --- the whole weight of it, slow, like a barge coming off the mud.

Somebody in Gravamen had spent a knot of thread to put a cart on Needle Lane. In the morning, I went to meet the sort of gentleman you don't book.
