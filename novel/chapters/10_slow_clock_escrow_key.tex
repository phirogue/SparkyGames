\chapter{The Slow Clock and the Escrow Key}

The Horological Society's master clock runs four minutes slow. The Horologists know why. They are not fixing it.

I went to Gravamen because I was sent for. The Clerk had told me my file was attracting attention, and here was the attention, days later, under my own door: a letter from the Horological Society, sealed in wax the color of brass, requesting the assistance of \emph{the party of the irregular filings} in a matter of time owed. The Horologists owe the Court \emph{time}, literally, under contracts older than their tower, and a guild that owes the Court its own currency hears of everything irregular on the Court's books --- and a cat who intends to spring a bell from an evidence vault by paperwork had better apprentice himself to somebody, so I went. I did not arrive, this once, in the middle of somebody's catastrophe. The catastrophe had read my file, and sent for me by name.

The rounds that week had handed me my first failure as heir. The fish-market hem --- the first ward I stitched on my own authority, not mending her work but making mine --- let go inside a week. My stitch, not hers, and nobody came in the night to save it. I went back and did it again, uglier and deeper into the old cloth of the wall, and the second stitching holds to this day. I carried that to Gravamen like a stone in a boot: the standing was inherited. The hand is not. The hand you buy by doing a thing twice.

The Society's tower is a marvel: a building that is one instrument, every floor a wheel-train, the bones of it ticking like a chest full of hearts. Its master clock governs the district --- every counting-house regulator, every undertaker's hall clock, every watchman's turnip sets itself by the tower --- and for eleven years, I now know, the tower has lied to all of them by a growing margin, because for eleven years, every night, the Horologists have been \emph{skimming}. Four minutes here, a matter of seconds there, shaved off the district's clocks in the dead watch, gathered, wound onto a mainspring of appalling beauty in the tower's crown --- hoarded.

For their founder.

He is dying, the old master who built the tower's heart --- he has been dying for years with the stubbornness of his own escapements --- and the Society holds a contract, his first commission, signed by the city in an age when the city still signed such things: one sunrise, owed to him, dated \emph{on completion of the great work}. The great work was completed forty years ago --- his hands set the tower's crown-wheel the same season my witch walked out on the black glass; this city's debts all keep the same anniversaries --- and the sunrise was never paid; there was a war of lawyers, and the paper slept. He will not survive to spring. The sunrise he is owed will rise without him unless it is \emph{brought forward} --- and you cannot bring a sunrise forward, but you can, if you are a guild of desperate clockmakers, assemble one out of hoarded minutes: eleven years of skimmed time, spent all at once, one impossible golden hour over Hollowmere, out of schedule, with the old man's chair carried up to the tower gallery to meet it.

Hoarding time is Toll evasion, and the Court's auditors had noticed eleven years of the district's minutes failing to reconcile, and were en route.

The Society laid it all before me the way the city has learned to lay things before me: on a table, at night, with the door barred, half in hope and half in shame. What they wanted was a miracle. What they had was --- the Clerk's own phrase, arrived at last --- \emph{a paperwork error}. The right kind.

Here is the whole of the law of it in plain words, once, so that the reader need never practice it: the Horologists were not stealing time. They were taking back time the city owed them --- one sunrise, promised in writing, forty years past due --- and they had the paperwork to prove it. The trade calls that a \emph{lien}, and a lien is not evasion; it only has manners to keep: the taking-back declared, itemized, and spent in the open, before witnesses, with the balance struck.

They stared at me. I am told --- by Tansy, who had ridden along uninvited in the usual manner and sat through the council on the table's edge washing her ears with unbearable magisterial calm --- that I had begun, at some point in the exposition, to pace the table the way \emph{she} used to pace a hem.

The auditors arrived at dawn: two of them, grey-gloved, correct, with the Court's unhurried courtesy and dockets already opened. And the Society, coached to the syllable, did not hide the mainspring. They \emph{declared} it. Laid the sunrise contract on the bench beside eleven years of skimming-ledgers --- they had kept ledgers; they are Horologists; they could no more skip an entry than skip a tooth on a wheel --- and petitioned, formally, to discharge the city's oldest unpaid debt of light by public performance, all parties to witness, the Court to meter the spend and certify the balance.

I watched two auditors of the Hollow Court confront a filing that was irregular, enormous, and \emph{correct}, and I watched them fall in love. They stayed three days. They re-metered the mainspring twice for the pleasure of it. They found against the Society on two counts of procedure and waived both fees in consideration of \emph{exceptional bookkeeping}, a phrase I intend to have carved somewhere eventually.

And on the fourth evening, the Horologists spent the hoard.

The city has a golden hour now, in its memory, that appears in no almanac. It came on like a tide of honey: every clock in Gravamen stopping together, politely, as if for a funeral --- and then the light. Sunset ran backward. The undertakers came out onto their steps. The fog itself went up the terraces amber, and every sunbeam in the district --- I felt them, all at once, the whole map of warm rectangles that a cat carries of any city --- \emph{ignited}, an hour of them, out of schedule, unbudgeted, free. Word had gone around the night-side in the mysterious total way it goes: rooflines and window-ledges filled with cats sitting up and down Gravamen like gargoyles' apprentices; Sootbeard on his corner three districts away reported afterward, formally, one (1) unscheduled sunrise, \emph{received}. Merrow's lamplighters stood their ladders and let the lamps sleep through it. The old master sat his chair on the tower gallery with his guild around him and the Court's two auditors standing witness at the parapet, gloves folded, metering the golden air with instruments of brass and, I will testify, not looking at the instruments overmuch.

He died eleven days later, out of schedule and in no one's debt, and the Court processed him --- I have this from the Clerk --- on a docket marked \emph{paid in full, with sunrise}, and no one below the city found anything irregular in it at all.

The golden hour goes in this file for three reasons. Because the precedent is now law: \emph{the Court will honor a debt of light against a debt of time, and will help compute it} --- hold that sentence; the whole last chapter of this book stands on it. Because the auditors, who became first colleagues and then, in the way of auditors, something like friends, taught me over three days how collateral dockets are structured and --- their eyes gleamed the way brass gleams --- \emph{mis-structured}. And because in the middle of the golden hour I looked away from the light, the only creature on that roofline looking away, and I watched the Mere.

The black glass did not take the gold. The whole city ambered, and the lake lay in the middle of it like a hole cut in the page --- and around its rim, in the amber fog, shapes stood at intervals, unhurried, dry, \emph{watching the light spend}, and I understood that we had just shown the patient thing under the water exactly how much this city still had left in its accounts worth taking.

An hour bought and spent for love, over a city, in the open. And sunk in the vaults below, behind a grille on grey ribbon: a hundred \emph{years} of hours, banked in bronze.

The Mere and I, that golden evening, were doing the same arithmetic. Only one of us was hoping.

\scenebreak

The Locksmiths' Fellowship holds secrets in escrow, and Box 9-Crown was due to open that midnight.

I must explain the trade, because the day-side thinks a locksmith sells locks. The Fellowship's true stock is \emph{kept} secrets: locked boxes deposited with conditions --- open on a date, on a death, on a marriage, on a bell --- held in a hall with the best iron in the city and a charter with better teeth than the iron. And the charter's terrible clause, the one that makes the whole trade honest, is this: a box whose condition has come due, and whose named claimant does not appear, is \emph{unclaimed}, and an unclaimed secret is read aloud. On the steps. At midnight. In full. The Fellowship does not peek, does not edit, does not mercy. It is the reading-aloud that guarantees every other box in the hall: depositors trust the Locksmiths precisely because they have heard, from those steps, at some midnight of their lives, what the alternative to collecting your business costs.

Box 9-Crown was deposited forty years ago. Condition: \emph{the death of the depositor, plus one season}. The depositor: E. Vane, of Thimblefield.

The season had turned that week.

I learned of it the way the whole night-side learned of it, which was all at once, from the Fellowship's crier posting the midnight docket at dusk --- and by full dark the steps of the Locksmiths' hall held the strangest congregation of my season. Half the night-side wanted that box read: the Circuit's younger seats, hungry for anything of hers; Mistress Shear, near the front, wearing her correctness like frost; every broker of rumor in three districts; and --- fog-smelling, gloved, at the edge of the lamplight where the eye slides --- the Quiet Gentleman, attending in person, which the oldest heads on the steps said had not happened at a reading in living memory. The other half wanted it burned: Cardew, planted on the top step with her stick like a boundary-stone, on the simple grounds that whatever a dead witch locked away for forty years was locked away from \emph{somebody}, and the somebody was likelier than not standing in this crowd. And at the rim of it all, out where the lamplight failed, the fog off the canals stood in its shapes and did not disperse, and I marked their spacing, and their patience, and counted the Mere among the interested parties.

Nine-Crown. She numbered it in the year of the seam, and she numbered it \emph{nine}, and by then I no longer believed in this city's coincidences at all.

The Fellowship's law allows one interruption of a reading, and one only: a claimant. The box named its claimant in the deposit-deed, per the crier's docket, as \emph{my heir, in law} --- not by name, in the standard form --- and Elspeth Vane died, the whole city knew, without kin, without apprentice, intestate as weather. There was no heir in law. There was going to be a reading.

There was a cat on the steps.

I went up them at a quarter to midnight, past Shear's frost and Cardew's stick, and I sat down on the reading-stone itself in front of the Keeper of the Fellowship --- a vast, slow, iron-colored woman with the manner of a door --- and before the assembled night-side of Hollowmere I made my claim: heir in law to Elspeth Vane, of Needle Lane, called the Needle; her familiar of fifteen years; bearer of her unfinished magic, her collar, and her strand; mender of her wards; sewer of her shroud-seam, witnessed by the tide and Mother Cardew; presently investigating her murder.

The steps went up like the roosts in the seed audit. The Keeper looked down at me for a long iron moment. Then she pronounced what I had gambled the whole night on: that the claim was a real question, fit for a real court; that no reading may proceed while a real question stands; and that the Fellowship, whose charter predates the city's courts and defers to exactly one older jurisdiction, would refer the question of whether a cat can inherit to the only bench with standing over the estates of the dead.

The Hollow Court. A hearing. The box stayed --- sealed, guarded, condition suspended --- pending the ruling.

Two things happened in the crowd in the moment the gavel-word came down, and I was watching for both. Mistress Shear closed her eyes --- relief or fury, I could not read which, and the not-reading kept her pinned to my board another chapter. And the Quiet Gentleman raised one gloved hand, at the edge of the lamplight, in the gesture of a man acknowledging a well-played trick at cards --- then wrote something in a small book, and the writing of it reached me later that night, folded, fog-smelling, under my own door, in the only correspondence he has ever sent me. It said:

\emph{You have discovered the trade's one secret: possession is nothing; standing is everything. The hearing will be worth attending. I hold paper on several of the parties. --- G.}

And below that, in a hand suddenly smaller, as close to human as I ever saw him come:

\emph{She deposited it the week after the water. I brokered the box-fee. She paid it in tears --- one vial, escrow-grade. I have never sold it. Some accounts one keeps.}

\scenebreak

Home, late, the fog walking me the last three streets like a bailiff. And on the sill of her window, waiting for me, a vole.

Fresh. Whole. Laid straight along the sill-stone --- not dropped, \emph{placed}, wedged at the corner where the shutter meets the frame, exactly the way I had wedged the herring for the blind postmaster of the roosts, which is the way you leave food for a proud creature you do not intend to watch eat. The kill was clean, and old-fashioned, and close work. Some senior professional of the night-side, then, keeping accounts of his own.

I ate it on the sill in the fog, and I read it the plain way, the warm way: the city had begun, in its unbudgeted fashion, to keep its cat. Brindle carries my credit; Merrow chalks my mark; Sootbeard logs my crossings; and now somebody, wanting no receipt, had sent supper. A kindness from everyone and no one. I did not ask whose hand. Of all the exhibits in this file, it is the one I was glad to leave unattributed.

Tansy asleep in the exact center of the hearthrug; the board over the mantel crowded now, threads crossing threads, the whole city pinned and strung --- and I sat up on the mantel among my exhibits and put the chapter in order, as she taught me, before sleep.

The Court will honor strange debts, strangely computed, if the paper is true: \emph{proved, in gold, over Gravamen.}

The Court now owes me a hearing on the one question --- can a cat inherit? --- whose answer unlocks everything at once: the box and its forty-year secret; the estate; the standing to petition, as her heir, against dockets a stranger cannot touch. A bell's docket, say.

And in a vault under the city, behind grey ribbon: her confession --- I was as sure of it as I have been sure of anything since the kettle --- her own account of the water, the working, and the eight lights I watched go out at the corner of a dream. Deposited the week after the seam, sealed against the day of her death plus one season, addressed to an heir she would not name and kept count toward for forty years. \emph{Nine is the number I owe.}

She knew the bill would come due. She banked the truth against it, and paid the fee in tears, and spent the rest of her life assembling --- stray by stray, nine attempts, one signature --- somebody to read it.

\emph{Nine is the number I owe.} I know where I first heard that sentence: the chart-table, the lamplight, her voice above the needle, amused --- \emph{that winter I tried eight strays} --- and me small and certain in the middle of her half-sewn work. That night, before sleep, I reached for the Day itself, the way I had reached for it all season --- to sit in the warm of it a minute, under the lamp, beside the voice.

It is not there. The Toll took it at the flats, and I had felt it go, and that night on the mantel I finally went to look at what the going means. It means the fact is still mine --- eight strays, nine owed; her voice said so; my own file says so --- and the room the fact was kept in is gone. No lamp. No table. No voice over the needle. My own handwriting, telling me there was a voice. I sat with the difference a long time, up among the exhibits, with the whole case strung and pinned within reach and the one place I wanted to go in it nowhere at all.

The case was all still there. The warm was not.

The hearing was set for the dark of the moon. Before it, one more parcel of her estate was to find me --- the last, the strangest, thirty years late and insistent on a signature.

But that is the next chapter, and I have been fifteen years learning not to pull a thread before its turn.
