\chapter{The Ninth Bell}

The last Remembered Day came to me that same night, unhunted, in her chair.

I had never slept in the chair. The chair was not mine to fill; that had been a rule of the new world, and I had kept it through everything. But I came up from the workroom with my shadow newly whole and the name written at the head of the page, and the house was doing a thing it had not done since the kettle: it was \emph{waiting} --- and the chair stood in the moon's lamp with her shape still in the cushions, and I understood, in the way one understands the last turn of a route, that the rule had expired. That this was the door the dreams had been walking me toward all season, and it would only open from inside her chair, and it would only open once.

I got up into it. I put my chin where her hand used to fall.

And it was morning. \emph{That} morning.

I will set it down plainly. It has no fog in it, no water, none of the tactful corners of the other dreams --- because it is not her memory of a great working, guarded even in death. It is only her last ordinary day, kept in the cushions of a chair, and the pale strength paid it out to me whole, at par, one final draw against an account that closed at dusk.

Grey early light. The kettle on for her own tea --- the first kettle, the innocent one. She moves slowly; I had not seen it in the living days and I saw it now, the whole dream saw it: she is \emph{spent}. Weeks of it. Wards failing all over her district faster than they should --- the alarm-threads she keeps on forty years of workings tugging at her one after another, here a hem gone at the fish market, there a binding loose at the tannery, each one an evening's mending, each mending drawing down a purse that does not refill at her age --- and she knows. I watched her know. She stands at the workroom wall with her tea going cold, reading her own cut city the way I would later read her cut walls, and she says, to the wall, in the flat voice she kept for arithmetic that would not come out otherwise:

``Ward by ward. You are emptying me first, my grey one. That is craftsmanlike.''

She does not weep, and she does not send for the Circuit, and now I know why on both counts: the confession in the iron box. \emph{He is owed.} You do not call the constables on your own creditor. You settle, or you are collected, and she had spent forty years failing to invent a settlement, and so she did the only other thing she has ever done with a debt whose day has come. She put her books in order.

I watched her do it. It takes the whole dream, and it is the most terrible housekeeping ever performed, because none of it is for her. The workroom tidied --- for whoever must read the room after. Her second-best thimble set square on the workbox --- for the trade it would someday buy. The beeswax candle --- the good one, bought forty years ago and never yet burned --- out of its drawer and stood in its holder in the parlor, unlit, where a guest would see it. The red kerchief, washed at the basin, wrung, and folded on the sewing table --- not put away; \emph{folded, on the table, where a cat coming over the sill could not miss it}. She had washed it that morning. I had carried that fact since the prologue of this book like a pebble in the pad, and here was its whole weight: she folded her collar for me before noon, on the day she died at dusk, because she did not intend to be wearing it when the collecting came, and she did not intend it to go to the tide with her either.

Last of all, the sunbeam. Late morning, the good one, the March-through-October beam that crosses the work-table at eleven, and I am asleep in the middle of her chart-papers in it, because I was; I remember the day now from both sides, hers and mine, and from my side it was nothing, it was any day, it was a nap. From her side: she sets down her pen, and looks at me a long time, doing what I now recognize as the other ledger, the margin one. Then she crosses the room and \emph{picks me up} --- lifts me off her work the way you lift someone who might wake, one hand under, one hand over, the single liberty I permitted in this world and permitted to her alone --- and she carries me, the whole slow length of the workroom, to the hearthrug, and puts me down in the poorer, warmer light, and stands over me with her emptied hands, and says:

``There. Mind the house, Ash. I am going to see about the lamps.''

That is all. That is the whole bequest of the last Remembered Day: she carried me off her unfinished work and set me down by the fire, and went out on her own feet to be exhausted a final ward further, and the dream ends not at the parlor --- it spares us both the parlor --- but at the door, with her hand on the latch and her back to me and the evening coming up the lane to meet her, and I woke in her chair at the greyest hour with my face wet in the way of cats, which is invisibly, and no one will ever prove otherwise.

And I knew that \emph{after} had come.

The note came out of the ninth pocket smelling of geranium and forty years. Her late hand, small, unblotted, entirely herself:

\emph{If you are reading this, then you have done everything in the correct order, which means you are mine, which means I am past scolding you and it has all become your business after all. Very well. Three items, and mind the sequence, you know how I am about sequence.}

\emph{The bell wants ringing. The Court has held the city's hour a hundred years and the Court, of all of them, keeps honest books --- make the books say so. Nothing anchors without a measure, and I had the ninth bell the first time. You will need it.}

\emph{Spend nothing you cannot re-sew. I mean this. Your lives are not a purse, whatever the arrangement downstairs has taught you. They are the only thing I ever made without owing.}

\emph{And when you meet my first --- for you will; sequence, Ash; you were always the ninth of nine --- tell him what he is owed. I could not walk into the fog to say it, and I will not say it through you. You have read my books. You know the words.}

\emph{The kettle is not your fault. Whatever happens, whatever you saw: the kettle is not your fault. I know you took it off the fire. I know it took three tries.}

\emph{--- E.}

I sat in her chair while the grey came up the windows, and then I did what she said, in sequence, because I know how she is about sequence.

I went and made the books say so.

\scenebreak

The redemption of the Ninth Bell of Hollowmere took four days, one cat, two auditors, a clerk, and no miracles at all, which is why it worked.

I petitioned as the Needle's heir. The bell had been signed out once in a century --- one night, forty years ago, on the Circuit's surety, \emph{for the great sewing} --- and a surety, my grey-gloved colleagues confirmed, gleaming, confers standing on the surety's successors in the matter of the collateral. I had inherited, along with a house and a strand and nine pockets, the right to be heard on the docket of a pawned bell.

And the docket, once a profession finally located its error, was glorious. The auditors metered the vault itself --- the first audit of the Court's own holdings, they believed, since the pawn was struck --- and the finding ran thus: the bell had been taken as \emph{dead} collateral, a thing of bronze, valued at its metal against a plague-season's unpaid Tolls. But the bell was never dead stock. The bell held the ninth hour --- every night's unrung hour pooling into it for a hundred years, unclaimed, unspent, \emph{accruing} --- and the Court's own charter, the Clerk read aloud with two inkwells going, requires accrual on held assets to be credited against the securing debt. A century of banked hours, at the Court's own posted rate for time --- the rate it charges the Horologists; there was rich justice in that --- against one plague-season of Tolls.

The debt had been paid off, by the bell itself, sometime in the reign before last. Everything since was overage. The city of Hollowmere, the finding concluded, did not owe the Hollow Court. The Hollow Court owed the city \emph{an hour a night for a hundred years}, and the instrument of repayment was hanging in its own evidence vault, and the precedent for repaying a debt of time in light --- \emph{proved, in gold, over Gravamen} --- was already on the books, because we had put it there.

The Clerk wrote out the finding fair, twice, and stamped it --- with feeling, I would testify; the paw print came down like a bell itself --- and rose from his desk, and took his lamp, and personally led the way down the cross-aisle to the grille on its grey ribbon.

``I have processed irregularity all my death,'' he said, unlocking it. ``Permit me, once, to originate some.''

They rang it that night.

Not hoisted --- there was no time and no tower; the seam, my whole pelt could feel by then, was down to its last threads at the anchor; the fog stood in the streets at noon; the Drowned walked the Mereside terraces in numbers the day-side could no longer unsee. The Court rang it \emph{where it hung}, in the vault under everything, at the stroke where the ninth hour of the night had fallen silent a century ago --- the Clerk's own hand on the rope, two auditors metering, one cat present as petitioner of record ---

and the Ninth Bell of Hollowmere spoke for the first time in a hundred years, down under the city, up through the stones, out through every cellar and hearthstone and bone of the town: \emph{nine}.

I was already running for the water when the first stroke caught me. The city was behind me doing the thing I will spend the epilogue describing, and ahead of me, out past the sea-wall, at the far low line of the ebb, the black flats lay shining under the fog like a spilled mirror, and out on them, small at the anchor of the world, something person-shaped stood wearing the last of my witch's greatest seam, unpicking by the light it had paid half a century of half-seconds to reach.

It was the neap. Of course it was the neap. The mender comes at the neap.

\scenebreak

Tansy was at the sea-wall stair, planted across my path, orange and entirely certain. ``I am coming. I am fierce. I can bite fog.'' ``Mind the house, Tansy,'' I said, and she turned and ran for Needle Lane as if the house were a thing that could get away.

I went out over the flats alone, on the ninth bell's ninth stroke, and the fog opened me a lane the whole way, which I understood was not courtesy. The Mere wanted witnesses.

The Unpicked stood at the anchor-stitch as it had stood in her parlor: unhurried, absorbed, wearing its woven habit of stolen threads --- her wards, her walls, forty years of her work wound about an absence --- and its hands, which I could see now as I could not in the parlor, because now I knew what I was looking at, moved on the great seam \emph{mirror-wise}, right to left, lifting her stitches whole, in her order, run backward, each one releasing into the black water below with a sound like a word unsaid. So that was the lamps, the night she died: he had lit the lane mirror-order because the order is the stitch, and a round run backward is an unpicking --- her ward was off the lane before he ever reached the gate. The anchor was most of the way out. Under the flats, under the glass, all the way down, the patient one lay against the underside of the world with its whole weight, no longer hoping. \emph{Certain.}

I did not fight it. I want the record to be exact, because everything in this book has been walking to this stone: I came onto the last stretch of mirror-flat, and I sat down on the wet shining black in front of the person-shaped absence in its coat of my inheritance, in easy reach of the hands that had murdered my witch and defeated me at my own hearth, and I said what her books had taught me to say, in the words she had trusted me to find.

``The account was never closed on her side either. Not for one evening of forty years. The window was never only for me.''

The hands stopped.

The flats were very quiet. The fog stood in its ring. Down under the glass, something vast recalculated.

``She wrote it,'' I said. ``Forty years ago, the week after the water, in a box with your number on it. \emph{He is owed --- write it at the head of every page.} I have read every page. It is at the head of every page. And I am her heir, in law, adjudicated, to hold and to answer for --- and I have come to answer for it. Not to the lake.'' I looked up at where a face was not. ``To you. Bodkin.''

And the Unpicked came apart.

Not as a defeat. As an \emph{undressing}. The woven habit unwound itself from the top down, thread releasing thread, her wards sighing off it into slack coils on the shining black --- the garment he had worn to do the work of a creditor, taken off, at last, because he had been called by name to a settlement, and you attend a settlement as yourself --- and when it was done, standing on the flats in the ring of fog, on the last thread-bare stitches of the anchor, was a grey tom. Old. Big-boned under a coat that had seen forty years of weather. One eye. Half of one ear. The stillness of a creature that has spent a long time being exactly as visible as it chooses.

``You fight like a housecat,'' he said. The voice was the alley voice, unchanged, and it broke something in me that the parlor had not managed.

``I was one.''

``Were,'' he agreed. And then, for the first time in our acquaintance, he said something that was not load-bearing, and it came out of him like the anchor coming out of the seam: ``You were the only thing in that house I could not unpick. Fifteen years I watched her wind you. Nine lives, kit. Nine, on her own needle, out of her own evenings --- I sat on the sea-wall and \emph{watched the lamp burn} while she sewed you a fortune, and I told the water: you see. You see what she has. She could always make more. She only ever could not make \emph{mine} back.''

``She could not make yours back,'' I said. ``That is in the box too, in her own hand, blotted. \emph{Thread that has become stitch is not thread any more.} It is the first law of her whole craft and you know it better than I do; you have been unpicking her stitches all season and not one of them has come off the seam as a life. They come off as \emph{slack}.'' I put my paw on the coils of released warding lying dead on the black mirror between us. ``Look at it. This is what the lake has been paying you in promises. Spent is spent, Bodkin. Open the seam to the bottom and your eight lives do not come out --- nothing comes out but the thing that told you they would. It found someone with a grievance and it fed the grievance a refund that does not exist. It lied to you the way you have been not-lying to me: precisely, and to purpose.''

``To purpose,'' he agreed. ``Everything to purpose. All of it mine, and none of it small. Put it all on the bill.''

I put it on the bill. And then I did what a settlement requires, because I am her heir and her books are exact on the point: before anything is paid, the account is stated entire, aloud, by the party holding the pen. So I stated it.

``Elspeth Vane, stitcher of Hollowmere, unpicked in her own parlor at dusk. Before her, the two stitchers this city agreed to forget: the lady of the Tatterstage, who kept her seam under the greasepaint, and the second, whose file the Circuit closed with nobody looking. They were the only hands left in Hollowmere that could have re-anchored the seam once it opened, and you removed them decades ahead of the need, the way a professional removes the locks before the robbery.'' He did not correct me. ``The lamplighters' hall, emptied on the true word that there was work elsewhere, from the one voice that knew where elsewhere was. The roosts, drawn down over years, coat by coat, until missing looked like weather. Fenn, who walked into the fog while his lamp was still burning. The frame, hung on a hungry living neck so the Circuit would spend its season watching the living. The wisps on the lamplighters' round, taught the route and the taste of it. And under the whole of it, wound small and banked deep, forty years of stolen half-seconds off the stock of every soul in the city. That is the account.''

He did not flinch, and he did not dispute the total. I crossed a charge off my own page besides, one Merrow had laid on me at the hall door: the teacher of the wisps' route, found.

``There is a line missing,'' I said. ``The parlor. That first night was my death too, and the Court did not post the appointment. The line for the posting hand stands blank.''

``It stands blank because the hand was mine.'' The one eye did not move off me. ``I posted it myself, kit --- the gentlest slot on the whole ledger. You were going to spend that life on the wraith whatever any soul did. All there was left to choose was the desk you woke at, and I chose it.''

``I took the long way home.''

``And the eleven minutes bought you nothing. The work was done between the boiling and the scream. Do the sum you have been refusing.''

``And the missing?'' It came out of him fast, and I heard it for what it was: the last plank of a bridge he had been standing on for years. ``The coats. The roosts. The hall --- the hall walked out on its own feet; every soul in it had heard there was work elsewhere that night, and I told them where elsewhere was. I fed it \emph{down-payments}, kit --- its terms, not mine --- but they are held, not spent; they went down whole; I know held from spent, I have been both.'' The one eye was terrible. ``It gives them back when the seam opens. That is the contract.''

``The contract,'' I said, ``is void for fraud on the consideration. The lake cannot pay you the eight; the eight are the seam. Your refund was always going to be the city, Bodkin. Everyone's. That is all it had to sell, against the one thing it had spent forty years making sure you could never have. And you have known it all season, because you have carried the proof in your own teeth --- every stitch you lifted, coming off the seam as slack. I said it aloud once, on a sea-wall, to what I thought was fog. You heard it, and you have never once looked at it, because grief is the one auditor that refuses its own evidence.'' I stood up on the black glass. ``She knew. It is the last thing in the box. She could not give you your lives back, and she could not survive giving you the rest of hers, and so she did what she did with every debt she could not pay at the source. She paid it forward. Nine lives, made new, given to your kind --- \emph{whichever is dearer, mercy or justice} --- and then she sat in her chair at the end of an emptied day and heard your paws on the sill, and---''

``\emph{Don't},'' said Bodkin.

``---and put the kettle on,'' I said. ``For two.''

The fog ring stood. The bell's ninth stroke was long gone but the bronze of it was still in the stones, in the water, in the pads of my feet. And the oldest professional in Hollowmere, the fond ghost of my whole season, the murderer of Elspeth Vane, sat down on the mirror-flats in front of me the way Shear had sat down inside her own life, and said, in the alley voice, very evenly, the way you report a position:

``She said, `You'll take tea, then.' Forty years, and that is what she said. And I stood in her parlor with the night's work in my hands and I could not tell if it was surrender or welcome, and I have done the one thing since that I have never done in this city.'' The eye closed. ``I did not look. Four seconds, kit. Her own rule. I did not spend four seconds looking, because if it was welcome, I could not have collected, and I had made the water a promise that could not survive her pouring the tea.''

``It was both,'' I said. ``It was her hand. You knew her hand. It was always both.''

``It was always both,'' said Bodkin, and the last plank went out from under him, and he was only old, and grey, and one life deep, sitting on the black glass at the anchor of everything, mourning the woman he had murdered, forty years too late and one evening too early, with her heir and her kerchief three feet away and the drowned certainty of the Mere coming up under all of us like a page turning.

Because it came, then. Of course it came. It had heard the settlement it could not survive.

\scenebreak

The water did not rise; the \emph{flats} rose, the whole mirror flexing upward like the lid of an eye, and the ring of fog fell in on us from every side at once, and the Drowned came out of it arriving --- all of them, a city's century of them, dry-footed on the buckling glass --- and under the failing anchor the patient one put its whole weight on the last threads, no longer patient, and the seam began, stitch by stitch down its forty-year length, to \emph{sing} --- the sound her wards made dying in the parlor, scaled to a lake.

``It needs an anchor,'' I said. ``Now. A new one.'' I was already pulling the pale strength up through me, all of it, and the first spool out of my shadow, and I knew the price of an anchor; I had watched it paid at the corner of a dream --- and I had seven lives banked and a mirror-hand and her thread, and I stood on the flats and offered it, out loud, formally, the way the estate answers: ``Take mine. Seven. It held on eight; it will hold on seven; I will argue the difference with the lake personally---''

``Spent is spent,'' said Bodkin.

He said it quietly, and he was standing when he said it, and everything unhurried had gone out of him except the last thing, the craftsman's thing, the thing that had pulled me out of the watch's path and taught me the paws and mended my thread eleven times between audits.

``Your own law, kit. You just taught it to me on this very stone; do me the courtesy of losing an argument on it. An anchor wants a life that is \emph{finished paying}. Yours are new-made and owed forward --- she made them without owing and you will not owe them to this.'' He looked at the water. ``Mine has been finished for forty years. I kept it back out of spite, and spite is paid off. And---'' the one eye came around to me, and it had in it, at the end, the whole voice of the alley, dry, fond, appalling, ``---I have an appointment. First one in my life. Listen.''

Under the flats, through the stones, out of the deep vault of the city, the Ninth Bell of Hollowmere began the hour again --- the second ringing of the restored schedule, the ninth hour arriving on time for the first time in a hundred years --- and I understood, in the way you understand a route's last turn, what the Court had known when it stamped my petition with feeling. The bell does not only ring hours. It rings \emph{appointments}. A scheduled death, announced, seen coming a long way off: the one kind of arithmetic that has never once failed to balance at that desk.

``Tell the Clerk,'' said Bodkin, taking the needle-end of the anchor-thread up in his teeth the way we do, the way she taught us both from opposite sides of one lamp, ``that the walk-in apologizes for the filing.''

And the ninth stroke came down, and on it, mirror-handed, in her exact stitch run the true way round, the first familiar of Elspeth Vane sewed himself into the bottom of the world.

I will not describe the working, and not because I cannot. It was the reverse of everything I had watched at the corner of the dream: not eight lights pulled out of a standing shape, but one light, going down the needle willingly, into the seam, whole --- and the seam took it the way linen takes the last stitch of a shroud, and closed, and the singing stopped, and the whole length of her forty-year working pulled taut and true from the Wickhouse shore to the far fog with a sound like a kettle coming off the fire.

The flats settled. The Drowned, all across the mirror, stopped arriving --- stood a moment, the way a page stands upright before it falls --- and began, one by one, unhurried to the last, to \emph{leave}: back down into a lake that was, for the first time in a century, only water on top. Deep under the glass, the patient one went down and down and away from the underside of the world with its promises voided and its down-payments --- the Court's foreclosure went through that same night; the auditors had the papers drawn before the bell finished striking; I am told the Mere is the first defendant in the Hollow Court's history to be found in contempt \emph{in absentia, at the bottom of itself} --- with its down-payments ordered disgorged.

And on the black glass at the anchor of everything sat one cat, holding one strand of new grey stitching where an old grey tom had been, while the fog let go of the flats, and the ninth hour of the night came down over Hollowmere like a lamp being turned up, and out along the sea-wall, along the terraces, out of the fog and the years and the held dark under the water, the missing of the city began, in their coats and their feathers and their forty kinds of silence, to walk home.
