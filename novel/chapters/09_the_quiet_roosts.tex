\chapter{The Quiet Roosts}

The thread ran south-southwest, high and taut and tended, and I followed it the way you follow a river to the sea: knowing where it goes, going anyway.

I want to say a word about \emph{tended}, because it is the word that mattered. Threads slacken. Even great cables sag between their tyings, and a familiar-thread whose witch-end has gone silent should hang like washing-line in rain --- I know; mine does; I groom around it daily and we do not discuss it, my thread and I. This one was taut as a harp's business half across the city, and where the roofline winds had frayed it, it was \emph{mended} --- neat grey splices, close work, old-fashioned, at intervals of years. Somebody had been maintaining a dead line for four decades, the way you maintain a grave, or a grudge, or a hope.

It crossed the Shambles high over Brindle's stall. It crossed Wickrow within sight of Sootbeard's corner, and I wondered, passing, how many of its menders' crossings his log had never held. And then it began to descend, wide and slow, the way a gull descends, down the terraces of the Mereside, toward the water --- and it dipped, before the water, at the roosts.

The quiet roosts. I had been hearing the phrase all season the way you hear a dripping tap: the Mereside dovecotes, the oldest pigeon-fastness in the city, from which the Post's silence had never really lifted. Toby's seed, the bought election, the murder-night hush --- all of it had washed out of the upper city with the seed audit. Down here it had not washed out. Down here, I discovered that night, there was nothing left to wash.

The roosts were empty.

Not roosting-elsewhere empty. Empty the way the Lamplighters' hall was empty: coats over boots. Dovecote after dovecote, terrace after terrace, the little wooden cities of the pigeon-nation stood open to the fog --- droppings gone chalky with weeks, seed rotting in the throats of the feeders, nest-bowls holding cold eggs that no accounting anywhere will ever settle --- and over it all the deep wrong Mereside silence, which I now understood was not the absence of pigeon-noise but the \emph{presence} of something else: the hush you hear inside a bell after it has been stopped with a hand.

One bird remained.

He was old past flying, blind in both eyes, deaf --- I judged --- to everything but vibration, and he sat his empty rail in the greatest dovecote like a postmaster at a closed counter, and he was, I realized as I came up the ladder-holes making my footfalls heavy so as not to lie to him, the only creature in that whole city of the missing who had never once been asked for his testimony, because who would climb to ask it? A cat climbed. The last witness of the quiet roosts gave his deposition to a cat, feeling the questions through the wood of the rail.

What he gave me, in the pidgin of taps and stillnesses we built between us over an hour: the quiet had not come with the murder. The quiet had come up the chimneys \emph{years} ago, slow as damp --- first the dreams going out of the squabs, then the gossip going out of the elders, then, roost by roost, the going. Not flight. Going. \emph{Down.} They went to the water to be paid, he tapped --- I have turned the phrase every way and it does not improve --- they went to the water to be paid, and the payment was the quiet, and the quiet was already here.

The seed on the upper roofs bought a single night of silence from the living. Down here, the silence had been bought outright, and forever, whole roosts of the Post's oldest stock drawn down toward the black glass over years --- fed forward, the way the wax was fed, the way the coats' owners were fed --- to the account of the patient thing whose seam was loosening one stolen half-second at a time.

I left the old bird my second-to-last strip of dried herring, wedged where his rail meets the post. He stood on one foot upon it, which among the Post is the taking of salt, and he tapped one thing more, unasked, as I went down the ladder-holes: \emph{the mender comes at the neap}. The mender. Comes at the neap tide, when the water is furthest withdrawn and the flats lie bare. He had felt the mender's footfalls on the roost-boards for forty years, four small drumbeats, lighter than a gull's --- and the mender's visits and the goings-down, he wanted me to know, feeling my weight on his rail like a set of scales --- the visits and the goings had \emph{kept different calendars}. The mender tended the thread. Something else emptied the roosts. Two hands on the Mereside, the old bird was telling me, and he had outlasted them both, and could tell them apart blind, and nobody had asked.

I have thought since about how much of this book was given to me by witnesses everyone else had walked past: a rain barrel, a sleeping dog, a frost-pane, a blind bird on a dead rail. She used to say the city never stops testifying; there is only the question of who has the manners to take the deposition.

\scenebreak

It was the neap.

I had known it was the neap when I set out; the tide-tables are posted at the fish market and a cat who intends to trespass on the Mereside consults them. I tell myself now --- I told myself then --- that I went down to the flats because the thread's last descent crossed them, and the knot in its lowest slack could be read nowhere else. That was true. Everything I have ever told myself has been true. I have learned this season what truth is for, and I no longer make the mistake of confusing it with innocence: I went down to the flats at the neap because the mender came at the neap, and some part of me, older than the case and worse at arithmetic, wanted to \emph{see}.

The Mere at neap-ebb is a landscape from the underside of dreams: black flats shining like a spilled mirror, the fog withdrawn to a waiting distance, the sea-wall's weed-line hanging overhead like the tide's own high-water ledger. The thread came down out of the dark in one long grieving curve and ran its final length a paw's height over the flats, out past the last stone, toward the deep pool that never ebbs, where the seam --- I knew now, I had dreamed the sewing of it --- lay anchored in the bottom of the world.

And in its lowest slack, just past the last stone, there was a knot. I could see it from the wall. Close work. Old-fashioned.

I want the record to show that I saw the rest coming, and went anyway. This was not Gravamen's alley, not weather merely arriving. The fog stood off the flats in a horseshoe, waiting with the manners of an usher; the pools between the stones lay too still; the Drowned do not hide and were not hiding --- I counted five of them, spaced across the flats like pieces already moved, dry-footed on the wet mirror, arriving at nothing, which meant the arriving was for later, which meant it was for me. Every whisker, every professional instinct, every windowsill lesson said: this is the price of the reading. The appointment was announced, as the Clerk had promised. I saw it a long way off.

I paid it.

I went out over the flats fast and low, spending as I went --- the dark stretches taken soundless, the two gaps where arriving became arrival taken at a flat sprint that left my heart loud on the wrong side of my ribs, and at the cold places, where the mirror reached up through my pads like a hand, something of hers going out of me to meet it until the cold changed its mind --- and I reached the last stone, and I read the knot with my nose an inch from forty years of close grey splicing, and I learned the two things I had come to learn.

First: the knot was kin to the knots on my own thread. Not similar. \emph{Kin} --- the same hand's habit, the same tuck and doubling, the mend of a mender who learned mending in one particular lamplit parlor. I had carried its brothers at my own chest through every refusal at the Clerk's desk and never once been able to look at them squarely.

Second, and I had not come to learn it and it has never left me: a familiar-thread hums with its two lives, a chord. This one, an inch from my ear, hummed its single sustaining note, and the note was in \emph{her key}. Strung by her hand, tuned by her tying, the twin of the thread I wear. Whatever hung at the far end of this line, out past the last stone, under the black glass or over it, my witch had bound it to herself once, the way she later bound me --- and it had answered her working the way I answer, and its half of the chord was the half mine will make when I am gone.

There was a third thing. I had not come for it, and it did not wait to be asked.

A lamp came down the terraces behind me, swinging on a round that does not exist. I knew the walk before I knew the man --- a lamplighter's pace, steady as chalk-strokes --- and then I knew the man: Fenn, whose round Merrow rated the steadiest of her remaining four, the same lamplighter who had put his face in his hands the night I mended the hall's ward. He came down the sea-stair and out onto the mirror with his lamp lit and his eyes open and nothing behind them. I ran at him. I put myself under his boots and made every noise I own, and he stepped around me the way you step around furniture in a house you have lived in all your life, and set his lamp down on the mud, and walked into the horseshoe of fog, and the fog leaned in from three sides and folded him away standing. It was quick, and it was gentle, and it is the worst thing I have watched in this city. His lamp went on burning where he left it.

Then the fog spoke to me.

It has no voice of its own. It has never needed one. It holds the voices of everyone it has taken --- held, in escrow, unspent --- and it spent some now, one sentence at a time, each sentence in a different mouth.

``Stand aside,'' it said, in an old lamplighter's voice, the voice of a man whose route has walked him longer than he walked it.

``Let the seam open,'' it said, in a woman's voice with the roost-keeper's burr in it.

``And we walk home tomorrow,'' it said, in a child's voice. ``On the morning ebb. All of us.''

The offer was plain and I will report it plainly. Not power. Not my lives back. Not the case. The coats filled; the boots claimed; the roosts loud by noon; Merrow's chalk-tally ended at last; every missing name in this book walking up the terraces in one grey morning --- against one cat standing aside from one seam. It had priced me correctly. It did not offer me a single thing for myself.

I listened to every voice it spent, and the last voice was Fenn's --- five minutes held, and lent back already --- and Fenn's voice said, ``It is warm down here, cat.'' That was the only sentence in the whole transaction I believe was a lie.

And under the listening I did the one thing I am for. I audited. Voice after voice, mouth after mouth, the whole inventory of the city's held --- and one voice was not in it, and could not be in it, and was never going to be in it. The Mere returns what it holds; it holds the missing, whole, at the bottom of the world. She is not at the bottom of the world. She is nowhere a lake can reach. Dead is not held; spent is spent; it is her own iron law, and the lake, of all creditors, keeps it. If it could have said one sentence in her voice, I think it would have owned me. It could not, and it knew it could not, and so it stood there in its ring, holding out a city.

``No,'' I said.

Fenn's lamp burned on the mud between us, and I made myself look at it while I said the rest, because a refusal is cheap until you price it in other people's coats.

``I am her estate. The estate declines. The seam stays sewn. I will find another way to bring them up --- and if there is none, charge that to me. Not to her seam.''

The fog took the horseshoe apart without hurry. The voices went back into escrow. The lamp on the mud went out --- not blown; drunk --- and the dark that came down over the flats was the Mere being done with negotiating.

The tide does not run at the neap. The Mere is not the tide.

The water came up the flats in one silent folding, unhurried, total, like a page being turned by something reading in bed, and the five Drowned arrived all at once from five directions into the place where I was, and I had spent what I had crossing out, and the last thing I did with the last of my breath was the thing my whole training had been for: I looked. Four seconds. Longer. Down through the black turning glass toward the pool that never ebbs, where something vast lay against the underside of the world with the patience of owed money --- looking back up at me with what a lake has instead of a face, and \emph{hoping} --- and past it, at the edge, at the tactful corner of my own drowning, four small drumbeats on the mirror-flats, lighter than a gull's, going away.

The cold went. Then the noise. Then the part of me that was worried about the noise. The offer stayed.

\scenebreak

``Back so soon,'' said the Clerk. ``The form remembers you, which saves us both time.''

The desk. The lamp. The queue of one. This was no refusal and both of us knew it: the appointment had been posted on the flats in five standing figures and I had kept it with my eyes open. My second true death, of nine. The Toll was taken, and I will state plainly what it took, because it was not a quantity. The Clerk consulted the schedule, and the schedule named its fee, and the fee was a Remembered Day --- one, particular, itemized in the Court's short hand. The chart-table. The lamplit evening at the half-sewn wall-chart, me small and certain in the middle of her work, her voice above, amused, talking to the needle: \emph{That winter I tried eight strays.} It went across the desk whole, the way a coin goes: entirely, and to someone else. I keep the words --- they are in the chest-ledger, in the small hand, and notes are not the thing. The warmth of that table belongs to the Hollow Court now. He took it professionally, and almost gently, and neither helped. And the stamp with its paw-print came down on a page that did not say REFUSED, and the not-saying sat in the corridor's cold light like a stone.

Then came the measuring.

Part of the form of a true death --- I had not known; the first time, the night of the parlor, I had been past noticing --- is the thread-audit: the Clerk draws your thread out along the brass rule set into his desk's edge, reads its length against the ledger, and files the difference. He drew mine. He read it. He stopped.

He ran it back through his fingers, slowly, spectacles down, to the first of the small grey knots --- the mends I have carried since the refusals began, the knots I have groomed around and not discussed with myself --- and he examined it for a long time, and then the next, and the next, and I sat in the cat-sized chair (there is a cat-sized chair now; it appeared at the fifth or sixth visit; neither of us has ever mentioned it) and watched death's own clerk confront a document he could not file.

``There is work on this thread that is not ours,'' he said, at last. ``I have noted it before. I have written CLERICAL ERROR, once, and I do not write fiction, as a rule.'' He looked at me over the spectacles. ``Close work. Old-fashioned. Whoever ties these does not care to have them noticed --- and they are \emph{mends}, sir. Someone has been re-tying you. Your arithmetic will not balance because it is being balanced from outside, between my audits, by a hand that pays nothing in and takes nothing out and appears in no schedule of this Court.'' He laid the thread down with the exaggerated evenness of a man mastering an emotion unbecoming to his office. ``Your file is the thickest irregularity below this city, and it is attracting \emph{attention}, and I find---'' he stopped, and turned the lamp up a degree, which I had learned by then to read as the outer limit of his tenderness, ``---I find I no longer altogether mind. Go carefully. That is not in the form.''

He was not quite done. He turned the ledger back to the season's first page, and laid one translucent finger on an entry there, and left it a moment without comment. ``One thing more, sir. Your first death. The parlor. It kept an appointment --- and this Court did not post it. The line for the posting hand is blank.'' He looked at the blank the way he had looked at the knots. ``I have kept this desk a long time. I have never once seen that line blank.''

He walked me out himself, that visit. Down the corridor of shelves --- and it was in the corridor, at a cross-aisle I had passed on every visit and never once turned down, that the whole third part of this book announced itself, because something in the dark of the cross-aisle \emph{breathed}.

One long bronze note, below hearing, felt in the floor: the sound a great bell makes in its sleep, years after the last time it was rung, when a footfall crosses its vault. I stopped. The Clerk stopped. Down the cross-aisle, behind a grille, on an evidence-shelf built to a scale the rest of the shelving only rumors, lay a bell the size of a parlor --- green-shouldered, dust-shrouded, its docket hanging from its crown on grey ribbon like a toe-tag.

``Ah,'' said the Clerk. ``Yes. The Ninth.''

He said it the way you name a colleague's old scandal. And standing in that corridor, freshly dead for the second time, with my thread full of another creature's mercy and my chest full of black water, I received at last the answer to the first wrong thing I ever noticed --- eight bells from the tower, and no ninth any more --- and the answer was: \emph{pawned}. The ninth bell of Hollowmere was pawned to the Hollow Court a century ago, by the city itself, in council, under seal --- collateral against a plague-season's worth of unpaid Tolls --- and never redeemed. The docket is open to this day. And since the night they hauled it down, said the Clerk, in the tone of a man quoting a finding he had signed and not liked, the city's nights have run one hour short: the ninth hour of the dark was rung by the ninth bell, and an hour that is never rung is never \emph{kept}, and so it pools down here with the rest of the unclaimed. Nobody in Hollowmere has dreamed their last dream to the end in a hundred years. Every sleeper in the city wakes one hour early and calls it morning, and the ninth bell sleeps in an evidence vault with the whole unspent hour of the city banked in its bronze, accruing.

``It did not only ring the hours,'' the Clerk added, in the tone of a footnote. ``The hours kept the appointments.''

The working of forty years ago had rung nine bells; I had heard them in the Remembered Day, and the ninth had been rung \emph{out of this vault} --- signed out, the docket showed, for one night, on the Circuit's surety, for the great sewing; and returned. The last time it spoke. Her seam was rung shut on a pawned bell's borrowed voice.

``The docket remains unresolved,'' said the Clerk, and dusted nothing off the grille's brass. ``The Court does not forgive collateral, and the city has never once petitioned for redemption. It would take---'' and he permitted himself the thing that in the Clerk stands where other men keep smiling, a small dry ceremonial pause, ``---a paperwork error. There is always a paperwork error. Locating it, however, is a profession.''

He looked at me. I looked at the bell.

The stairs up are longer than the fall down. They always are. I climbed them that night with the case entire on my back --- three suspects on the board, a fourth in the log, and my one unwritten name under it; a method forty years old that spends lives for half-seconds; a thread in her key running out past the last stone; a mender who comes at the neap and re-ties a dead witch's cat between audits; the missing, the wax, the roosts, all draining down one slope toward a patient hope under black glass --- and the shape of the third part of this book assembling itself in me stair by stair, out of a clerk's dry hint and a bell's held hour.

The Mere is fed on the missing and the quiet, and it is winning, softly, one unpicked seam at a time. Against a thing like that, claws are a rumor. \emph{Paperwork} is an appointment.

Tansy was asleep on the hearthrug when I came in, in the exact center of it, compassed by one chewed bootlace; she had waited up, the room said, and lost. Under the door, in Brindle's counting-hand, was the answer to a question I had paid her to put about before I went down: where the senior grey of the Court of Whiskers deputation had passed the night of the neap. \emph{Nowhere that paper reaches} --- not at the Ratsmeet, not at any roost the Exchange prices. It did not clear him, and I left him in the log, off the board, and the leaving was not free.

I stood a while at the board on the chimney breast, and then I took a pin, and under the whole assembly of the case --- the stub, the docket, the counterfoil, the blank card, the shroud-thread, the grey wool running toward Gravamen --- I pinned the one new exhibit I had carried up the long stairs, which was a sentence, mine, in the small honest hand:

\emph{The city is owed an hour, and I know where it is banked.}

Then that is where we are going.
