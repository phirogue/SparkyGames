\chapter{The Quiet Gentleman}

Gravamen is where the city keeps everything it would rather not look at, which is why it is the best-looking district in Hollowmere.

The undertakers have money, and taste, and no neighbors who complain. Black brick, white trim, brass fittings with no fingerprints on them. The Horological Society keeps its tower there, running four minutes slow. The Undertakers' Circle keeps its quiet yards. And below it all, in a sense that is not quite geography, Gravamen is Court-adjacent: the Hollow Court's day-side face, the place where the paperwork of endings is drafted before it is filed in the building under everything. Debts drain to Gravamen the way water drains to the Mere. It is the city's low place for owing.

I did not arrive well. The arrangement I have with the reader is total honesty, and what happened on the way matters to the file.

The rag-wraiths took me at the boundary of the district, where the fog off the canals meets the crematory warmth and makes a weather with opinions. Three of them --- three; I had never heard of two together, and here were three working as a \emph{set}, a wardrobe of the missing: a drover's coat, a housemaid's apron still tied at the waist of nobody, and a child's sunday jacket that I will not describe further because some inventory belongs to no file. The seam of the city was loosening --- Cardew had said it, Merrow's lamps said it, and here it was said again in worsted and calico: more missing, more empty clothes walking, the Hush --- the old binding that keeps the city's two sides politely strangers --- fraying from the middle outward.

I fought well. Not vanity; calibration: I fought better than the cat who died in the parlor by an order of magnitude, and it was not enough, because three is not one, and the child's jacket fought \emph{wrong} --- small and quick and heedless, the way its owner must have been --- and while I answered it the drover's coat came down on me like weather off a hill.

The cold went. Then the noise. Then the part of me that was worried about the noise.

The corridor. The filing. The desk. The Clerk was already shaking his head.

``You are not on the docket.'' He held the sheet up, translucent fingers, that winter-sounding pen laid down beside it. ``No appointment, no death. We do not process walk-ins.''

There was one chair in front of the desk. I sat in it. There had been three of them, I said. The child's jacket alone---

``A defeat off the docket spends no life,'' said the Clerk. ``You will be sent back up, minus a filing fee and whatever your satchel held. A \emph{true} death is announced. You will know the appointments when you keep them.''

He took the fee --- a measured pinch of my banked shine. Then he drew my thread out across the desk for the filing. Partway along it he stopped. There was a knot in the thread, small and grey, close-worked. He was still a moment too long. Then he took up the pen, wrote two words in the margin of my file, and laid it down again. I read the words upside down: CLERICAL ERROR.

He brought the stamp down. In all that quiet it was very loud.

The stamp said REFUSED. It was still a paw print.

``The gutter two streets east of where you fell drains poorly,'' he said, as I left, without looking up. ``If you are quick, the satchel will still be there. Your file is becoming irregular.''

The stairs up are longer than the fall down. They are no shorter for the practice.

\scenebreak

I retrieved the satchel --- the Clerk's drainage intelligence was, like everything at that desk, exact --- and I sat on a Gravamen roof in the last of the night, putting my coat and my nerve back in order, and that is when I saw the third thread.

Understand what the sky of this city looks like to me by now. Threads everywhere, always: the day-side's ten thousand small tethers, and over them the great slow cables of the guilds, and here and there, rare as comets, a \emph{familiar-thread} --- the deep silver line that runs between a witch and her bound creature, the kind I wore for fifteen years, the kind that hangs cut at my chest with one strand left. You cannot mistake one for anything else. It has a note in it. It hums with the two lives it joins, and the hum is a chord.

Over Gravamen, strung high between the Horologists' slow tower and the fog, ran a familiar-thread humming a chord of one.

Not mine. Mine hangs at my own chest; I know its weight the way you know your own heartbeat. Not hers. Hers I would know under floodwater, and hers ended, cut clean, over the black glass of the Mere. A third: old silver gone the grey of long weather, taut, tended, \emph{alive} --- and with no witch on the end of it. I sat very still on that roof and followed it with my eyes as far as eyes would go: south and west, out over the terraces, toward the water. A familiar-thread with one voice in the chord. A bound creature whose witch does not answer, and whose thread has not been cut.

The ledger in my chest opened to the page with the two nines on it, and I did not write the line between them, and my not-writing it had begun to feel like holding a door shut with my back.

There was a knot in the thread's far slack, I thought --- too far to be sure, a small close-worked interruption in the line, old-fashioned --- and while I stared at it, dawn came up over Gravamen, and somewhere below me a door I had not noticed opened, and stayed open, in the manner of a door that is \emph{for} you.

I had come to find the Quiet Gentleman. I should have known better than to think finding him was a thing I would do, rather than a thing that would be done to me.

\scenebreak

His office is not hidden. That is the first thing to report about it: no cellar, no password, no crooked stair. A tall black-brick house on Gravamen's best square, brass plate polished blank --- blank, like the card --- door ajar at the exact angle of expectation. Inside, a hall smelling of fog and beeswax --- \emph{beeswax}; item --- and a long room of shelves that I mistook, for one honest heartbeat, for the Hollow Court's, because they were filled floor to ceiling with folders tied in ribbon.

Not grey ribbon. Every color. Every color a promise is made in.

He was at the window, looking out at the square, and I want to do for the reader what nobody managed to do for me, which is describe him. I cannot. Neither can anyone else; I have since asked rats, magpies, gargoyles, and a ghost. He is a gentleman, and he is quiet. There is a coat, of good cloth; there are gloves. Above the collar the eye slides, politely, the way it slides off other people's grief. No one remembers meeting him for the first time, and I --- this is the part I can least explain --- I cannot remember whether that morning \emph{was} my first time. The room was strange to me and my paws knew the floor.

``The witch's cat,'' he said. His voice is the sound a signature would make. ``No. My error. The Needle's \emph{heir}.'' He turned from the window. ``You have been spending well: a magpie's regard, a lamplighter's knot, an old mother's debt, a guildmaster ruined into gratitude. And one purr, recovered against my house's paper.'' Nothing in his tone accused. Accusation is settlement's first cousin, and settlement is the one thing that starves him. ``You are collecting, little rival. It is well done. I say so professionally.''

``I have questions.''

``You have \emph{payment},'' he corrected, gently, and gestured, and I understood the gesture was the room's whole law. On a reading-table by the window lay a single folder, untied, and beside it, in a dish, exactly the space where a favor-knot would sit.

What I did next I set down in the way my witch would pass on review, so: I looked first. Four seconds and more. The folder was the counterfoil's twin --- his own file of the transaction, the quiet channel's other end --- and the price in the dish's emptiness was one knot, unspecified, \emph{his choosing later}. That is his whole game in one dish: not the knot you give him, but the not-knowing which. I thought of Tansy's contract, of the old ones on the ropewalk roof: \emph{he never signs anything that can be settled; read it before you touch it.} And I thought: a cat has one thing a gentleman structurally cannot have, which is the option of walking away from a table with something already on it.

So I paid him nothing, and I asked my questions anyway, and here is the strangest entry in this whole strange file: \emph{he answered them}. Freely. Precisely. I have come to believe the folder on the table was never the offer; the offer was the conversation, and the conversation was bait of another kind. The Gentleman does nothing that is not collection.

What he told me, with the fog pressing its face to his good windows:

That yes: the night's arrangements had come through his house. Three favors, called blind, brokered without asking --- ``the not-asking, as the Guildmaster told you, is what the quiet costs; I heard him say it; I hear most things said in rooms I have papered.'' A cart-slot from the Chandlers. A scattering of seed by a human hand --- ``the hand was human. Its debt was not.'' I sat with that sentence while the clock in his hall ticked four minutes behind the world, and it opened like a lattice: some day-sider, indebted to a night-side creditor, worked off the debt with an evening's honest labor, hauling seed to a roofline. The debtor human; the debt's \emph{owner} something else.

``And the third favor?'' I asked.

``The third favor,'' said the Gentleman, ``emptied a hall.''

The room was very quiet. Even his clock seemed to owe its ticks somewhere.

``I do not know where the Lamplighters went,'' he said, and I believed him --- I, who read rooms for a living, believed him, and I was right to, and being right about him was somehow worse. ``I brokered the calling of a debt the hall owed --- old oil, older gratitude; guilds are made of such stuff --- and the form of the payment was: \emph{be elsewhere, all of you, from seven bells}. Where elsewhere turned out to be was no part of the paper. My clients' purposes are their own. I am a channel, cat. Water does not ask.''

``Water,'' I said, ``is what the drowned say too.''

Something happened then that I have no language for: the place where his face is \emph{considered} me. ``Yes,'' he said, at length, approvingly, as if I had correctly totaled a column. ``You will want to know about my client.''

I had not asked. Asking would have been payment --- eagerness is a coin; he mints it. He told me regardless, and every sentence had the weight of a thing given for a reason I could not see. That is the only way he gives anything:

``I never met them. I meet everyone, little rival; I met you before you were worth meeting. This client I have never met. The instructions arrived as instructions sometimes do in my trade: a knot, tied overnight, in my own ledger's binding-thread --- inside my own house --- with the commission written in the tying. Close work.'' He paused, and the pause was itself close work. ``Old-fashioned. I have not seen its like since I was a much smaller concern.''

The Clerk's voice, unbidden, from a refusal I had not yet had and a night I had already had: \emph{whoever ties these does not care to be noticed.} Two desks, both in the debt trade, one knot-hand neither could file.

``And the payment?'' I asked. ``What does a client with no face pay a house like this?''

The Gentleman crossed to his shelves, drew out a shallow drawer that had no business fitting between two others, and set its contents on the reading-table beside the untouched folder: a spool. Small, plain, wound with thread of a grey I knew and my whole body did not want to know --- the grey of the shroud-seam, the grey of tide-taken things --- and wound \emph{tight}, years and years of it.

``Time,'' he said. ``Stored. Spent-down life, wound and banked. It is not a currency I deal in twice; it does not settle, it only \emph{ends}, and ending is against the interests of my house. But a channel takes the toll the water carries.'' He looked at the spool the way Wick had looked at the thread on his blotter. ``There are perhaps four beings in Hollowmere who could wind such a spool. I have made inquiries. Three of them are dead, and one of the three is your witch --- and the winding is not her hand. It is her hand's \emph{mirror}.''

He set the spool a half-inch further from the folder, as if the two accounts should not touch. ``Price the stock, little rival, as I priced it when it crossed my table. Nothing living winds its own life and survives the winding; what is on that spool was not saved. It was taken. Taken small: a gargoyle's blink, a street's held breath, the quiet between a bell and its echo --- half-seconds nobody misses, gathered over decades, wound tight, and banked. That is the stock. Under the stock, an advance: something in this city holds lives in escrow, and it has been extending credit against its holdings. My client did not ask. Water does not ask.''

\scenebreak

I walked home the long way, over the roofs of three districts, because there was too much in the satchel of my chest and walking is how a cat files.

Entered into the case, that morning, in order:

That the murder night was purchased entire --- silence, transport, and an emptied hall --- through the city's one channel that never asks, by a client even the channel has not seen.

That the client pays in wound-down life: years off a spool, the coin of the Toll itself, the one currency in Hollowmere that cannot be earned honestly by anything that means to go on living. The Gentleman's phrase, in my ear the whole way home, keeping time with my feet: \emph{it does not settle, it only ends.}

That the client's knot-work is close, old-fashioned, mirror-handed --- at the Gentleman's ledger, at my own thread across five refusals I had not fully counted, at a warehouse wall on the Mere-edge. One hand, everywhere, and every place it touched, a professional shrugging: \emph{not one of ours.}

That over Gravamen hums a familiar-thread with one voice in its chord, running toward the water, and I had not followed it, and I told myself it was because the case comes before the curiosities, and the ledger of my chest recorded instead, in the small honest hand it keeps for entries against myself, that I had been \emph{afraid} of where it went.

And that the Quiet Gentleman was now, formally, a suspect --- the second strong name on a board that had just lost Wick --- and that he knew it, and found it fitting, and had all but pinned himself to my board with his own gloved hands. Guilty of brokering, unasking, of profiting on the night my witch died: certainly. Guilty of the needle-work itself? A being made entirely of open accounts does not spend the one coin that closes them. But a suspect is not crossed off on elegance. He stays, I wrote, until the method rules him out.

The method. Everything now ran through the method: who in this city can spend life like money and stitch like her mirror. I needed to date the technique --- how long had that hand been working? --- and for dating old crimes, Hollowmere keeps exactly two archives that never lie. One is under everything, and charges a toll.

The other has been standing on a corner of Wickrow for three hundred years, and it had, I had heard from the pigeons, recently done something unprecedented.

It had blinked.
