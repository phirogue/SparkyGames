\chapter{The Gargoyle and the Ghost}

Sootbeard has watched one street for three hundred years, and last month, he blinked.

You will want the context, because the day-side walks under him daily and sees a rainspout. Sootbeard is the gargoyle on the corner of Wickrow's oldest counting-row: granite shoulders, a beard the soot of thirty thousand chimney-seasons, and the fixed downward glare of a sentry whose watch has never once been relieved. He was installed --- he is precise about the word; he did not \emph{perch}, he was \emph{installed}, by masons, with ceremony, under an ordinance --- as one of a regiment of civic monuments raised to watch the streets of Hollowmere in the years when the city still remembered what it was watching for. The regiment was decommissioned in 1731. Nobody told him. Or rather --- and this is the detail that tells you everything about both parties --- somebody \emph{did} tell him, and he declined the order as improperly countersigned, and stayed on watch.

Three hundred years, one street, no relief, no pay, and no blink. Gargoyles do not need to blink; the eyes are granite; the watching is the working. And one night last month, between one lamplighter's lamp and the next, Sootbeard blinked --- his first in three centuries --- and in the half-second of that blink, something crossed his street unobserved.

He wants it understood that he noticed the blink itself. A lesser monument, he says, would not have.

I heard of it through the pigeon-net, which had resumed operations with the zeal of the reformed and was carrying, among the usual scandal, a strange small item: the gargoyle on Old Counting Row was attempting to file a report, and had been attempting for a month, and there was no one left in the city authorized to receive it.

I went and received it.

I sat on his ledge through a whole turning of the fog while he delivered it, in the voice gargoyles keep --- gravel settling in a well --- and in the form he had held ready for a month, which was the form of his commissioning, sworn and structured: item, the night; item, the wind; item, the state of the lamps (wrong, he noted, lit wrong for weeks now; he had logged each error; I could have wept for the wasted vigilance of him); item, at four minutes past seven bells by the Horologists' tower --- \emph{which runs four minutes slow, adjust accordingly} --- an interference; a blink; duration estimated at one half-second; contents of street upon reopening of watch: unchanged; contents of street during closure: \emph{unknown}, and the word came out of him like a stone breaking, because in three hundred years there had never been an unknown in his log.

``Something made me blink,'' he said. ``Nothing makes granite blink, small colleague. Nothing on the schedule of this world. It was not weariness. I do not tire; I erode, and I have three centuries of margin. Something reached into my watching and \emph{spent} it --- half a second of my attention, taken and used, the way one takes a coin from a blind man's cup. I wish to report a theft.''

A theft of attention. Half a second, lifted clean out of a sentry three hundred years incapable of lapse, on the street his watch made otherwise uncrossable --- and the night of it, I confirmed against my ledger with the cold going down my spine like meltwater, was the murder night. Old Counting Row lies on the clean route between Gravamen's quiet channels and Needle Lane. Whoever went to my witch's parlor went through Sootbeard's watch, and paid the crossing with a coin that buys \emph{inattention}, and the coin, I now knew from a gentleman's shelf-drawer, was wound-down life. You do not dodge a gargoyle. You purchase his blink.

``Your report is received,'' I said, and I meant it as a courtesy, and it landed on him like relief of watch. Three hundred years, and the first receipt. He asked to whom he should henceforth report. I gave him the truth: to me, for the present, in the matter of the murder of Elspeth Vane. He asked my rank. I considered the file, and the collar, and the strand, and I said: \emph{the Needle's heir, investigating}. Sootbeard saluted --- granite grinding on granite, a fist to a brow, dust of three centuries sifting down --- and he has saluted me at every meeting since, and I have stopped trying to prevent it, because you cannot argue with an ordinance.

\scenebreak

But a blink has no witness, by construction. That is what it is \emph{for}. So we reconstructed it, Sootbeard and I, from the three witnesses on Old Counting Row that do not have eyelids.

His idea, not mine. Three hundred years of watching teaches you what else watches.

The first witness was the rain barrel at Number Nine. Standing water remembers ripples the way wax remembers heat --- poorly, but honestly --- and the Weft-side of a barrel's surface holds the pattern of its last true disturbances in a lattice a patient reader can lift. I read it by moonlight, paws on the rim, while Sootbeard recited the month's weather from memory to rule out the innocent rings, storm by storm, cart by cart, drunk by drunk. Under all of it, dated to his blink by the interference-fringe of a lamplighter's footfall he could vouch for: one set of ripples with no source. Rings spreading from a center that held \emph{nothing} --- something had passed the barrel closely, at speed, its wake writing on the water while its weight declined to exist. Small, the wake said. Low. Four-footed.

I wrote it in the ledger. The ledger and I did not discuss it.

The second witness was the dog. There is always a dog --- Number Fourteen, a wall-eyed old mastiff who sleeps in the areaway and has barked at the night complete and without editorial judgment for eleven years, a colleague of my Number Twelve friend by correspondence of profession. He did not bark at the crossing --- of course --- but a sleeping dog dreams its street, and dreams keep drafts of what the waking mind was robbed of. I sat with him --- this is a thing cats can do, and I will not be explaining it to a species that cannot; it is called listening, and you sit \emph{downwind of the dream} --- and in the draft of that night his street ran quiet and complete, and through it went a shape his dream had tried to bark at and could not, because every time the dream-bark rose, the shape spent something, and the bark \emph{was already over}. The dream remembered the shape the way sleep remembers a fly: an interruption with a silhouette. The silhouette had a tail.

The third witness was the frost.

It was the window of the old assay office, disused, north-facing, the kind of glass that grows the same fern-garden of frost every cold night of its life --- and frost-ferns, Sootbeard testified, grow by the breath of the street: every warm exhalation that passes bends the crystals' habit by a hair. On the murder night it had frozen hard. The pane held, pressed in its ferns like a flower in a book, the record of every breath that had gone down Old Counting Row --- the lamplighter's, the mastiff's slow tide, a late clerk's gin-warm mutter --- and one passage of \emph{no breath at all}: a corridor through the frost-garden where the ferns grew bent around a warmth that had come and gone between two of their branchings, faster than breathing, low to the ground, unbreathed because its bearer had --- I understood this in my own chest, with a jolt like a misjudged jump --- \emph{held its breath}. As you do. As I do, crossing a watched street, out of a professionalism older than any regiment.

Small. Low. Four-footed. Tailed. Breath held for the crossing.

Sootbeard ground through the same arithmetic beside me, three centuries of watch-craft arriving at the same address, and being Sootbeard, he filed it as a sentry files:

``Parameters, small colleague: small; low; four-footed; tailed; breath held for the crossing. There the report ends. My watch was closed for the duration of the interference. A sentry files what he saw, and I saw nothing. I do not log conclusions my eyes were shut for.''

And he stopped, correct to the last grain of him, leaving a silence on the street where the conclusion belonged.

``A thing that has learned to travel as one,'' I said, first, into that silence --- and the ledger of my chest recorded, in the small hand it keeps for entries against myself, that I had reached for that sentence the way a falling man reaches for a gutter, before anyone had said the word I was falling from.

\scenebreak

The residue settled the method; now the method needed a date. And for dating, Sootbeard had one more service, and it cost me what remained of my composure.

The reconstruction wanted checking against the one other recorded blink in the city's history --- for there was one; he knew his regiment's logs entire; monuments gossip on a timescale of decades --- a blink reported by the gargoyle formerly installed over the Tatterstage door, forty years ago, the night the theater's leading lady died mid-line at her own opening. That gargoyle was decommissioned with the rest and sold for hardcore --- crushed for road-fill --- when the Tatterstage closed; his log survives only in Sootbeard's memory. To compare fringe-patterns --- his word; I have given up resisting his vocabulary --- Sootbeard needed to \emph{attend the site}, and Sootbeard cannot fly. He was installed.

Which is how the district of Wickrow was treated, at two of the morning, to the spectacle of a civic monument walking to the theater district --- roof-line to roof-line where the timbers would bear him, chimney to chimney like a man fording a river on stones --- carrying, in the granite basket of his two cupped fists, held at parade height before him like the regimental colors:

me.

I had explained that I would make my own way. I had explained it twice. Sootbeard had replied that the route bore no relation to a cat's route, that the timing was operational, and that the colleague would be \emph{conveyed} --- and this must be said exactly: being carried is an indignity I have tolerated from one person in my life, and she is dead, and I sat in a gargoyle's fists above the sleeping city with the fog going by underneath and the chimneys bowing past like courtiers, and I found that the indignity had somewhere in fifteen years become a thing I missed the way you miss a language. I rode at parade height the whole way and said nothing and my dignity and I have agreed the file may show a redaction here.

The Tatterstage stands --- barely --- off the old theater square: boarded, bird-limed, its gilt gone the color of weak tea. Its door-gargoyle's empty plinth remains over the architrave like a healed socket. Sootbeard set me down on it, took post on the parapet opposite with the grinding of a fortress settling, read the sight-lines of his lost colleague for a long silent time, and pronounced: same fringe. Same theft. Same coin, same hand, same \emph{crossing-craft}, forty years apart.

``Your suspect,'' he said, ``has been stealing half-seconds in this city since before you were littered, small colleague. Report continues below.'' A pause; granite eyes on the boarded door. ``The house is not empty. It auditions.''

\scenebreak

Every night at the wrong hour, the Tatterstage holds auditions for a play that closed forty years ago. Nobody gets the part.

I went in through the fly-gallery, and I will tell you what the inside of a dead theater is: it is a parlor the size of a guild-hall where the kettle has been screaming for forty years. Dust in curtains of its own; the great chandelier a cocooned moon in its hoist-bag; and on the stage, lit by nothing and visible the way the drowned are dry, a lady in a costume of a fashion two reigns gone, running a scene against the empty house.

The Understudy. That is the name the night-side gives her, with the tenderness this city keeps for its worst-luck cases: she was the understudy who got her chance --- one night, the lead's throat closed by fog, the house full, her name chalked on the board --- and she died on that stage mid-line, at her own opening, and she cannot leave until the scene \emph{ends correctly}. That is the shape of her stitch, the loose thread of her: a line unfinished. The Hollow Court could tie her off --- it is exactly their trade --- but she has declined the service, forty years of politely declining, on the professional grounds that the scene is not over.

And she cannot finish it, because she remembers her death wrong, and rehearses the error.

Nightly she runs the scene toward her exit; nightly, at the fatal line, she directs her scene-partner --- whatever shadow or draft or auditioning ghost is to hand --- into the wrong blocking, the mistake her memory has enshrined; nightly the scene breaks, and the house sighs, and she begins again. She has directed forty years of auditioners into her own error. She quotes only from plays --- it is all the speech she has left; a whole language of borrowed lines, and she wields it with terrible precision --- and when I first came down the fly-rope she looked at me across the dark house and said, from some old comedy: \emph{``Enter the cat, which is more than the management deserves,''} and I was hers from that line onward, and I do not care who knows it.

The true blocking still existed, in three places. (Everything in this city exists in three places; I have stopped finding it strange; Hollowmere is a fabric, and fabric keeps its creases.) The printed programme of the fatal production, with the scene-plot, survived in the collection of a retired prompter --- ninety-one years old, sharp as vinegar, resident in a nursing-house whose matron enforces a no-cats ordinance with the zeal of a woman promoted past her judgment; I will say only that ordinances have gaps, that laps were sat in, that the prompter talked to me for two hours the way the lonely talk to cats, reciting the blocking of a forty-year-old tragedy from memory while I purred --- Tansy's purr has re-taught me the trade --- and that the matron found nothing amiss but a faint warmth on an old man's knees.

The chalk ghost-marks were still under the boards of the Tatterstage itself --- the stage manager's original blocking marks, preserved under forty years of dust like the frost-ferns in the assay glass. And the third witness was the Understudy's own body: a performer's feet remember what her grief has rewritten, if you can get her past the place where the memory flinches.

So we performed it. She and I. Cue by cue.

I cannot act --- a cat is sincerity armed --- but her scene-partner's part, in the fatal scene, is silent: it enters, it crosses under the practical lamp, it stands where the light falls, and it \emph{receives her line}. Forty years of auditioners had been directed by her, at the crux, to cross behind the lamp --- her error, her enshrined mistake --- and the scene died there each night because behind the lamp there is no light to die into, and a line delivered into darkness lands on nothing.

I took my blocking from the chalk and the prompter, not from her.

She swept toward the crux; her whole habit turned to direct me into the error --- \emph{``No, no, upstage, the shadow, always the shadow''} in a line from some play I hope never to identify --- and I stood where the chalk said. Under the lamp. Downstage. In the light.

And her line found me there, and landed, and I saw forty years of rehearsal come apart in her face like the wisp-lattice --- because with the blocking true, she was standing where \emph{she} had stood, saying what she had said, seeing what she had seen, and what she had seen was not what she remembered. The scene ended. Correctly. For the first time.

She stood a long moment in the practical lamp's light, a lady two reigns out of fashion, unpicked at last from her own error, and before the Court's gentle machinery could arrive to tie her off she looked down at me --- her final audience, her one correct scene-partner --- and gave me her exit line. Her own. Not borrowed. The only unquoted sentence, I believe, that she had spoken in forty years, and the reader of this file will understand that I would rather report almost anything else, because I had come there for evidence, and I have never since been able to decide what I received:

``It was not the fog that stopped his breath, little one,'' she said. ``The fog was \emph{bought}. I saw the half-second come into the house --- I was in the wings, where you see everything --- I saw it cross under my lamp, low, and take the prompt-breath out of the night, and my line went out into a silence that had been \emph{purchased}, and the house heard my heart refuse, and I have been forty years blaming my own feet.''

Then, quieter, as if handing over a property she had held for the run of the show: ``And write into your file what the bills never carried, little one: my lady was a stitcher. She kept a seam under the greasepaint --- silver, at the hairline; I painted it over every night and asked no questions, because an understudy sees everything and says nothing.''

She straightened. Curtsied to the empty house, to the cocooned chandelier, to me.

``Tell them the notice was wrong,'' she said. ``The performance did not fail. It was \emph{robbed}. And so, small colleague of the wings --- so were you.''

She took her bow. The house lights of some better theater came up for her, somewhere I could not follow, and the Tatterstage was, at last, only a building.

\scenebreak

I sat on the empty stage a long time with the practical lamp cooling beside me, doing the sum the whole chapter had assembled, and it would not come out any way but one.

The technique was forty years old. The lead whose throat closed --- a stitcher, one of Cardew's \emph{twice before}; \emph{the fog was bought}; the half-second under the lamp; a death arranged so that an understudy's heart would break on schedule --- the hand that murdered my witch had been working in this city for forty years, patient, close, mirror-handed, spending life like small coin. Forty years. The Circuit's rival witches, the guild's men, the whole day-side gallery of plausible suspects --- I ran them through the new arithmetic that night and watched them fall out of it one by one, too young, too new, too \emph{mortal}. What in Hollowmere is forty years at a craft and still working? Witches, a few. Gargoyles. Ghosts. The Gentleman, whose house is older than the question.

And cats, said the ledger, in the small hand, are allotted nine lives, and I have stood in the Clerk's corridor six times this season --- once on the docket, five times REFUSED, a distinction he maintains and I have stopped disputing --- and am writing this book.

The ledger, having opened that page, would not shut it politely. There is a grey at the Court of Whiskers --- the senior of the deputation that serves our papers, oldest of the old ones on the ropewalk roof --- a tom of the right vintage, grown and working forty years ago. I entered him. I hated every stroke of the entry, and I said nothing to anyone.

Something crossed a sentry's street shaped as a cat. Something took the prompt-breath out of a theater, low, under the lamp. Something taught wisps a route, and travels the overstreets as if born to them, and cuts a familiar-thread with one clean bite, and ties close old-fashioned knots that neither death's clerks nor debt's gentleman can file --- and over Gravamen, humming toward the water, hangs a familiar-thread with one voice in the chord, and I had not followed it, and I was running out of ways to tell myself why.

Forty years ago, something happened in this city worth a lifetime of half-seconds.

I needed the Remembered Days. All of them. And the only way to them was to sleep in the places where it is least safe for me to sleep, which is a sentence my witch would have underlined twice and forbidden once, and she is dead, and the forbidding has lapsed, and the underlining stands.
