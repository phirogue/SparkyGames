\chapter*{Prologue: The Night of the Kettle}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Prologue: The Night of the Kettle}
\markboth{PROLOGUE}{PROLOGUE}

Dusk over Hollowmere, and the lamps going up one by one, like the city buttoning its coat.

From the ridge of the Fish-Giver's roof I can see most of what matters: the chimney forests of the Shambles, the canals doing their slow grey thinking, the fog beginning its evening climb up from the Mere the way it always does, patient as a cat at a mousehole. Somewhere below, a door slams. Somewhere else, a man says something unkind about a horse. The city, in other words, in working order.

She puts the kettle on when the lamps go up. I am punctual. She is predictable. The arrangement works, and has worked for fifteen years, which is longer than any dog on this street has lasted, and I have seen off three.

Her name, for the record --- because tonight of all nights the record matters --- is Elspeth Vane. The city calls her the Needle of Hollowmere, mostly behind her back, always with respect. She is a witch, which in this city is a trade like any other: she sews. Wards, hems, bindings, the long invisible seams that keep a place from coming apart at its corners. Magic here is needlework. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling candles.

And I am Ash. Her cat. Other titles have been proposed over the years --- familiar, companion, mouser-in-residence, \emph{that animal} --- and I answer to none of them and come to all of them, provided there is a fire on.

Tonight calls for a gift. A vole, ideally. Voles say what flowers cannot.

\scenebreak

There. Under the chimney pot. Fat, smug, unaware.

I go low. The tiles are still day-warm under my belly, and the wind is doing me the courtesy of blowing the wrong way for the vole and the right way for me. There is a red feeling that lives at the bottom of a cat, older than cities, older than names, and it comes up through me now like heat through a floor: the world narrowing to one point with fur on it.

Tonight the world is simple, and the world is \emph{red}.

The vole makes its argument, which is brief, and mostly consists of running. I make mine. Mine is better. There is a scuffle among the chimney pots which I will describe, in the interest of dignity, as \emph{professional}, and then it is over, and the vole has been acquired. Thoughtfully dead. Arranged neatly, carried gently, the way a gift ought to be.

She will say ``not on the table,'' and mean \emph{thank you}. Fifteen years. The arrangement works.

I take the rooftop road toward home with the vole warm in my mouth and the evening going purple at its edges, and this is the last hour of my life in which the world is simple, and I spend it thinking about a sunbeam I remember from March. I want that noted. Whatever else the record says of me, let it say I was happy, and did not know it, which is the only way anyone ever is.

\scenebreak

Below me now: the Shambles, shut for the night, stalls sleeping under canvas. Eight bells sound from the tower --- the whole count, one to eight, dropped into the fog like coins into a well.

There used to be a ninth bell. There is no ninth any more. Nobody remembers when that changed, and nobody finds that strange, and the not-finding-it-strange is itself the strange part, if you are the sort of creature who keeps accounts. Cats keep accounts. It is most of what we do, between meals.

And between two chimneys, strung across the dark like a hair caught in a comb: a thread of silver light. Humming.

Everything alive trails a thread, if you have the eyes for it. Threads run through this city thicker than smells --- out of windows, along gutters, wound around wrists and doorposts and the necks of sleeping children --- the underlay of everything, which she calls the Weft. Witches stitch with them. Cats only see them. Hers I know by heart, the way you know a voice through a wall: silver, with a particular twist to it, like handwriting.

This one is silver too. And it is nobody's I know.

I did not chase the silver thread. A cat of fifteen years' standing, carrying a gift, does not go haring off across the roofline after every unexplained piece of glimmer the night hangs up. I looked at it. I considered it. I made a small note in the ledger of things that are wrong tonight, and I went on my way, and the lie I told myself about not \emph{wanting} to chase it weighed nothing at all.

Almost nothing.

\scenebreak

Street level, Needle Lane, and the fog rolls up from the Mere to meet me, and the shadows deepen --- deep enough to wear, if you know how. A cat in proper shadow is a rumor. I have been a rumor most of my life.

Something small and hungry climbs out of the gutter.

A gutter-wisp: bottle-sized, bottle-shaped, a swallowed lantern of a thing, all glow and appetite. They eat small lies. It is one of the tidier arrangements of the night-side --- the city breathes out its little dishonesties and the wisps hoover them up, and everyone pretends this is not happening, which feeds them further. This one heard mine. The one about not wanting to chase the thread. It has come to collect.

I would like to tell you I handled it with grace. What happened is that a cat and a lit appetite had a disagreement in a foggy gutter, and the fog was on my side, and after a certain amount of undignified geometry the wisp reconsidered its position on my honesty and went back down the drain, dimmer.

I sat a moment in the fog and put my coat back in order, and that was when I noticed the quiet.

No pigeons.

Understand: pigeons are never quiet. Quiet is against their religion. The roosts over Needle Lane run a continuous parliamentary session of complaint and scandal from dusk until dawn, and I have slept under that noise my whole life the way you sleep under rain when the roof between you and it is sound. It was not there. The roofline sat against the sky, empty as a shelf.

The ledger of things wrong tonight was becoming a page with writing on both sides.

\scenebreak

The lamps on Needle Lane are lit in the wrong order.

You would have to live here to see it. The Lamplighters walk a sequence, every night, the same way a woman signs her name the same way: third lamp, then first, then the one by the pump, a route so old it has worn a groove in the evening. The order is the point --- I have heard one of them say so, on a doorstep, to an apprentice: \emph{we light them in an order, lad, and the order is the stitch}. Tonight the lane is lit like a mouth of broken teeth, dark-bright-dark-dark-bright, a signature forged by someone who has only ever seen it written.

The Lamplighters never miss. The dog at Number Twelve is awake --- I can smell him being awake --- and he does not bark, which he has never once in his loud and tedious life failed to do.

And halfway down the lane, the fog stops being fog.

It has sleeves. It has a collar. It has the shape of a person who is not in it --- a coat filled with occupied air --- and every cut thread on this street is running toward it, sliding through the dark like water finding a drain. That is when I see what has been done to the lane: the threads are \emph{cut}. Window-threads, door-threads, the little tethers of household luck --- snipped, all of them, and drifting to this one standing shape, which is gathering them in.

They have a name, things like this. Rag-wraiths: clothes whose owners are missing. Not dead --- \emph{missing}. The distinction matters to the things themselves, I am told, and it is about to matter a great deal to me.

It turns what it has instead of a face toward me. Whatever unpicked these lamps is still at its work, and I am standing in its light.

I fought it. I will say this once, plainly, because what came later has made the memory expensive: I put every nature I own against it, the red and the green and the black, and I even spent the pale --- the moonlight-colored strength that is hers, that she stitched into me, that I carry and cannot make --- and none of it was enough, and I am not ashamed. It was above my weight and it knew it. In the end I did the intelligent thing, which the stories never put in: I slipped away. Three streets before I stopped, and I stopped because my legs decided it, not me.

Nothing followed. Nothing was missing. Ash: one. The weather: nothing.

And yet. It was not hunting me. All that size, all that gathered-up stolen thread, and it spent none of it on a cat. It was \emph{working}. Whatever it was doing to that street, I interrupted it about as much as a fly interrupts a window.

I did not know yet what work it was about. I have had a long time since to learn, and the learning is this book.

\scenebreak

Her window is ahead now.

Dark.

Her window is never dark.

Two ways home from here. The long way passes the Lamplighters' hall --- and whatever silenced the lamps went wrong there first; the professional in me knows it the way a tongue knows the broken tooth. The fast way is over the fences. Now.

I am going to tell you what I chose, and then I am going to spend the rest of my lives wondering about it.

I took the long way. Eleven minutes, perhaps twelve. Because a cat is a creature of routes and the route had a wrongness at the head of it, and because fifteen years of her voice in my ear said \emph{look before you leap, Ash, it is the entire difference between brave and stupid}. I told myself she was out. I told myself the kettle was late, the lamp unlit, some errand, some visit. The wisps, if any were near, will have eaten well on that.

The hall stood open. Door wide, ladders racked, coats on their pegs. The kettle on their little stove was cold --- and a guild hall's kettle is never cold; it is the heart the place beats with. Forty men and women walk out of this room every evening and light a city, and the room was holding its breath, and nobody was in it.

Caught on a lamp-hook by the door, glinting where it had snagged: a thread of silver.

\emph{Her} stitching. I would know it under floodwater. She was here --- tonight, in this room, my witch, who has not walked out on an evening in years. Why was she \emph{here}?

I ran then. Out of the hall and eleven streets home, and I do not remember eight of them. The fog thinning near the Mere. Ordinary houses, ordinary gates, a cat crossing an ordinary city at an unreasonable speed. Her street. Her gate. Her drainpipe, the one I have used nine hundred times, and up, and in through the workroom window --- a door has never once been an obstacle; it is, however, always an insult ---

and the house is doing the wrong thing with sound.

A kettle, screaming. And nobody going to it.

\scenebreak

The workroom first, and the workroom is wrong. The threads on the walls --- her lifetime of small tied workings, the hems of the house, forty years of her work hung like a garden --- cut. All of them. Hanging like torn hems, every single one snipped at the same height, cleanly, as if by someone \emph{tidying}.

The parlor door stands open, and I do not go through it. Not yet. I am a cat who has learned to look at a room before he is in it. I stop at the threshold, low, and I look.

Something is standing in the parlor doorway on the far side, wearing the room.

It is person-shaped the way a coat on a hook is person-shaped, and it is made of \emph{her} threads --- it has been pulling them out of the walls and putting them \emph{on}, winding forty years of her work around its absence like a man dressing in another man's clothes. Where the light of the hearth should catch a face there is a weave, still moving, still settling, thread finding thread with a sound like the smallest rain.

It is still working. It does not stop when it sees me.

That is the part I will keep, of everything that night: it looked at me --- whatever it looks with --- and it \emph{decided I was not an interruption}, and it went back to its work.

Every hair I have is standing up. Good. Let them.

I went for it. Of course I went for it. She was somewhere past that doorway and the thing between us was wearing her wards, and there is a red feeling at the bottom of a cat older than sense. I remember the first spring off the floorboards. I remember the weave turning, unhurried, and a sleeve coming around like a laden clothesline let go in a gale. I remember the pale strength going out of me in one bright useless flare --- \emph{spend it}, her voice said, from fifteen years of lessons, \emph{spend everything, it is not for keeping} --- and I spent everything, and it was not enough, and the room went sideways, and then the cold came up through the floor in a way that floors do not do.

I want to tell you dying hurts. It would make better telling. What it does is \emph{leave}: the cold goes first, then the noise, then the part of you that was worried about the noise. What is left over found itself standing in a corridor that was all filing and no building, under a ceiling too far up to be a ceiling, in a light the color of four in the morning.

Nothing hurt. I found this deeply suspicious.

A desk. A lamp. A queue of one, and the one was me.

The being behind the desk was thin the way a ruled line is thin, translucent at the cuffs, with spectacles that had given up asking to be needed. He did not look up. Around him, shelves went away in every direction that shelves can go, and some that they cannot, all of them full, every folder tied with grey ribbon.

``Name?''

``Ashcat,'' I said. ``Of Needle Lane.'' It seemed a night for the full formal.

``Needle Lane.'' Something moved behind the spectacles that was almost interest. ``Yes. We have had a busy evening from Needle Lane.''

He wrote. The pen made the sound of winter. Then he reached the column that mattered, and stopped.

``Nine allotted.'' He said it to the paper, not to me. He turned the sheet over, as though the back of it might explain; the back of it did not. ``I have not filed a nine since I was at a much smaller desk. Vane's familiar.'' A pause you could have hung a coat on. ``One spent.''

He did not say whose the busy evening was. I did not ask. Not there. There are rooms where questions are expensive, and every whisker I own said this was the most expensive room in the world.

``The arrangement, since it is your first,'' said the Clerk. ``Deaths are appointments. Tonight, you had one.'' He said it without cruelty, the way a man reads weather. ``Arrive dead \emph{without} an appointment and you will be refused at this desk --- we are a court, not a revolving door. A true death --- the kind you will always see coming --- costs a life, and after the first, the Toll. Any other defeat is refused and returned. The first visit is complimentary. Do consider it a courtesy rather than a habit.''

The stamp came down. It was, I noticed, a paw print. Neither of us remarked on this.

The stairs up are longer than the fall down. They always are.

\scenebreak

Hearing came back first. Floorboards under one cheek. The smell of cold hearth. Four paws --- count them --- four. Whatever had been standing in the doorway was not standing in the doorway.

Get up, Ash.

The parlor.

Elspeth in her chair. The light gone out of her hands.

\begin{center}\ldots\end{center}

The kettle was still screaming. It had been screaming this whole time, and I did not hear it until now. She hates it when it does that.

I took it off the fire. One paw, on the handle, the way I had watched her do it ten thousand times from the hearthrug. It took three tries. Nobody will ever know.

And then the house was quiet, and there was nothing else to take off any fire.

I got up on the arm of her chair, which I am not allowed to do, and nobody told me to get down. Her hands were folded in her lap, and her face was not frightened, and not surprised, and not anything --- the needle gone from it, the thread gone from it. Between the kettle boiling and the kettle screaming, any professional will tell you, is no time at all. Whoever did this to her did it \emph{fast}, faster than any needle honestly goes, and did it in her own parlor, in her own light, between one breath of steam and the next.

Fifteen years. Every evening. She always came to the window.

I do not know how to be a cat that nobody is expecting.

I stayed on the arm of the chair while the fog pressed its face to the glass, and I will not tell you how long, because I do not know, and a cat is not obliged to keep time for anyone. At some point I found myself looking at the sewing table. Her red kerchief lay on it, folded --- washed that morning, waiting to go back around her neck, where it had lived so long it was less a garment than a fact of her.

I put my head through it. Nobody tied it. It stays.

The thread at my chest --- the one that was always hers, the tether every familiar wears, silver with that particular handwriting twist --- cut. Of course. Cut like everything in this house was cut.

Not quite.

One strand left. Thin as spite. Running out from under the window, over the sill where her geraniums used to argue with the slugs, down into the fog, into the city.

I looked at it a long time. Outside, eight bells rang somewhere, and no ninth.

Then that is where we are going.
