\chapter{The City Talks}

Find the teacher, she said. But a teacher is found through pupils, and the night's pupils were scattered across three guilds and two species, so I went and asked everyone in the city who cannot keep a secret, starting with the ones who keep them best.

The Ratsmeet convenes under the fish market in the hour the ice melts.

That is not poetry; it is scheduling. When the fishmongers' ice goes to water and runs down through the gratings, the under-market becomes briefly uninhabitable to anything that minds wet feet, which is everything except rats and one sufficiently motivated cat. The rat-kingdoms are a nation under the floor: organized, literate, unionized, with a fierce line in municipal record-keeping. Nothing moves through the Shambles at night that a rat does not book.

There is a protocol for a cat attending the Ratsmeet, and I had it from Mirri the ratcatcher's daughter, who negotiates with rats instead of catching them. The protocol is this: do not eat anybody. Do not say ``trap.'' Do not say ``terrier.'' Do not be a cat about it.

And do not argue with the doorman, who is a goose.

It was not my goose --- not the tannery Watch goose with the chain of office --- but they are all cousins, geese. This one considered my references at the culvert-mouth for a cold quarter hour, hissed once on principle, and let me through with the air of a professional who wants it understood that letting me through and approving of me are separate line items.

The Grey Boss holds court on a throne of corks.

He is old the way paper is old: grey to the point of transparency, whiskers gone wispy, eyes like wet ink. ``I remember the ninth bell,'' he said, before I had finished sitting. ``You will hear it said no one does. I am no one, then.'' The court laughed the way courts laugh. He watched me not laugh.

I put my question with the manners Mirri had drilled --- to the Meet, not the Boss --- and asked about the murder night on Needle Lane.

``The witch's cat,'' said the Grey Boss. ``We had a lively night. Everyone had a lively night.''

A rat's silence is a price list. So is a rat's phrasing. I turned the sentence over in the drip of the melting ice: \emph{a lively night} --- on a lane his own bookings would show empty. Empty lanes are not lively. Unless the liveliness was the emptiness itself: a whole nation under the floor, booking nothing, all night, on a street where nothing had ever needed booking before --- and being paid, or frightened, or \emph{favored} into finding that unremarkable.

``You cannot clear out of an empty street,'' I said, slowly, watching him. ``But you can be cleared out of one that is about to stop being empty.''

The old rat's ears made a small movement his court could not see. ``The cat can count,'' he said, dry as his corks. Then he gave me my change: ``A cart,'' he said. ``No chandler on it --- we know the chandlers' boys, every one, and this driver we did not know and have not seen since. And behind the cart, in no hurry, a gentleman. Going nowhere in particular, the whole way down the lane.''

``What sort of gentleman?''

``The sort you don't book,'' said the Grey Boss, and the whole Ratsmeet went quiet, and the audience was over, and the goose saw me out with one satisfied hiss.

By morning, I knew, the Chandlers would hear a cat had been asking. Good. Nervous men check their locks, and checking your locks tells the whole street which doors matter to you.

\scenebreak

The pigeons took longer, because the pigeons were having an election.

The Pigeon Post is Hollowmere's gossip made systematic: every ledge a listening post, every roost a sorting office. Whoever leads the Post decides what the city overhears. They elect a Postmaster by hat --- you perch on the brim of your candidate's chosen hat, the whole electorate at once, and the hat that bows lowest under its voters wins. I am told the founders considered this incorruptible. It had not occurred to them that somebody might simply feed the electorate.

A slick young cock-pigeon called Toby was winning on unlimited seed.

It was Ash's problem --- it was \emph{my} problem; I find I have started writing like the Clerk files, and I will not apologize --- because of the one fact this whole account keeps circling: the night my witch died, every pigeon in the city went quiet at once. Not frightened quiet, which is loud the moment the fright passes. \emph{Bought} quiet. And now here was an election swimming in mystery seed, and I do not believe in prosperous coincidences.

I followed the seed backward. It is the sort of work a cat is shaped for: nights on the roofline, upwind, unhurried, watching grain arrive in quantity at the roosts and walking the supply line back hop by hop --- ledge, gutter, chute --- without ever once being seen following, because pigeons notice a cat on a roofline the way bankers notice fire. The trail ended above Needle Lane, at a grain-chute swept a little too neatly, and there the arithmetic stood up and announced itself: good seed, bought in bulk, and hauled \emph{up}. Nobody hauls seed up. Seed falls; that is the entire economic principle of being a pigeon. This seed had been carried to the rooftops and scattered from above, wide as weather, by somebody with \emph{hands}.

I took what I had to the Perch --- the full assembly, hats and all --- and I put it before the old Postmaster in front of his entire electorate, because some testimony only works in public. I laid it out in order, the way she taught me: the seed, the chute, the hauling-up, the particular night the whole Post had spent with its beaks full. And I watched an old bird's pride fight an old bird's honesty, feather by feather, and honesty win by a neck.

``We voted with our beaks full,'' he said, at last, to the assembly, ``and we called the quiet weather.''

From a Postmaster, before the whole Perch, that is a confession with plumage. The roosts went up in the kind of noise they had failed to make on the night that mattered. Toby attempted a point of order and was sat on by his own electors, which is the only pigeon institution I have ever admired without reservation. Elections were suspended pending fresh hats and a seed audit.

But mark what the confession actually said, because it is the chapter's whole cargo: the seed came \emph{before} the night. Days before. Laid in like stores against a winter. The murder was not an act of passion that happened to find the city conveniently deaf; the murder had a \emph{quartermaster} --- someone who provisioned the silence in advance, in bulk, by hand, the way a clerk provisions a shipment. I went home over the roofs with that word sitting in my chest like swallowed lead, and under her old window a lamplighter was relighting Needle Lane, and the first pigeon in a month settled onto the ledge above the dark glass and said something rude about me to the whole street.

The city was talking again. I had never in my life been so glad to be insulted.

\scenebreak

And then the city, having resumed talking, immediately talked about me, and I was summoned to court.

Not the Hollow Court. The other one. The Court of Whiskers convenes on the ropewalk roof at the wrong hour on purpose --- attendance being itself the entry fee; a cat who will not lose sleep for the law is a cat whose opinion the law can spare --- and I arrived on the summons of three yowls and a dead moth left on my sill, which is how our bar serves papers. The docket that night was a kitten.

Tansy: small, orange, absurd, with the build of a mitten and the conviction of a siege engine. Her case, as the assembled seniors laid it out from their chimney-stacks, was this: to save her three brothers when the spring flood took their crate down the canal, she had sold her purr to the Quiet Gentleman for the favor that fished them out.

The contract was legal. That was the scandal of it. No theft, no trick: consideration given --- three lives; consideration received --- one purr, surrendered in escrow. But a kitten without a purr cannot be comforted and cannot comfort, which is the entire profession of a kitten, and the Court of Whiskers wanted the precedent \emph{killed} before the Gentleman --- of whom more, much more, in later chapters; this was the first time I heard the name said aloud, and every tail on that roof went still while it was said --- before the Gentleman opened a \emph{market} in such things.

The kitten in question was asleep in the lee of a chimney through her own hearing. Midway through the arguments she woke, located me --- I do not know how; we had never met --- marched across the assembled jurisprudence of the rooftops, sat down against my flank, and told the Court: ``This is my counsel. He is the best one.''

I had issued no such appointment. The Court minuted it anyway. I am given to understand the minute stands to this day, and that it is the only thing in the archives of the Court of Whiskers with a smile in the pawwriting.

What could I do? I did the job. The old ones gave me the one piece of law that matters where the Gentleman is concerned --- \emph{he never signs anything that can be settled; read it before you touch it} --- and I went down into the Shambles to the strongroom where his paper lodges. No guards. His paper guards itself; that should tell you most of what the later chapters will cost. And on a shelf in that dry dark room, her purr: a small knot of thread, sitting in escrow, purring faintly to itself, which is the loneliest sound I have heard in a city that contains the Hollow Court.

And the knot had grown a loop nobody tied.

I read it three times to be sure, in the Weft-light, the way the Court of Whiskers had charged me to read. The contract was flawless --- his contracts are always flawless; flawlessness is his signature --- but a purr, it turns out, is not inert goods. A purr \emph{accrues}. Warmth begets warmth even on a shelf; the thing had been quietly earning the whole season, and the interest had knotted itself a new loop, and the new loop appeared in no schedule of the contract, because the Gentleman's paper is exact and exactness cuts both ways: what is not conveyed is not conveyed. The interest was nobody's. Unclaimed goods, on his shelf, tangled into his escrow.

You cannot break the Gentleman's knots. But an unclaimed loop may be unpicked by any finder, and a knot that has grown into another knot comes undone \emph{with} it, if the unpicker is patient and the unpicking is clean, and I was, and it was. Three nights, the last one until nearly dawn, loop by loop, her small stubborn purr humming against my paws the whole time like a trapped bee getting hopeful.

It came loose all at once, like a cork, and went home at volume. I am told --- I was there; ``I am told'' is cowardice; I \emph{saw} --- that Tansy, three streets away and sound asleep on my hearthrug, where she had installed herself without leave two days earlier, woke mid-purr, purred so hard she fell off the rug, and went back to sleep still running.

On the empty shelf where the escrow had sat: a calling card. Blank, both faces, smelling faintly of fog. It had not been there when I started.

I carried it home in a shadow-pocket, and it weighed what secrets weigh, and I pinned it to the board on the chimney breast beside the candle stub and the docket and the shroud thread, and I sat on the mantel a long time looking at the only blank exhibit in my whole case, which said, more clearly than print: \emph{noted. --- G.}

Tansy stayed. I explained to her that I live alone. ``Yes,'' she said. ``We live alone.'' She moved the remainder of her personal effects --- one chewed bootlace --- behind the coal scuttle. She sleeps on my paws, which obstructs the paws, and purrs like a small wrong engine, and somewhere in the Gentleman's strongroom a page of his ledger is one line lighter, and I have been advised by the Court of Whiskers to consider what that will someday cost me, and I have considered it, and I find I would pay it again.

\scenebreak

One more thing belongs to this chapter, because this chapter is about what a city says when it starts talking again, and not everything the city said was about murder.

The Fish-Giver missed two dawns.

For eleven years that man has set out scraps behind his stall at first light for half the strays of Thimblefield --- rain, frost, fish-glut or fish-famine, the scraps go down and the man stands in his doorway saying good morning to animals who officially are not there. He has never learned my name. He calls every cat ``puss,'' with the same democratic courtesy he shows the magistrates who buy his herring. And he missed two dawns, and the half of Thimblefield that eats behind his stall assembled on his roof-ridge like a jury, and I went down to investigate, because cats do not worry --- cats \emph{investigate}.

The trouble was on his door, in paper: rent behind, lease called, seven days --- through a bank that banks for the Chandlers. Nobody was going to knife the Fish-Giver in his parlor. They were going to \emph{file} him out of it. I had been learning all season what this city does with knives; here was my education in what it does with ink, and the lesson had a smell I recognized from a counting room in Wickrow.

No monsters, then. No wraiths, no wisps. A purely human-sized catastrophe, and it wanted purely cat-sized assets. He had three, and did not know he had any.

The first was down his own drainpipe: his grandmother's brooch, lost nine years ago, mourned to every customer since --- a story I had dozed through from his roof forty times. No hand fits that pipe. A paw does. It cost me a wet shoulder, a spider's entire opinion of me, and one bent whisker, and I laid the thing on his step at midnight, where he found it at dawn and sat down on his own threshold with it and cried like a well-fed child, and the gleam of it, fenced honest at Brindle's rates, was worth precisely a quarter-day of rent, which is not what it was worth to him, which is the point of gleam and the whole reason the Exchange will outlast the banks.

The second asset was the pigeon-net, newly loquacious and feeling it owed me: the landlord's man, the roosts reported within the hour, takes his evenings loudly at the Bent Anchor, and has a sister, and the sister is a magistrate.

The third asset was that the magistrate keeps a cat, who owed me a favor of long standing, and the favor was called and paid off the page, cat fashion: silently, completely, and without either government being consulted. I do not know what her cat said to the magistrate; I know that certain paperwork was reviewed, that a lease's calling was found procedurally defective, and that a bank which banks for the Chandlers chose, on reflection, not to have the defect examined in open court.

Dawn, three days later. The shutter went up. The scraps went down. The jury on the roof-ridge dissolved into a congregation, and the Fish-Giver stood in the middle of it grinning at nothing, wearing his grandmother on his coat, saying good morning to animals who officially are not there.

He never learned any of it. That is the point. That is the whole point. Somewhere my witch was --- wherever the tide files its cases --- knowing perfectly well what I had been doing all week, saying \emph{not on the table, Ash}, and meaning what she always meant.
