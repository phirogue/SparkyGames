\chapter{The City Talks}

Find the teacher, she said. But a teacher is found through pupils, and the night's pupils were scattered across three guilds and two species, so I went and asked everyone in the city who cannot keep a secret, starting with the ones who keep them best.

The Ratsmeet convenes under the fish market in the hour the ice melts. Not poetry: scheduling. When the fishmongers' ice runs down through the gratings, the under-market turns briefly uninhabitable to anything that minds wet feet, and rats do not, and one sufficiently motivated cat did not. The rat-kingdoms are a nation under the floor --- organized, literate, unionized --- and nothing moves through the Shambles at night that a rat does not book.

There is a protocol for a cat attending, and I had it from Mirri the ratcatcher's daughter: do not eat anybody; do not say ``trap''; do not say ``terrier''; do not be a cat about it; and do not argue with the doorman, who is a goose. This one considered my references at the culvert-mouth, hissed once on principle, and let me through with the air of one who wanted it understood that admission and approval were separate line items.

The Grey Boss holds court on a throne of corks.

He is old the way paper is old: grey to the point of transparency, whiskers gone wispy, eyes like wet ink. ``I remember the ninth bell,'' he said, before I had finished sitting. ``You will hear it said no one does. I am no one, then.'' The court laughed the way courts laugh. He watched me not laugh.

I put my question with the manners Mirri had drilled --- to the Meet, not the Boss --- and asked about the murder night on Needle Lane.

``The witch's cat,'' said the Grey Boss. ``We had a lively night. Everyone had a lively night.''

A rat's silence is a price list. So is a rat's phrasing. \emph{A lively night} --- on a lane his own bookings would show empty. Unless the liveliness was the emptiness itself: a whole nation under the floor, booking nothing, all night, on a street that had never needed booking --- and being paid, or frightened, or \emph{favored} into finding that unremarkable.

``You cannot clear out of an empty street,'' I said, slowly, watching him. ``But you can be cleared out of one that is about to stop being empty.''

The old rat's ears made a small movement his court could not see. ``The cat can count,'' he said, dry as his corks. Then he gave me my change: ``A cart,'' he said. ``No chandler on it --- we know the chandlers' boys, every one, and this driver we did not know and have not seen since. And behind the cart, in no hurry, a gentleman. Going nowhere in particular, the whole way down the lane.''

``What sort of gentleman?''

``The sort you don't book,'' said the Grey Boss, and the whole Ratsmeet went quiet, and the audience was over, and the goose saw me out with one satisfied hiss.

By morning, I knew, the Chandlers would hear a cat had been asking. Good. Nervous men check their locks, and checking your locks tells the whole street which doors matter to you.

\scenebreak

The pigeons took longer, because the pigeons were having an election.

The Pigeon Post is Hollowmere's gossip made systematic: whoever leads the Post decides what the city overhears. They elect a Postmaster by hat --- the whole electorate perches at once on the brim of its candidate's chosen hat, and the hat that bows lowest wins. I am told the founders considered this incorruptible. It had not occurred to them that somebody might simply feed the electorate.

A slick young cock-pigeon called Toby was winning on unlimited seed.

It was my problem because of the one fact this whole account keeps circling: the night my witch died, every pigeon in the city went quiet at once. Not frightened quiet, which is loud the moment the fright passes. \emph{Bought} quiet. And now here was an election swimming in mystery seed, and I do not believe in prosperous coincidences.

I followed the seed backward: nights on the roofline, upwind, unhurried, walking the supply line back hop by hop --- ledge, gutter, chute --- without once being seen following. The trail ended above Needle Lane, at a grain-chute swept a little too neatly, and there the arithmetic announced itself: good seed, bought in bulk, and hauled \emph{up}. Nobody hauls seed up. Seed falls; that is the entire economic principle of being a pigeon. This seed had been carried to the rooftops and scattered from above, wide as weather, by somebody with \emph{hands}.

I will also report, because this account has sworn off flattering me, how the second night of that trail ended. There is a gutter on the granary side that I have used four hundred times, and on the four hundred and first it resigned. Wet iron, old fixings, a cat with his head full of seed --- I went down three storeys in the company of the gutter, which at least had the grace to go first, and I was dead before I finished being embarrassed.

The corridor again. The light the color of four in the morning. The queue of one. The Clerk looked at me over the spectacles for a long, costly moment, and consulted the book, and did not pick up the stamp.

``You have no appointment,'' he said.

``I fell,'' I said.

``I am aware. Falling is not an appointment. Falling is an accident that has got above itself.'' He turned the book around so that I could see the page, which was blank, and turned it back. ``REFUSED. The desk does not entertain walk-ins. Mind the door on your way out; it sticks.''

It does stick. I woke in the granary yard with rain in my whiskers, a gutter across my tail, and my banked shine lighter by a filing fee --- the desk charges for walk-ins; it is a court, not a charity --- humiliated in a place beyond anatomy, and one entry richer: the desk below keeps its own calendar, and nothing I did to myself by carelessness would ever move an appointment up. I would like to say I never tested the rule again. This account has rules about lying.

I took what I had to the Perch --- full assembly, hats and all --- and put it before the old Postmaster, because some testimony only works in public. I laid it out in order, the way she taught me: the seed, the chute, the hauling-up, the particular night the whole Post had spent with its beaks full. And I watched an old bird's pride fight an old bird's honesty, feather by feather, and honesty win by a neck.

``We voted with our beaks full,'' he said, at last, to the assembly, ``and we called the quiet weather.''

From a Postmaster, before the whole Perch, that is a confession with plumage. The roosts went up in the kind of noise they had failed to make on the night that mattered. Toby attempted a point of order and was sat on by his own electors, which is the only pigeon institution I have ever admired without reservation.

But mark what the confession actually said, because it is the chapter's whole cargo: the seed came \emph{before} the night. Days before. Laid in like stores against a winter. The murder was not an act of passion that happened to find the city conveniently deaf; the murder had a \emph{quartermaster} --- someone who provisioned the silence in advance, in bulk, by hand, the way a clerk provisions a shipment. I went home over the roofs with that word sitting in my chest like swallowed lead, and under her old window a lamplighter was relighting Needle Lane, and the first pigeon in a month settled onto the ledge above the dark glass and said something rude about me to the whole street.

The city was talking again. I had never in my life been so glad to be insulted.

\scenebreak

Then the city, having resumed talking, talked about me, and I was summoned to court.

Not the Hollow Court. The other one. The Court of Whiskers convenes on the ropewalk roof at the wrong hour on purpose --- a cat who will not lose sleep for the law is a cat whose opinion the law can spare --- and I arrived on the summons of three yowls and a dead moth left on my sill, which is how our bar serves papers. The docket that night was a kitten.

Tansy: small, orange, absurd, with the build of a mitten and the conviction of a fishwife. Her case, as the seniors laid it out from their chimney-stacks, was this: to save her three brothers when the spring flood took their crate down the canal, she had sold her purr to the Quiet Gentleman for the favor that fished them out.

The contract was legal. That was the scandal of it. No theft, no trick: consideration given --- three lives; consideration received --- one purr, surrendered in escrow. But a kitten without a purr cannot be comforted and cannot comfort, which is the entire profession of a kitten, and the Court of Whiskers wanted the precedent \emph{killed} --- every tail on that roof went still while the name was said --- before the Gentleman opened a \emph{market} in such things.

The kitten slept in the lee of a chimney through her own hearing. Midway through the arguments she woke, located me --- I do not know how; we had never met --- marched across the assembled jurisprudence of the rooftops, sat down against my flank, and told the Court: ``This is my counsel. He is the best one.''

I had issued no such appointment. The Court minuted it anyway. I am given to understand the minute stands to this day, and that it is the only thing in the archives of the Court of Whiskers with a smile in the pawwriting.

What could I do? I did the job. The old ones gave me the one piece of law that matters --- \emph{he never signs anything that can be settled; read it before you touch it} --- and I went down to the Shambles strongroom where his paper lodges. No guards; his paper guards itself, and that should tell you most of what the later chapters will cost. On a shelf in that dry dark, her purr: a small knot of thread in escrow, purring faintly to itself, which is the loneliest sound I have heard in a city that contains the Hollow Court.

And the knot had grown a loop nobody tied.

I read it three times in the Weft-light, as the Court had charged me. The contract was flawless --- his contracts always are; flawlessness is his signature --- but a purr is not inert goods. A purr \emph{accrues}. Warmth begets warmth even on a shelf, and a season's interest had knotted itself a loop that appeared in no schedule, because his paper is exact and exactness cuts both ways: what is not conveyed is not conveyed. The interest was nobody's, and an unclaimed loop may be unpicked by any finder, and a knot grown into another knot comes undone \emph{with} it, if the unpicking is clean. Three nights, the last until nearly dawn, loop by loop, her small stubborn purr humming against my paws like a trapped bee getting hopeful --- and it came loose all at once, like a cork, and went home at volume.

I am told --- I was there; ``I am told'' is cowardice; I \emph{saw} --- that Tansy, three streets away on the hearthrug where she had installed herself without leave two days earlier, woke mid-purr, purred so hard she fell off the rug, and went back to sleep still running.

On the empty shelf where the escrow had sat: a calling card. Blank, both faces, smelling faintly of fog. It had not been there when I started.

I carried it home in a shadow-pocket --- it weighed what secrets weigh --- and pinned it to the board beside the candle stub and the docket and the shroud thread, and sat on the mantel a long time looking at the only blank exhibit in my case, which said, more clearly than print: \emph{noted. --- G.}

Tansy stayed. I explained to her that I live alone. ``Yes,'' she said. ``We live alone.'' She moved the remainder of her personal effects --- one chewed bootlace --- behind the coal scuttle. She sleeps on my paws, which obstructs the paws, and purrs like a small wrong engine, and somewhere in the Gentleman's strongroom a page of his ledger is one line lighter, and I have been advised by the Court of Whiskers to consider what that will someday cost me, and I have considered it, and I find I would pay it again.

\scenebreak

One more thing belongs here, because not everything the city said, once it was talking again, was about murder.

The Fish-Giver missed two dawns.

Pell, the paper on his door calls him. No cat uses it; a name is for creatures who might be confused with somebody else, and there is nobody else like him. For eleven years that man has set out scraps behind his stall at first light for half the strays of Thimblefield --- rain, frost, fish-glut or fish-famine --- and stood in his doorway saying good morning to animals who officially are not there. He has never learned my name; every cat is ``puss,'' with the same democratic courtesy he shows the magistrates who buy his herring. Two dawns, no scraps, and the half of Thimblefield that eats behind his stall assembled on his roof-ridge like a jury, and I went down, because cats do not worry --- cats \emph{investigate}.

The trouble was on his door, in paper: rent behind, lease called, seven days --- through a bank that banks for the Chandlers. Nobody was going to knife the Fish-Giver in his parlor; they were going to \emph{file} him out of it. All season I had been learning what this city does with knives; here was its ink, and it smelled of a counting room in Wickrow. No monsters, no wraiths, no wisps: a human-sized catastrophe, and it wanted cat-sized assets. He had three, and did not know he had any.

The first was down his own drainpipe: his grandmother's brooch, lost nine years ago and mourned to every customer since --- a story I had dozed through from his roof forty times. No hand fits that pipe. A paw does. One wet shoulder and a bent whisker later, the brooch lay on his step at midnight, and at dawn he sat down on his own threshold with it and cried like a well-fed child. Fenced honest at Brindle's rates, its gleam was worth precisely a quarter-day of rent, which is not what it was worth to him --- and that is the point of gleam, and the reason the Exchange will outlast the banks.

The second asset was the pigeon-net, newly loquacious and feeling it owed me: the landlord's man, the roosts reported within the hour, takes his evenings loudly at the Bent Anchor, and has a sister, and the sister is a magistrate.

The third was that the magistrate keeps a cat, who owed me a favor of long standing, and the favor was called and paid off the page, cat fashion: silently, completely, neither government consulted. Certain paperwork was reviewed; a lease's calling was found procedurally defective; and a bank which banks for the Chandlers chose, on reflection, not to have the defect examined in open court.

Mark that one. It goes on spending for the rest of this book, so I will state it here where I learned it: that was the first fight I ever won without a fight. A season of learning what this city does with knives, and the sharpest thing in it turned out to be a defective hinge in a lease. Paper has teeth. I filed the lesson where I file weapons.

Dawn, three days later. The shutter went up. The scraps went down. The jury on the roof-ridge dissolved into a congregation, and the Fish-Giver stood in the middle of it grinning at nothing, wearing his grandmother on his coat, saying good morning to animals who officially are not there.

He never learned any of it. That is the point. That is the whole point. Somewhere my witch was --- wherever the tide files its cases --- knowing perfectly well what I had been doing all week, saying \emph{not on the table, Ash}, and meaning what she always meant.

\scenebreak

One last entry from that week, and I want it on its own line, because it is the first line of a page I would be adding to for the rest of the season.

The morning the Fish-Giver's shutter went up, the Mere fog was still standing in the Shambles' low lanes at full market bell. Not thick. Just \emph{present}, an hour past its going-home time, the way a creditor is present in a doorway. The fishwives worked around it and called it weather. I sat on Pell's roof-ridge and watched it decline to leave until nearly noon, and that night I opened a fresh page in the ledger and headed it the way she headed a fraying hem she could not yet re-sew: \emph{WATCH THIS.}

The fog had never kept market hours before. Somebody had cut the Needle out of this city, and the city's oldest resident had noticed the same week I did.
