\chapter{The Carrying and the Wake}

Three witches came at first light.

They did not knock. Witches do not knock at a house where a witch has died; a knock asks a question, and the house has already answered it. The door opened to Mother Cardew's hand the way furniture obeys the very old, and behind her came two young ones I did not know, one carrying folded linen, undyed --- the only clean thing left in the room --- and one carrying nothing but her own held breath.

I was on the arm of the chair. I had been on the arm of the chair all night. I did not get down.

Cardew is the eldest of the Witches' Circuit, which is nine seats of working witches, or was; it is eight now, though no one had said so aloud yet in that room. She is small and grey and folded, like something kept a long time in a drawer, and her eyes went around the parlor once, at working speed, and I watched her see everything. The cut threads. The cold kettle sitting off the fire, which nobody could account for and nobody asked about. Elspeth.

They lifted her the way you lift someone who might wake. That is the thing I will allow them, all three, forever: they were witches of the Circuit and she was the Needle and there is politics even in a parlor, but they lifted her like a sleeping woman, and the young one who had been carrying her own breath let it go, once, in the doorway, in a small ragged sound she will be embarrassed about for years. I hope she reads this. It was the correct sound.

At the doorway, with the linen and its burden through it, Cardew stopped. She looked back at the walls, at forty years of workings hanging cut and dead at one tidy height, and she said:

``Who did this to the room.''

It was not a question. Burglars take things. Nothing in this house was taken --- not the silver thimbles, not the bolt of moon-colored cloth worth a bargeman's year, not the rings on the hand of the woman in the chair. Somebody had come into the Needle's own parlor and \emph{unpicked} it, and left everything of value except the value.

And then Cardew was looking at me.

Everyone else was looking at the floor. That is what people do around a dead witch's cat: they look at the floor, because a familiar who has outlived his witch is a piece of grief with legs and no one wants to catch its eye. Cardew looked straight at me, the way you look at a colleague, and waited.

I had one chance to be taken seriously, and I knew it. A cat who yowls is comforted. A cat who performs is petted. I sat up on the chair arm, and I told it --- all of it, in order, the way she taught me anything worth saying should go: the lamps lit wrong, the pigeons silent, the hall open and empty and its kettle cold, her silver thread on the lamp-hook, the fog with sleeves, the threads of the street running toward it like water to a drain. The workroom. The parlor doorway. The shape wearing the room, still working, that looked at me and decided I was not an interruption.

Cats can speak to those who trouble to listen. Most people do not trouble; it takes patience, and a willingness to be corrected. Cardew listened the whole way through without moving, and the two young witches stood in the doorway holding the morning open, and when I finished --- \emph{a shape wearing her work} --- the old woman was quiet for a long moment.

Then she said, quieter, to nobody: ``Again.''

She put the word down like something she had not meant to be holding. And I filed it, because \emph{again} means \emph{before}, and before means a pattern, and a pattern means the thing that killed my witch has habits, and things with habits can be found.

At the door, with her girls and their burden already in the lane, she turned once more.

``Mind the room, cat,'' she said. ``Rooms remember what people miss.''

Then they were gone into the fog with her, and the house was mine, and it was nobody's.

\scenebreak

I minded the room.

I went over it the way she went over a hem: slowly, twice, against the nap. A cat's advantages in an investigation are these --- we are low, we are patient, we are invisible to people who believe importance has a minimum height, and we have a nose that keeps better records than the Hollow Court. The parlor smelled of cold hearth and geranium and her --- I will not write about that part --- and, under everything, faint as a fingerprint on glass, of something that did not belong: pond-bottom. Old rope. Patient water. The Mere has a smell, and the Mere does not come up to Thimblefield, and it had been in this room.

And under the window, where the curtain hem brushes the boards, I found the candle stub.

Beeswax. Burned an inch down and pinched out, and stamped near the base with the mark of the bee: the Chandlers' Guild, who hold the monopoly on every honest light in this city and several of the dishonest ones. Understand what that stub was doing in that room: nothing. It had no business existing there. Elspeth burned tallow her whole life --- good witch-made tallow, smoky and stubborn like her --- and she called beeswax \emph{the rich man's smell} and would not have it in the house. Somebody else's light was in this room. Somebody stood at this window, in the dark of my witch's parlor, and burned a fine wax candle down one inch --- which is about an hour, as any cat who has slept beside a flame can tell you --- and pinched it out, and did not trouble to take it away.

An hour. Somebody \emph{waited} here, in the dark, comfortable, unhurried, while the kettle heated. In a parlor whose wards had stood forty years, and stood no more.

I sat with the stub between my paws while the light came up grey in the windows, and I began, formally, the way she began a working, with a naming of parts.

Item: a silver thread on a lamp-hook in an emptied hall. She was there, the night she died, and I did not know why, and hall-kettles do not go cold on their own.

Item: a street of cut threads, and a thing wearing them, that worked and did not hunt.

Item: quiet pigeons. Bought quiet or frightened quiet, but quiet has a price either way, and prices leave receipts.

Item: an hour of beeswax, pinched out, stamped with the bee.

Item: the word \emph{again}.

Item: one strand of her, thin as spite, running out under the window, into the city. Mine now. My inheritance, along with the pale strength I carry and cannot make, and this house, and the arrangement of my entire life, cancelled.

The pale wants explaining, once, so the rest of this account can spend it without stopping. A cat runs on four strengths: the red, which is the hunt; the green, which is patience; the black, which is the long watch and the soft foot. Those three are mine the way teeth are mine --- spend them and they grow back with meat and sleep. The fourth is the pale, moonlight-colored, and the witches call it plainly Moonlight: it is memory made spendable, it pays the prices the other three cannot, and no cat makes his own. Mine is hers --- she stitched it into me --- and spending it has never once felt like spending something of mine.

On the chimney breast above the mantel there is a cork board where she pinned the city's wants: little notes in little hands, \emph{Mistress Vane, the churn's gone sour-hearted}; \emph{Needle, the gate wards, before Lady's Day if you can}. Requests for her needle from a city that had no idea what it had. I got up on the mantel --- I am allowed on the mantel; I have always been allowed on the mantel, that was the treaty of the second year --- and I looked at that board a long time.

Then I knocked her pincushion off the shelf, took the longest pin in my teeth, and pinned the candle stub to the board, dead center.

My whole job used to be: be somewhere warm at the correct time. All right, then. New job.

\scenebreak

They gave her to the tide at the turn of the evening, because the tide is the only thing in Hollowmere that has never been bribed.

That is the custom for witches here, and no one day-side troubles to know it, so I will tell it: no grave, no fire. A witch's work is thread, and thread must be tied off, not buried. They sew the shroud shut over her in her own stitch --- the dead woman's own pattern, worked by whoever loved or owed her most --- and they give her to the water at slack tide, and the sea unpicks what it is owed at its own pace, which is the only honest interest rate in the city.

The quay at the Shambles' edge was fuller than I had expected and emptier than she deserved. The Lamplighters sent four --- everyone the hall has left, I would understand later, and understanding it later is a stone I still carry. They stood in a row with their ladders shouldered like oars carried up from the water, and their lamps lit at half-wick, which is their mourning. The Magpie Exchange sent a bird I did not yet know, black and white and watchful on a bollard, with one respectful button laid on the stones before her. There were cats on the roofline, a dozen at least, sitting the way we sit at funerals, which is exactly the way we sit anywhere else; grief is nobody's business but the griever's. And the Chandlers' Guild sent one man, early, in very good black, standing precisely where he could be seen being sorry.

I noted him. The board on the chimney breast noted him.

The rite is the seam. Mother Cardew took the bone needle and the grey thread, and began the shroud's last seam in Elspeth's own pattern --- and four stitches in, she stopped.

She stood there on the quay with the linen raised in her unsteady hands, an old woman in front of a city, and I watched her courage fail in a way I would not wish on an enemy. ``I don't know her hand,'' she said. Quietly. To the linen. ``Sixty years, and I don't know her hand.''

Nobody moved. The tide turned underneath us --- you can feel it through stone, through the pads of your feet, the moment the sea changes its mind --- and the seam stood open four stitches, and the Needle of Hollowmere lay waiting on her bier for somebody who knew her stitch.

I have watched those hands from a windowsill every evening of my life. Through fog, through frost-flowers, through my own reflection: the needle down, the thread drawn, the little tug at the end of each stitch like a word ending. I know the hand better than I know my own name --- which she also gave me, so it comes to the same thing.

I got up onto the bier.

Somebody started forward to stop me --- some notion of decency, a cat on a shroud --- and Cardew said, ``Let him,'' in a voice that ended the subject for a generation.

I will not pretend my part was neat. A cat is not built for needlework: I pushed the bone needle with a paw, drew the thread in my teeth, and the first stitch took me three tries, like the kettle, and nobody will ever know that either, except that I am writing it here, so now everyone will, and I find I do not mind. The pattern was in me the way routes are in me. Her hand, remembered by my whole body at once. Down, through, draw, tug --- \emph{a word ending} --- and again, along the linen, while the tide stood slack and the lamps burned at half-wick and a row of cats on a warehouse roof watched a colleague work.

The seam closed. The tide took her without discussion, the way she would have wanted --- no speeches, no splash, the grey linen going down through the grey water until it was a paleness, and then a memory, and then the sea seeing to its accounts.

One lamplighter forgot not to salute.

Then Mother Cardew did the thing for which I will forgive her everything else, always. In front of the guild delegates and the roofline and the man in very good black, she bent down --- it cost her; I heard her knees pay it --- and she tied a small grey knot into the thread at my chest, where the whole Weft could see it. A favor-knot: a debt, formal and visible, the real currency of the night-side. The old woman put her debt on record to a cat.

``A debt,'' she said. ``Mine. You will know when to spend it.''

And then, walking me back up the quay while the crowd made itself busy elsewhere, low, for my ears alone:

``It has happened twice before. Both times a stitcher. The Tatterstage's lead player, forty years gone --- one of ours, though the playbills never knew it, and I will let her keep how. And Widow Sarn of Hemlock Row, three winters after that --- she sewed sails, she taught half the quay to mend nets, and her terrier sat out two winters on her step, waiting to be let in. Nobody looked, either time --- the Circuit closed the files, and I signed the closing, and that is my portion of this and I will carry it. I am too old and too watched to open them again.'' She stopped at the sea-wall and looked at the water a while. ``You are neither. You were her ninth, you know. She would never say what she meant by keeping count. Find who did this, cat, and I will hold what doors I can.''

Ninth. I filed the word next to \emph{again}, in the drawer of things said to me that were heavier than the sayer knew.

\scenebreak

Home, then, to a house with nobody in it.

Night had come down while the city walked back from the water. The lamps went up along her street --- most of them; two dark, and I checked the ledger in my chest before I could stop myself --- and I came in through the workroom window and sat in the dark parlor among the cut threads, and let the house be quiet at me.

The kettle sat where I had left it, off the fire, on the cold hearthstone. And I found that of everything in the room --- the empty chair, the cut walls, the shroud-grey light --- the kettle was the wrongest thing, because a kettle off the fire is a kettle \emph{finished}, and nothing here is finished. Nothing here has even begun.

I went up to the board on the chimney breast. The candle stub hung from its pin, throwing its little shadow. Beside it now I set the things the day had brought, each on its own pin, in a cat's tidy row: a thread of grey shroud-linen, for the Tatterstage's hidden stitcher and for Widow Sarn of Hemlock Row, whom nobody looked for, and whose terrier waited. A gull feather from the quay, for the man in very good black who came early to be seen. And through the loop of it, where I could reach it with my eyes from the hearthrug, the end of the one uncut strand --- hers, mine --- still running out under the window into the city, patient as only thread is patient.

Somewhere out there was a shape that wore my witch's work like a coat. Somewhere nearer, probably, was whoever lit its way with an hour of fine wax and pinched the flame out afterward, tidily, the way you put away a tool you mean to use again.

\emph{Again.}

I slept that night on the hearthrug, because the chair was not mine to fill, and I dreamed of her table years ago, a chart of the harbor wall half-sewn under lamplight, and a much smaller cat asleep on the middle of it, deployed as a paperweight, and her voice above me somewhere, amused, saying something I could not hear yet.

I woke with my paws working at the rug, stitching nothing, in her hand.

In the morning I went to find the Magpie.
