\chapter{Creditors}

I took the docket-scrap back to Brindle at the next dawn, because paper is her native language and mine only by adoption.

She read it the way she reads everything, head cocked, one eye at a time, and I watched her be fluent in it: the watermark, the fold-grease, the ink that had been wet in fog. ``Counting-house paper,'' she said. ``Chandlers' counting-house. This is the flimsy --- the copy that rides with the cart. The originals live under the ledgers, second yard off Wick Row.'' She flicked the scrap flat with one black claw and paused --- half a second, no more, on the cart-slot seal in its corner --- and if I had been less new to all this I would have priced that pause properly and asked about it. I was new. I filed it instead. Half-seconds, as this account will go on to demonstrate, are the whole currency of the case.

``Second yard off Wick Row,'' she said again. ``Mind the night shift, pet.''

\scenebreak

Wickrow after dark is a district that has never once seen itself.

Every doorway wears a little flame --- lamp above the lintel, votive in the transom, candle-boxes at the sills --- and every flame is a warded bee: the Chandlers' mark, burning in its own wax, paid up by the tenants the way other streets pay for locks. A district that sells light to people frightened of the dark, and in the whole of my professional life I have never once seen it dark. That is not comfort. That is \emph{stock control}.

I went in over the roofs, dropped to the yards where the roofs ran out, and met the night shift.

The night shift was a hound.

Not the honest kind, that discusses territory at the end of a chain. A counting-house hound, off its chain, walked between the yards by a man with a lantern and let loose in each one in turn while he counted his keys --- a big-shouldered, low-voiced professional who found my scent in the second yard's woodstack and made his argument at once, in the old red language, with the door at his back and the whole yard's geometry on his side. I gave that fight everything the goose had taught me and a little of what the dog at Number Twelve had, and I will summarize it this way: I am writing this book, and that hound tells the story differently, and we are both entitled.

But it cost me the quiet. Somewhere across the yards the keys stopped counting, and the lantern came around, and boots --- more than one pair --- and I came out of the woodstack low and fast into the alley-mouth with the light swinging after me, and the alley-mouth was blind, and this is the moment my whole account has been walking toward, so mark it:

A paw hooked my scruff and hauled me sideways through a gap I had not seen, into a doorway's dead shadow, and held me there --- \emph{held me}, like luggage --- while the watch went past close enough to smell the lamp-oil and the supper on them.

When the boots were two yards gone the paw let go. I turned around with every dignity I had left and found a grey tom considering me from the dark: old, big-boned under a coat that had seen weather, one eye, half of one ear, and the stillness of a creature that has spent a long time being exactly as visible as it chooses.

``You fight like a housecat,'' he said.

``I was one.''

``Were,'' he agreed, and did not say sorry.

I have turned that minute over more times than any other minute I own. What I saw then was an old alley professional doing a stranger a kindness in the oldest currency the roofs deal in. What I see now --- but this account has rules, and one of them is that I tell it in the order I lived it. In the order I lived it, he was the first creature since the parlor who had put a paw on me, and the paw had been \emph{sure}, and something in me that had been walking on three legs since the kettle went quiet put its fourth foot down.

He looked me over once, professionally, the way Cardew had looked over the room.

``Watch the paws, not the claws,'' he said. ``Claws are a rumor. Paws are an appointment. The dog told you where it would be four moves before it got there, and you were reading the teeth.'' He tipped his head at the yard I had come out of. ``Wax door, far side, under the loading beam. The counting room is behind it, and Wick's paper is worth the reading.''

``You know what I am looking for,'' I said. It was not a question, and he did not treat it as one.

``I knew your witch to look at,'' he said. ``Everyone did.'' He was already moving, grey into grey, and at the gap in the fence he paused with the one eye half back toward me. ``Bodkin,'' he said --- his name, given the way the old ones give it, once, take it or leave it --- and was gone over the fence with no more noise than a thought.

A bodkin, she told me once, in the windowsill years, is the blunt needle. The one you use to unpick.

I took the wax door.

\scenebreak

The counting room was warm --- wax-warm, hive-warm --- and lit by its own stock, and guarded, because of course it was. The Chandlers do not set dogs on their paper. They set the paper's own keeper: a poured thing, man-high, tallow-shouldered, with a gentle unfinished face, that stood up out of the corner shadows when I crossed the threshold and placed itself between me and the ledger-shelves with the unhurried certainty of a door swinging shut.

I had learned, by then, to spend four seconds looking. I spent six. The thing was slow and its patience was perfect, and strength was not going to answer it --- but it \emph{was} a working, and a working is stitches, and stitches can be read. The threads of it ran down into the floor, into a lattice woven under the boards --- an ugly, effective, journeyman weave, nothing like her hand --- and a lattice is only a gate lying down. She unpicked gates in front of me four hundred times. Watching is not doing; but I had stopped merely watching the night I mended her door.

So while the keeper leaned its warm bulk after me, always a beat behind, I went at the lattice --- paw and tooth, loop by loop, in the order the weave itself suggested if you troubled to ask it --- and the wrong loops fought me, and the right ones came away like a lie confessed, and on the last of them the keeper stopped mid-lean, considered its own hands with that gentle face, and poured itself politely back into the corner, off duty for the first time in years. I would almost say it looked relieved. Wax runs to sentiment. It is the bees in it.

Under the ledgers, exactly where the flimsy's watermark said they would be, the originals.

And in the originals, her name.

\emph{Vane}, in a strong small impatient hand --- the hand, though I did not yet know it, that I would come to know almost as well as hers. Instruction after instruction, dated inside one single week of this spring: buy Vane's paper. All of it. The tallow debt to the renderers' yard. The two winters' rent she owed on the workroom before the good years. The chandlery account, seven years old, forgiven-in-name and never in law. \emph{Buy it quiet, pay the premium for quiet, and call it due Lady's Day.}

Every debt my witch owed in the world, bought up in seven days, by one buyer.

I sat on the counting-house desk in the light of the enemy's own candles and made myself read it twice, because the first reading had a red wash over it and red is not a reading light. Then I folded the page --- teeth and paw; there is a knack, and I have it now --- and slid it into my shadow, into one of the nine pockets she sewed there, where it weighed what secrets weigh, which is nothing, and rode like a stone anyway.

Because here is what the ledger meant, and I had gone one whole roof toward home before I made myself admit it. A man who buys every debt you owe and calls them due on one date is not planning your murder. He is planning your \emph{eviction}. Paper says what Wick wanted, and what Wick wanted was my witch out --- alive, ruined, and out. You do not go to the expense of killing a woman you have spent a premium to lawfully destroy. And --- this thought arrived later, on the drainpipe, and stopped me half up it --- you \emph{particularly} do not kill her if her needlework is holding shut something you profit by. A dead seamstress mends nothing.

I needed to know what her needle had been holding for Ellery Wick. And for that I needed people who watch the Chandlers professionally and hate them fluently.

Fortunately, the city keeps a guild of exactly those.

\scenebreak

The Lamplighters' hall burns lamps at its own door.

Think what that costs a guild that cannot spare the oil: their own light, spent outward at the dark, all night, every night, as a point of order. I came to it over the yards and stopped on the wall opposite and read the front of that building like a notice: \emph{we are not gone. We light what we have. Come home.}

Inside, the hall was one cold room with its heart beating slow. Half the coats on the pegs hung over boots nobody had come back for --- pairs of boots, toes out, waiting, under coats that kept their shapes the way the wraith on Needle Lane had kept a shape, and I understood with my whole pelt why the empty coat is the worst thing this city makes. Forty lamplighters used to walk Hollowmere. What was left of the guild fit in that one room, around one lamp, with room to spare.

They knew me the moment I came in --- \emph{the witch's cat} went around the room in the way words go around a room full of people not saying them --- and the room braced. A grieving hall, and a cat in it, wanting something. I know what I am; I know what my wanting costs rooms like that.

The one to convince was obvious. Torn ear; chalk on her paws --- hands; I keep the ledger in my own tongue --- a shift-boss's way of standing, facing the door, near the lamp, where the next bad news would have to go through her first. Merrow.

I did not lay Wick's page down. Paper first is an auditor's manners. Instead I did the thing I had been carrying up through my chest since the doorway, which had the advantage of being true: I looked at the coats over the boots, and I asked --- in the plain way, whiskers down, no performance --- after their missing.

The room stopped bracing.

``Not dead,'' Merrow said. Flat, fast, a door long since slammed on that word. ``The Court said not dead. Our filings came back \emph{marked}: no appointments under those names. So they are SOMEWHERE---'' and here her voice did the thing that grief does when it has been on shift too long, which is nothing at all, ``--- and this hall lights lamps until they walk back in.''

She looked at me a long moment, chalk-handed, torn-eared, and something about a cat asking after the missing instead of the murder seemed to settle a question I had not known I was sitting.

``Walk my round with me, cat,'' she said. ``Rounds first. Paper after.'' So she had seen the page's corner in my shadow after all. You do not make shift-boss by missing things.

\scenebreak

Her round was forty-one lamps, and something was walking it ahead of us.

We both saw it by the fifth: lamps she had lit at the round's head, dark again behind us --- not blown, not failed; \emph{drunk}, wicks wet and whole, the little warded bees drained to grease. And the order of the darkening was her order, her exact route, the sequence her guild has walked so long it has worn a groove in the evening. At the fifteenth lamp we doubled back quiet and caught them at it: a pair of wisps, fat as lamp-oil themselves, working her lamps \emph{in sequence}, one keeping watch while the other drank.

Wisps do not do that. Let me put it as plainly as I can, because it is the hinge of everything that follows: a wisp is appetite in a bottle. Appetite does not read route-maps. Appetite goes to the nearest, the brightest, the easiest --- it does not skip a bright lamp to keep a \emph{schedule}. These two had been taught. Something had walked this round with them enough nights to teach appetite a route, the way you teach a dog a beat, and had chosen, of all the rounds in the city, the round that stitches Wickrow's dark shut.

Merrow took the left one and I took the right, and a shift-boss of the Lamplighters, it turns out, swings a ladder like a guildhall falling, and the fight was short and the lane the brighter for it. She relit her lamps in order --- \emph{her} order --- to the round's end, and at the hall door she chalked a fresh tally on the wood, one stroke among hundreds, and I understood that the strokes were rounds completed since the night of the missing, and that the tally was itself a lamp of a kind.

``You came about the night,'' she said. ``Ask, then. I answer what is asked. No more.''

That is a lamplighter's honesty and I respect it and it is a doorway exactly the width of your own wits. I sat, and thought, and asked --- not \emph{who killed her}, which she could not know, nor \emph{what happened to your missing}, which she would have answered with the Court's cruel paperwork and nothing more --- but this: ``What work did Elspeth Vane do in Wickrow that nobody paid for?''

Merrow looked at me the way you look at a colleague who has just found the fault you had walked past. Then she took down the shift-book from behind the meeting-board, and opened it to a page her thumb knew, and laid the page where I could read it.

``That is Wick's hand,'' she said, tapping the requisition pasted at the head. ``And that is the night nobody signed.''

The tally ran down two facing pages, and it said this: for two years, at the turn of every season and after every hard blow off the Mere, ward-shifts at the \emph{Wickhouse} --- the guild's great warehouse on the Mere-edge --- logged, walked, witnessed, and paid to \emph{nobody}. The escort-lamplighter's fee in the book, every time; the stitcher's fee, blank, every time. She sewed a rich man's warehouse shut, season upon season, for free. Nobody sews free. She least of anyone --- I have watched her price a churn-blessing to the ha'penny and chase the ha'penny.

Unless the sewing was not for the rich man at all. Unless what her needle held shut in that warehouse was something she would have paid to keep held, and the rich man merely owned the building it lived behind, and knew what he had, and let a witch ruin her evenings and her ledgers on it for two years, gratis, while he bought up her debts with the money her work was saving him.

I did not say any of this at the hall. It was not yet mine to say. But Merrow watched me reading, and read me reading, and when I looked up her face had the grim satisfaction of a woman whose grievance has finally found a colleague.

One more thing happened in that hall, and I set it down because the city should know it. Behind the meeting-board their own ward hung torn --- the hall's hem, the guild's own guard-stitching, ripped the night of the missing and left that way, because the one who always mended it was one of the coats. They had been living inside a torn ward for a season sooner than let anyone else touch her seam. Merrow saw me looking. The whole room saw Merrow seeing.

Cats inspect seams. It is the ancient arrangement; nobody has ever said it aloud, and nobody needed to then. I got up on the meeting-board, and I mended their ward in front of everybody --- better than the parlor door; I had been practicing on grief for weeks by then --- and when it was done the hall hummed faintly, the sound of a room with someone minding it, and one of the four remaining lamplighters put his face in his hands, and everyone else looked at the lamp.

Merrow walked me to the door. At the threshold, in front of her whole guild, she tied a lamplighter's knot into the thread at my chest --- rough work, honest, tight --- where the entire Weft could read it.

``The hall owes the cat,'' she said, for the room.

And then, bending close, for me alone, in the voice she keeps for the end of bad shifts:

``Wisps do not learn a route. Somebody taught them the taste. Find the teacher.''
